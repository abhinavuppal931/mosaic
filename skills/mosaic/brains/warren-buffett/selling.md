# Selling: Warren Buffett

Built from 86 sources (partnership letters 1958 to 1970, Berkshire letters 1978 to 2025, lectures, interviews and annual meeting transcripts to 2025) through the verified batch notes. His rules for selling, trimming, adding and re-checking, most-used first. The core is stable for 60 years: sell the business when its economics or its people break, almost never on price. The one big refinement came in 2017, when he said the no-sell pledge covers controlled businesses, not marketable stocks.

## Rules

### 1. Buy what you never want to sell, then hang on
Sources: 12 (S012, S018, S022, S030, S043, S079, S080, S097, S122, S126, S134, S036).
"I mean, the real thing to do with a great business is just hang on for dear life." (S122, 1998-05) "So there’s no reason to sell a wonderful business except for kind of extraneous factors." (S126, 2008-05) He quotes Coke's Robert Woodruff on when to sell: never (S022, 1997-02). On selling, his one-liner is that Berkshire likes to buy: "We like to buy." (S018) Coke and American Express were left untouched for decades: "we will most certainly leave our holdings untouched throughout the year." (S043, 2024-02) Japan (2025-05, newest): "We will not be selling any stock. I mean, that will not happen in decades." (S134)

### 2. Controlled businesses are kept; marketable stocks can be sold (refined 2017)
Sources: 9 (S001, S012, S013, S031, S032, S036, S041, S080, S134).
1986 to 1991: three permanent stock holdings would not be sold "even if far overpriced" (S013, paraphrase), and of whole businesses: "Well, we won't sell it. We just don't sell businesses." (S080, 1991-04) The exceptions are a business doomed to losses or endemic labor strife: Berkshire "will not continue the operation of any business doomed to unending losses." (S032, 2013-03) and "Our approach is far from Darwinian, and many of you may disapprove of it." (S031, 2012-02) 2017-02 (newer): the pledge was clarified to exclude stocks: "we regard any marketable security as available for sale, however unlikely such a sale now seems." (S036) He prefers that flexibility: "It is also far easier to exit from a mistake when it has been made in the marketable arena." (S041, 2022-02)

### 3. Sell when the economics or the moat break, not when the price falls
Sources: 10 (S016, S022, S027, S034, S079, S093, S130, S134, S105, S045).
After buying, "Thereafter, you need only monitor whether these qualities are being preserved." (S022, 1997-02) The test is the business: "The way to look at a business is this going to keep producing more and more money over time?" (S079) Kodak let Fuji in: "but they let that moat narrow." (S079, 1998-10) Moody's intrinsic value shrank in 2007 even though the purchase price was right (S093, 2008-03). Tesco showed the signs together: "The company's market share fell, its margins contracted and accounting problems surfaced." (S034, 2015-02) Laws and politics can do it too (2025-05, newest): "values change, and they don’t always change upwards." (S134) A price drop alone is not news: "it's bad news for us when something goes wrong with a company" (S105, 2010-03)

### 4. Sell when management fails, deceives or loses focus
Sources: 7 (S022, S059, S080, S083, S122, S130, S025).
He named the exits in 1991: a business that loses money permanently, or a manager who lies or cheats (S080, paraphrase). Promised smoothness is a red flag; he sold Freddie Mac for it: "any time a large financial institution starts promising regular earnings increases you're going to have trouble" (S083, 2010-05) "there's seldom just one cockroach in the kitchen" (S083) Distraction kills value: "All too often, we've seen value stagnate in the presence of hubris or of boredom that caused the attention of managers to wander." (S022) When bad behavior appears, "you’ve got to take action fast" (S130, 2019-05). As a control owner he replaced Dempster's management after six months of lip service (S059, 1963-01).

### 5. Sell when the price runs far past value, but not on a target
Sources: 10 (S013, S105, S126, S027, S050, S024, S083, S079, S122, S052).
"In such a case, we will sell our holdings." (S013, 1988-02), of a market that overvalues a business. "we sell companies when we think they're fully priced, when we think that there's better uses for the money elsewhere" (S105, 2010-03) PetroChina was sold when it reached fair value against other oil companies: "we no longer felt it was undervalued compared to other oil companies, so we sold our stock." (S126, 2008-05) He sets no price targets: "We don't ever say if we ever get an offer of $50 million for this business we will sell it." (S079, 1998-10) Selling at a top only because others will pay more would make him a bigger-fool player: "that in effect I was practicing the bigger fool type approach to investing" (S122) Admitted failure here (2004-02): "I made a big mistake in not selling several of our larger holdings during The Great Bubble." (S050) and "Nevertheless, I can properly be criticized for merely clucking about nose-bleed valuations during the Bubble rather than acting on my views." (S024)

### 6. Swap into something clearly better
Sources: 10 (S013, S034, S052, S079, S097, S105, S029, S116, S122, S028).
"Sometimes, also, we will sell a security that is fairly valued or even undervalued because we require funds for a still more undervalued investment or one we believe we understand better." (S013, 1988-02) In the partnership years: "I was just constantly having to sell what was the least attractive stock in order to buy something I just discovered that looked even cheaper." (S079) His only big realized losses came in 1974 and 1975 when "we sold stocks that were very cheap in order to buy others we believed to be even cheaper." (S034) In 2008 and 2009 he sold J&J, P&G, ConocoPhillips and Moody's to fund Dow, Swiss Re, GE and BNSF while expecting the sold stocks to rise: "Charlie and I believe that all of these stocks will likely trade higher in the future." (S029, 2010-02) "So you sell if you need money for something else." (S122)

### 7. A falling price on an intact business is a reason to add
Sources: 13 (S011, S013, S016, S019, S079, S080, S093, S097, S105, S116, S122, S126, S134).
Washington Post fell about 25 percent after he bought: "What we had thought ridiculously cheap a year earlier had become a good bit cheaper" (S011) "delayed recognition can be an advantage: It may give us the chance to buy more of a good thing at a bargain price." (S013) Judge the business as if unquoted: "if there were no quote on Wells Fargo and I just owned it like I own my farm, I would look at the way the business is developing" (S097, 2009-03) "If they go down a lot and I have some money, I'll buy some more of them, you know." (S105) Newest (2025-05): "if it makes a difference to you whether your stocks are down 15% or not, you need to get a somewhat different investment philosophy." (S134)

### 8. Never sell on news, forecasts, downgrades or Fed guesses
Sources: 6 (S022, S038, S039, S087, S093, S134).
A holding is not "a financial dalliance to be terminated because of downgrades by the Street, expected Federal Reserve actions, possible political developments, forecasts by economists" (S038, 2019-02); 2020-02 adds an earnings miss to the list (S039). Panic over catastrophe news is the wrong reason to sell a fine business (S022, paraphrase). "These businesses will indeed suffer earnings hiccups, as they always have" (S087, 2008-10) Cyclical dips in owned businesses: "So that doesn't bother me in the least" (S093)

### 9. When you are wrong, get out fast; dithering costs
Sources: 11 (S011, S025, S028, S031, S033, S034, S050, S083, S097, S116, S020).
Tesco (2015-02): "An attentive investor, I'm embarrassed to report, would have sold Tesco shares earlier. I made a big mistake with this investment by dawdling." (S034) "My leisurely pace in making sales would prove expensive." (S034) Gen Re derivatives: "Fault me for dithering." (S025) "I, however, dithered." (S050), which cost about $100 million; the only answer was "the only answer was to get out of it" (S083). Textiles: "I should be faulted for not quitting sooner." (S011) He names the habit: "I made some errors of omission, sucking my thumb when new facts came in that should have caused me to re-examine my thinking and promptly take action." (S028, 2009-02) After a loss, do not chase it: "You don't have to make it back the way that you lost it." (S020)

### 10. Selling a wonderful business too early is the costlier error
Sources: 10 (S011, S017, S020, S021, S045, S056, S083, S122, S126, S069).
GEICO, sold in 1952: "which taught me a lesson about the inadvisability of selling a stake in an identifiably-wonderful company." (S021) Cap Cities was sold in 1978 to 1980 and again in 1993 below later prices (S011, S020). "In particular, my decision to sell McDonald’s was a very big mistake." (S045, 1999-02) He sold Fannie Mae out of distaste for a small position: "I surrendered to my distaste for holding small positions and sold the 7 million shares we owned." (S017) His summary (1998-05): "Everything we’ve ever sold has gone up subsequently, but some have gone up more painfully subsequently than others." (S122) And from 1962: "Over the years our timing of purchases has been considerably better than our timing of sales." (S056)

### 11. Do not trim winners for balance; let them dominate
Sources: 6 (S022, S039, S042, S064, S046, S025).
A few holdings growing into a large share of the portfolio is the reward, not a risk to trim (S022, paraphrase). "Over time, it takes just a few winners to work wonders." (S042, 2023-02) Losers shrink on their own: "the poor business tends to stagnate" in his words, "the \"poor\" business tends to stagnate" is how the letter puts it (S039). Past gains are no reason either way: "the inner warm glow that results from having held a winner last year is of no importance" (S064) He held Coca-Cola from $13.4 billion down to $8.1 billion without selling (S025, 2006-02).

### 12. Add to what you already know before buying something new
Sources: 7 (S017, S020, S043, S069, S122, S130, S034).
"the ideal purchase is to find, is to have something that you already like be selling at a price where you feel like buying more of it." (S122, 1998-05) In 1994: "Last year we went that direction by enlarging our holdings in Coca-Cola and American Express." (S020) He wants prices to stay low while he accumulates, and regrets stopping when they rose: of Fannie Mae, he stopped buying in frustration (S017, paraphrase); of 1966, results "would have been much better off from a long range standpoint if 1966 results had been five percentage points worse" is close to his words: "we would have been much better off from a long range standpoint if 1966 results had been five percentage points worse" (S069). In 2023 he added to Occidental and all five Japanese houses after meeting managements (S043, paraphrase).

### 13. Taxes, size and accounting shape the exit, never the decision
Sources: 8 (S024, S037, S044, S064, S122, S130, S134, S105).
"we sell securities when that seems the intelligent thing to do, not because we are trying to influence earnings in any way." (S037, 2018-02) Taxes are a factor, not a reason: "the performance of a stock last year or last month is no reason, per se, to either own it or to not own it now." (S064) The tax on a $15 billion Coke stake would be about $5 billion, which raises the bar (S122, paraphrase). Size slows exits: "Sometimes a year or more is required to establish or divest an investment." (S044, 2025-02) Partial positions he will drop fast: "Sometimes I'll start in buying something and not got a full position." (S105) Above 10 percent, reporting and short-swing rules lock positions, so he trims banks below it (S130, paraphrase).

### 14. Bonds, preferreds and workouts are loans: exit at maturity, call or a broken credit
Sources: 9 (S016, S031, S033, S062, S080, S086, S013, S078, S030).
Preferreds are "a form of lending money" in his phrase: "It's a form of lending money." (S080) He re-marks them on credit, rates and the common: USAir became "less secure than at the time I made it" (S016, 1991-02), so: "Even so, our investment is now less secure than at the time I made it." (S016) Bonds are held "ready to dispose of our bonds whenever something better comes along" (S013). Energy Future Holdings: "I totally miscalculated the gain/loss probabilities when I purchased the bonds." (S031) and, on selling at a loss in 2013, "That was a big mistake." (S033) Workouts end at deal close (S062). Redemptions by issuers end good positions he would rather keep (S030, paraphrase).

### 15. Position limits are mostly rules, not risk targets
Sources: 5 (S043, S072, S097, S130, S134).
In the partnership, a 40 percent ceiling: "the largest proportion we have ever had in anything, we hit our 40% limit" (S072, 1968-01) Japan was capped by promise: "it will not purchase shares that will take our holdings beyond 9.9%." (S043, 2024-02), a cap being relaxed by 2025 (S134). Bank holding company rules held American Express at 9.9 percent in 2009 (S097, paraphrase).

## Kill criteria this seat would write

These are checkable facts in his style, inferred from S016, S020, S022, S031, S034, S043, S079, S080, S083, S097, S122, S126, S130, S134.
- Unit costs stay above the low-cost rival with no credible way down (the USAir test).
- Market share falls, margins contract and an accounting problem surfaces in the same year (the Tesco test).
- Management starts promising smooth, rising quarterly earnings, or one undisclosed problem comes to light (the first cockroach).
- A manager lies, hides bad news from the board or regulator, or shows he loves money more than the business.
- Retained earnings fail to add at least a dollar of market value per dollar kept over a rolling five-year test, or three to four years at most.
- The business needs heavy new capital just to hold its place, or becomes a continuing cash drain.
- Pricing power moves to someone else: a competitor (Kodak and Fuji), a retailer (Kraft Heinz), or a new ad channel (newspapers).
- A regulator, court or legislature cuts the allowed return or adds open-ended liability (BHE wildfires).
- Low-cost imports take the market and the domestic plant cannot match them (Dexter, the Maine shoe plants).
- Management drifts into unrelated ventures, the Coke shrimp farm or Gillette oil well.
- A bank in the portfolio is forced to issue stock at a distressed price.
- The price reaches far above a conservative value range and a better use for the money exists (PetroChina).

## Re-check triggers

- Every year: did per-share earnings progress and did the moat widen (S027).
- Every five years: the retained-earnings test against market value (S001).
- Any new fact that contradicts the thesis: re-examine now, not after thumb-sucking (S028).
- A CEO change, especially at a bank or insurer, or a new CEO at an investee (S126, S025).
- A shift in industry structure: new ad channels, online retail, driverless cars, coal volumes for railroads (S016, S035).
- Regulatory or legal change to allowed returns or liability (S043, S134).
- Weekly real-economy data for the businesses held, such as railcar loadings (S093).
- A large price drop: re-check the business, not the quote, and add if it is intact (S097, S105).
- Market value to GNP or the bond-stock yield gap at an extreme (S085, S037).
- A candid post-mortem of each sale and each acquisition after the fact (S047, S122).
