# Stories: Warren Buffett

Built from 86 sources (partnership letters 1958 to 1970, Berkshire letters 1978 to 2025, lectures, interviews and annual meeting transcripts to 2025) through the verified batch notes. The cases he retells, most-told first, each with the lesson he draws for judging a business and its price. His mistakes take up as much room as his wins, because he gives them as much room himself.

## 1. The Berkshire textile mill: a cheap stock in a doomed business
Told in 17 sources (S001, S003, S011, S015, S016, S030, S032, S034, S039, S041, S043, S044, S067, S076, S080, S122, S134). He began buying a New England textile company in 1962 at $7.60, below working capital, and took control in 1965 after the CEO shaved an eighth of a point off a tender offer. He kept the mills for 20 years, spent more capital and bought another mill, and closed them in 1985. Lessons: a commodity business punishes good managers, cheap assets in a dying industry stay cheap only on paper, and pride is expensive. "That was a monumentally stupid decision." (S034) "My first mistake, of course, was in buying control of Berkshire." (S015) He still credits the manager: "Although I made an economic mistake in sticking with the textile business, I made no mistake in choosing Ken" (S016)

## 2. GEICO: the low-cost moat he found at 20 and bought three times
Told in 16 sources (S006, S010, S020, S021, S022, S024, S029, S030, S034, S035, S038, S040, S045, S122, S130, S134). In January 1951 he took the train to Washington on a Saturday, got a custodian to let him in, and Lorimer Davidson spent four hours explaining direct auto insurance. He put about 65 percent of his net worth in, then sold in 1952 and missed about $1.3 million of gain. Berkshire bought a third in 1976 to 1980 for $47 million while GEICO was near failure, and paid $2.3 billion for the other half in 1996. Lessons: a sustainable cost advantage is the moat, a franchise with a curable problem is not a turnaround, and do not sell a wonderful company. "GEICO has a major, sustainable competitive advantage." (S010) "which taught me a lesson about the inadvisability of selling a stake in an identifiably-wonderful company." (S021) "In other words, get the policyholder’s business by deserving his business." (S030)

## 3. Nebraska Furniture Mart and Mrs. B: value to the customer, and a handshake
Told in 13 sources (S009, S010, S012, S013, S014, S018, S019, S031, S033, S040, S050, S080, S097). Rose Blumkin arrived from Russia with no English, started the store in 1937 with $500, and outsold every rival in Omaha. He bought 90 percent in 1983 with no audit, no inventory count and no title check. He later admitted forgetting to get a non-compete; she left and opened a rival store in her 90s. Lessons: the best business gives the customer exceptional value, and character can replace diligence. "We gave Mrs. B a check for $55 million and she gave us her word. That made for an even exchange." (S010) "one built upon exceptional value to the customer that in turn translates into exceptional economics for its owners." (S009)

## 4. See's Candy: pricing power on almost no capital
Told in 11 sources (S009, S017, S027, S031, S034, S040, S042, S046, S079, S080, S130). Bought in 1972 for $25 million after he nearly walked away over $5 million: "The seller was asking $30 million, and I was adamant about not going above $25 million." (S027) By 2007 it had earned $1.35 billion pre-tax with only $32 million reinvested (S027), and $1.9 billion on $40 million of added capital by 2014 (S034). Prices go up every December 26 (S079). It never grew beyond its niche or travelled east, so its cash went elsewhere (S130). Lessons: the dream business needs little capital and has untapped pricing power, and quibbling over a small price gap nearly cost him the prototype. It also taught him to pay for quality, which led to Coca-Cola.

## 5. Coca-Cola: 52 years to act on what he could see
Told in 11 sources (S014, S015, S019, S020, S025, S042, S079, S080, S093, S105, S122). He sold Cokes door to door at six for 25 cents as a boy and did not buy a share until 1988. He bought about $1.3 billion by 1994, from the annual report alone, and held through a fall from $13.4 billion to $8.1 billion in market value (S025). A $40 share at the 1919 IPO fell to $19 within a year and later grew to over $5 million with dividends reinvested (S122). Lessons: a simple brand with world growth is worth holding through bad years, and the cost of thumb-sucking is huge. "It took only fifty years before I finally got it: The real money was in the syrup." (S020) "I've learned my lesson: My response time to the next glaringly attractive idea will be slashed to well under 50 years." (S015)

## 6. General Re and its derivatives book: a reputation outlived its discipline
Told in 11 sources (S024, S025, S027, S028, S036, S045, S049, S050, S083, S097, S126). He merged with General Re in 1998 for about $22 billion in Berkshire stock, trusting decades of observed discipline. Underwriting had slipped and a dealer book held 23,218 contracts with 884 counterparties. Winding it down took about five years and over $400 million. Lessons: culture can change under a famous name, do not own what you cannot understand, and exits from derivatives are slow. "This reputation, unfortunately, outlived its factual underpinnings, a flaw that I completely missed when I made the decision in 1998 to merge with General Re." (S027) "I couldn’t understand 22,000 of them, probably" (S097) He called the share issue a terrible mistake even after the company became a gem (S036).

## 7. Cigar butts and toads: Dempster, Sanborn, Hochschild Kohn, Waumbec
Told in 9 sources (S005, S015, S018, S034, S054, S056, S059, S062, S122). In the partnership years he bought assets at a discount: Sanborn Map's securities portfolio, Dempster Mill, first bought at $18 a share against $72 of book, where Harry Bottle cut costs and turned inventory into cash. Those worked as asset plays. Bought as businesses, they failed: Hochschild Kohn, a Baltimore department store bought below book, was sold three years later for about cost (S015), and Waumbec Mills became a mistake (S005, S034). Lessons: first-class managers cannot fix poor economics, cheapness does not cure a weak business, and the method stops working at size. "While we labored mightily, new problems arose as fast as old problems were tamed." (S005) Of his early acquisitions of cheap, mediocre businesses: "I kissed and they croaked." (S018)

## 8. USAir: a senior security in a high-cost commodity business
Told in 10 sources (S016, S017, S018, S020, S022, S023, S027, S042, S079, S122). He bought $358 million of USAir preferred in 1989 because he had cash and liked the long profit record and the seniority. Deregulated competitors had lower costs; the dividend stopped in 1994 and he wrote it down to 25 cents on the dollar. He then tried and failed to sell at 50 cents, and the stock recovered under new management before he sold in 1998. Lessons: a senior position does not protect you from industry economics, and luck rescued him. "my analysis of USAir's business was both superficial and wrong." (S022) "I was wrong in originally purchasing the stock, and I was wrong later, in repeatedly trying to unload our holdings at 50 cents on the dollar." (S023)

## 9. Dexter Shoe: paying for a so-so business with stock of a wonderful one
Told in 8 sources (S019, S027, S034, S036, S046, S047, S048, S105). He paid $433 million in Berkshire shares in 1993. Imports took the market and the business went to zero, while the shares given away grew to about $5.7 billion by 2015 (S034) and over $6 billion by 2017 (S036). Lessons: a moat can vanish to low-cost foreign competition, and issuing a great stock to buy a fair business multiplies the error. "To date, Dexter is the worst deal that I’ve made." (S027) "I clearly made a mistake in paying what I did for Dexter in 1993. Furthermore, I compounded that mistake in a huge way by using Berkshire shares in payment." (S047)

## 10. Selling wonderful companies too early: GEICO, Disney, Cap Cities, McDonald's
Told in 7 sources (S011, S020, S021, S045, S080, S122, S017). GEICO went in 1952 to buy a cheaper stock; Disney was bought in 1966 at an $80 million valuation and sold in 1967 at 48 1/8; Cap Cities was sold in 1978 to 1980 at $43 and bought back at $172.50, then a third sold again in 1993 at $630, below later prices; McDonald's was sold in 1998; Fannie Mae was sold because the position was small. Lesson: when you own a business you understand with a moat and good management, selling to tidy up or to capture a gain is the expensive error. "Everything we’ve ever sold has gone up subsequently, but some have gone up more painfully subsequently than others." (S122) "In particular, my decision to sell McDonald’s was a very big mistake." (S045)

## 11. Washington Post 1973 and 1974: price is not risk
Told in 7 sources (S008, S011, S019, S080, S082, S116, S122). The whole company sold for about $80 million against $400 million or more of properties. He bought about 9 percent; the stock then fell about 25 percent and he kept buying, for about 100 times his money (S116). Lessons: a lower price on an unchanged business lowers risk, and a steady business with a volatile stock is the best combination. "I have never been able to figure out why it's riskier to buy $400 million worth of properties for $40 million than $80 million." (S082) "What we had thought ridiculously cheap a year earlier had become a good bit cheaper" (S011)

## 12. Errors of omission: Fannie Mae, the Dallas TV station, pharma, Google
Told in 6 sources (S017, S027, S079, S093, S122, S130). In 1988 he decided to buy 30 million Fannie Mae shares, bought 7 million, stopped as the price rose, and sold, missing about $1.4 billion (S017). He passed on a Dallas NBC station at $35 million that later earned $73 million a year (S027). He saw high returns in drug companies and did little (S122), and Berkshire missed Google though GEICO saw its ads work (S130). Lesson: the costliest errors never show on the books, and they come from thumb-sucking on things he understood. "The only explanation is that my brain had gone on vacation and forgot to notify me." (S027) "the biggest mistakes have not been mistakes of commission, but of omission." (S079)

## 13. American Express and the salad oil scandal: a scandal that missed the franchise
Told in 6 sources (S006, S020, S023, S080, S097, S116). In 1964 a subsidiary's fraud crushed the stock; he judged the card and travelers check franchise untouched and put about 40 percent of the partnership in it. In 1994 he leaned toward selling until a talk with Hertz's CEO showed why merchants paid Amex more, and he bought more instead. Lessons: separate a one-off loss from the franchise, and ask someone who knows the business before you sell. "No one would have argued about the value of American Express. They just didn't want to own it for a while." (S080) In 2009: "that doesn’t mean that American Express isn’t a hell of a buy at $10" (S097)

## 14. BNSF: an all-in bet on America that eats capital
Told in 6 sources (S029, S033, S034, S035, S041, S043). Bought in late 2009, in a gloomy market, about 30 percent in Berkshire stock and the rest cash; the deal was agreed in 11 days (S041). Service failed in 2014 and record capital spending fixed it (S035). By 2024 he stressed that it must spend above depreciation for decades. Lessons: a durable, irreplaceable asset is worth a fair price in a panic, but reported earnings overstate owner cash in capital-hungry businesses. "Indeed, compared to most American businesses, railroads eat capital." (S043)

## 15. Kraft Heinz and Precision Castparts: right business, wrong price
Told in 5 sources (S032, S035, S038, S040, S130). Heinz in 2013 with 3G worked; the 2015 Kraft merger was overpaid as retailers gained power over brands, with a $3 billion impairment in 2018. Precision Castparts, bought for over $32 billion in 2016, was written down $11 billion in 2020 because he overestimated normal earnings. Lesson: a good business with a growing brand can still be a poor purchase. "We try to buy good businesses at a decent price, and we made a mistake on the Kraft part of Kraft Heinz." (S130) "PCC is far from my first error of that sort. But it's a big one." (S040)

## 16. Unforced errors of 2007 to 2014: ConocoPhillips, Irish banks, Energy Future Holdings, Tesco
Told in 6 sources (S028, S031, S033, S034, S097, S116). He bought ConocoPhillips near the 2008 oil peak, spent $244 million on two Irish banks that fell 89 percent, put about $2 billion into Energy Future Holdings bonds on a gas-price view without asking Munger, and sold Tesco too slowly after he lost confidence in management. Lessons: a commodity-price view is still a macro bet, homework cannot be skipped on small positions, and once the thesis cracks, sell. "I was wrong about oil and therefore that made me wrong about that stock big time." (S097) "Next time I’ll call Charlie." (S033) "An attentive investor, I'm embarrassed to report, would have sold Tesco shares earlier. I made a big mistake with this investment by dawdling." (S034)

## 17. Long-Term Capital Management: leverage kills the right idea
Told in 3 sources (S079, S126, S130). Very smart people with their own money ran trades that converged in the end, but at about 25 times net worth they could not survive the path. He bid for the portfolio in 1998 with AIG and Goldman Sachs. Lesson: never risk what you have and need for what you do not need, however high the odds. "If you risk something that is important to you for something that is unimportant to you it just doesn't make sense." (S079)

## 18. Apple: owning more each year without buying a share
Told in 3 sources (S040, S041, S130). Berkshire built a 5.4 percent stake from late 2016 to mid-2018 for $36 billion, sold about $11 billion in 2020, and still owned more of the company as Apple retired shares. He wanted the stock lower so the buybacks would go further. Lesson: a great business buying back stock below value compounds an owner's share for free. "That increase was costless to us, coming about because Apple has continuously repurchased its shares" (S040)

## 19. PetroChina: buy at a huge gap, sell at fair value
Told in 3 sources (S027, S050, S126). He bought about $500 million in 2002 and 2003 from the annual report alone, when he thought the company worth about $100 billion and it sold for $35 to $40 billion. He sold in 2007 at about $275 to $300 billion and paid $1.2 billion of tax; the stock kept rising. Lessons: a wide gap needs no decimals, and selling at fair value means accepting that it may run on. "we no longer felt it was undervalued compared to other oil companies, so we sold our stock." (S126)

## 20. The five Japanese trading houses: turn every page
Told in 3 sources (S043, S044, S134). Reading a handbook of Japanese companies in 2019, he found five trading houses at very low prices, with shareholder-friendly policies and modest executive pay. Berkshire spent about a year buying, funded with yen debt, cost $13.8 billion and was worth $23.5 billion by 2024, and he plans to hold for decades. Lesson: a bargain in a market others ignore still turns up for someone who reads everything. "We simply looked at their financial records and were amazed at the low prices of their stocks." (S044)
