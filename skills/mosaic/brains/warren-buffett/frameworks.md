# Frameworks: Warren Buffett

Built from the Berkshire Hathaway letters (1977 to 2024, S003 to S050), the Owner's Manual (S001), the Buffett Partnership letters (1957 to 1969, S051 to S078), lectures, op-eds, the FCIC interview, CNBC interviews and four annual-meeting transcripts (1998, 2008, 2019, 2025). Ordered by how many sources use each, ties broken by weight to this seat (business quality and owner economics). Float, which the insurance sections of almost every annual letter repeat, is placed last because it applies to insurers rather than to every stock. Scoring lines are guidance drawn from his own numbers and thresholds, not a scorecard he published.

## 1. The four-part buy filter (understand, economics, people, price)

- **What it is:** the test he applies to a stock exactly as he would to buying the whole company. Set in 1977 and restated for nearly fifty years. The first three are usually met by a handful of names; price is what blocks action.
- **Parts:** (1) a business he can understand; (2) favorable, durable long-term economics; (3) able and honest (later "trustworthy") management; (4) a sensible price. The whole-company version is the "business wanted" ad: large size (at least $5 million after-tax earnings in 1983, $10 million by 1986, $75 million pre-tax by 2015), demonstrated consistent earning power with no interest in projections or turnarounds, good returns on equity with little or no debt, management in place, simple businesses, and a stated price; an answer in about five minutes (S008, S011, S012, S016, S034). The filters are quick and reject more than 90% of what comes in (S122). Before buying, write down why the whole company is worth more than its price (S079, S080).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** first, on every buy, add or hold. A name that fails (1) never reaches the price question.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = all four pass, with price clearly below a conservative value. 5 = the first three pass but price is only fair, so it goes on the list and he waits (S004). 0 = he cannot explain the business or the people fail the trust test; that is TOO HARD, not a low score.
- **How his view changed:** the 1977 creed asked for a "very attractive" price; by the 1992 letter (S018) size and market conditions made him settle for "attractive". Newer: attractive.
- **Quote:** "We want the business to be (1) one that we can understand, (2) with favorable long-term prospects, (3) operated by honest and competent people, and (4) available at a very attractive price." (S003)
- **Also:** "We usually can identify a small number of potential investments meeting requirements (1), (2) and (3), but (4) often prevents action." (S004) And thirty years later: "Charlie and I look for companies that have a) a business we understand; b) favorable long-term economics; c) able and trustworthy management; and d) a sensible price tag." (S027)
- **Sources:** 25 (S003, S004, S008, S011, S012, S016, S017, S018, S020, S022, S023, S027, S034, S037, S039, S040, S043, S047, S049, S050, S079, S093, S105, S122, S126).

## 2. Owner earnings and honest earnings

- **What it is:** the earnings figure that matters for valuation is what an owner could take out while keeping the business's competitive position, not GAAP net income, not "cash flow" and never EBITDA.
- **Parts:** (1) owner earnings = reported earnings plus depreciation, depletion, amortization and similar non-cash charges, minus the average annual capital spending needed to keep competitive position and unit volume, plus any extra working capital; the capex figure is a guess and rough precision is fine (S012); (2) depreciation is a real cost, and capex roughly equal to depreciation is a necessity (S015, S035); (3) amortization of purchased customer relationships is not a real cost, software amortization is, so he counts about 20% of Berkshire's amortization charge as real (S032, S033, S034); (4) stock options and stock compensation are expenses: subtract what the options would fetch if sold publicly, which often cuts per-share earnings 5% and sometimes 10% (S045, S122); (5) interest coverage is pre-tax earnings over interest, not EBITDA over interest (S032); (6) judge Berkshire and its holdings on operating earnings, ignoring realized and unrealized gains in any quarter or year (S030, S042, S043, S130); (7) read balance sheets for 8 to 10 years before the income statement, because they are harder to game (S134).
- **When to use it:** whenever management, a banker or a sell-side model leads with an adjusted number, and on any capital-heavy business where depreciation may understate maintenance capex (he subtracts for BNSF's inadequate depreciation, S035).
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = reported earnings close to owner earnings, capex near depreciation, stock pay small and expensed, no adjusted metrics in the headline. 5 = a 5% to 10% gap from options or understated maintenance capex. 0 = the case rests on adjusted EBITDA or "cash flow" that ignores required capex.
- **Quote:** "we consider the owner earnings figure, not the GAAP figure, to be the relevant item for valuation purposes" (S012)
- **Also:** "capital expenditures that over time roughly approximate depreciation are a necessity and are every bit as real an expense as labor or utility costs" (S015) and "I like to look at balance sheets over an 8 or 10 year period before I even look at the income account." (S134)
- **Sources:** 25 (S008, S009, S012, S013, S015, S016, S018, S023, S030, S032, S033, S034, S035, S036, S038, S041, S042, S043, S044, S045, S047, S049, S122, S130, S134).

## 3. Intrinsic value: discounted cash and the bird in the bush

- **What it is:** the only logical yardstick for any stock, bond or business: the cash that can be taken out over its remaining life, discounted to today. Book value says what was put in; intrinsic value estimates what can be taken out (S009, S019).
- **Parts:** (1) estimate the cash in and out over the asset's life; (2) discount at the long-term U.S. government rate, the same rate for every business, so risk is a go or no-go gate rather than a higher discount rate (S047, S080, S122); (3) work with a range, not a point: his band is about 10%, and he and Munger would land on different numbers (S030, S130); (4) growth is only a component of value, and it can subtract value when it needs early cash that exceeds the discounted later cash (S018, S047); (5) add the "what will they do with the money" judgment to the "what do we have now" figure (S030); (6) the same formula for stocks and whole businesses, taxes aside (S047). Expectations move value sharply: $1 million of earnings growing 6% forever at a 10% rate is worth $25 million, but only $10 million if it just bobs around (S017).
- **When to use it:** on every price decision, buyback and share issuance. Ask his three bush questions out loud: how certain, how many and when, and what the risk-free rate is.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = a conservative value range sits far above price (his example: a $300 to $500 range against an $80 price, S080). 5 = price inside the value band. 0 = no range can be formed, so the name goes to TOO HARD.
- **How his view changed:** per-share book value was the published proxy for decades, "crude, but useful" (S034); in the 2018 letter (S038) he dropped it as the lead metric because it had lost relevance. Newer: intrinsic value judged from operating earnings and investments.
- **Quote:** "Intrinsic value can be defined simply: It is the discounted value of the cash that can be taken out of a business during its remaining life." (S001)
- **Also:** "How certain are you that there are indeed birds in the bush? When will they emerge and how many will there be? What is the risk-free interest rate (which we consider to be the yield on long-term U.S. bonds)?" (S047) and "Now, the intrinsic value is not a specific point. It’s probably a range in my mind that might have a band maybe of 10%." (S130)
- **Sources:** 24 (S001, S009, S011, S015, S016, S017, S018, S019, S020, S022, S024, S025, S029, S030, S031, S034, S038, S045, S047, S050, S080, S085, S122, S130).

## 4. The moat: economic castle, franchise and scuttlebutt

- **What it is:** a durable barrier that protects high returns on capital from competitors. He wants a valuable castle, an honest and able duke, and a moat that widens every year.
- **Parts:** (1) franchise versus "a business": a franchise sells something needed or desired, seen by customers as having no close substitute, and not price-regulated; a mere business earns exceptional profits only as the low-cost producer or when supply is tight (S017); (2) moat sources: brand or share of mind (S079), lowest operating cost (GEICO at a 23.5% expense ratio against about 38% for rivals, S012; NFM at 15% of sales against 40%, S016), a government franchise, a dominant local paper (S009, S010); (3) the competitor test: how would I like to compete with it given ample capital and people (S009); (4) the moat must widen, and when short and long term conflict the moat wins (S025); (5) a moat that needs a superstar or constant rebuilding fails (S027); (6) the Inevitables: a few firms whose dominance for an investment lifetime no sensible observer doubts, against many Impostors (S022); (7) scuttlebutt: start with the economics as about 80% of the case, then use competitors, customers, suppliers and ex-employees as the last 10% to 20% to try to kill the thesis (S069, S122), and ask a CEO which one rival's stock he would own for ten years (S079, S134).
- **When to use it:** on every business-quality judgment and on every hold review. He tracks an investee by two tests each year: did earnings improve given industry conditions, and did the moat widen (S027).
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = passes all three franchise tests, the moat widened this year, and he would not want to compete with it even with unlimited capital. 5 = a real edge that must be rebuilt, or one tied to a star manager. 0 = commodity product with no cost edge. Watch for erosion: he moved newspapers from franchise toward "business" in 1991 (S017) and said eroding fundamentals overwhelm managerial brilliance (S026).
- **Quote:** "A truly great business must have an enduring moat that protects excellent returns on invested capital." (S027)
- **Also:** "One question I always ask myself in appraising a business is how I would like, assuming I had ample capital and skilled personnel, to compete with it." (S009) and "A moat that must be continuously rebuilt will eventually be no moat at all." (S027)
- **Sources:** 24: the moat itself in 21 (S009, S010, S012, S016, S017, S019, S021, S022, S024, S025, S027, S028, S032, S033, S034, S035, S050, S079, S080, S126, S130); scuttlebutt adds S069, S122, S134.

## 5. Circle of competence

- **What it is:** invest only where you can judge the business better than most, know exactly where the edge of that area is, and stay well inside it. The size of the circle matters far less than knowing its boundary.
- **Parts:** (1) define the circle with "extraordinary realism" (S010, Mrs. B's rule); (2) stay well inside the perimeter, not at it (S033, S046); (3) pass on anything outside it without regret, because missing a great outside opportunity is no sin (S015, S078); (4) expand it slowly by reading widely, never by bending the rules (S130); (5) what he names as outside: fast-moving technology, internet valuation, patents, manufacturing processes, geological prospects, drug pipelines, really complex instruments (S046, S079, S122, S126).
- **When to use it:** before any analysis. If the business sits outside, the seat says TOO HARD and abstains rather than scoring.
- **Scoring guidance:** this is a gate, not a scale. Inside the circle: go on to the other frameworks. At the perimeter: pass. Outside: TOO HARD.
- **How his view changed:** in 1967 he would not enter businesses where technology "away over my head" is crucial (S069), and in 1998 technology failed the filter outright (S122). By 2019 he said of missing Google, "we screwed up" (S130), having seen its ads work at GEICO, and by the 2020 letter Apple was one of Berkshire's four most valuable holdings (S040). Inferred from S040, S044, S130: the circle stretched to a consumer-tech franchise he could judge as a business, not to technology in general.
- **Quote:** "The size of that circle is not very important; knowing its boundaries, however, is vital." (S022)
- **Also:** "It’s vital, however, that we recognize the perimeter of our circle of competence and stay well inside of it." (S033) and "In the great majority of cases we simply do not know enough about the industry or company to come to sensible judgments -in that situation we pass." (S064)
- **Sources:** 22 (S010, S013, S015, S018, S019, S022, S033, S034, S037, S039, S046, S048, S064, S069, S078, S079, S080, S105, S116, S122, S126, S130).

## 6. Mr. Market, the farm test and investment versus speculation

- **What it is:** Graham's allegory of a manic-depressive partner who quotes a price every day, which you may ignore or exploit but must never follow. Buffett's own version is the farm: you own a farm or an apartment house with no daily quote and judge it by what it produces.
- **Parts:** (1) a stock is a fractional interest in a business (S024, S082); (2) the market is there to serve, not instruct (S013, S093, S126); (3) the closed-market test: would you be happy if the exchange shut for two to five years (S019, S079, S105, S126); (4) investing looks at what the asset will produce; speculating looks at what the next buyer will pay; gambling is a transaction the economy does not need (S033, S047, S083); (5) a bubble starts from a sound premise that people forget while watching only price, with leverage and incentives as fuel (S083).
- **When to use it:** when a stock has moved sharply and the user wants to act on the move, and whenever the thesis depends on someone else paying more later.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = the owner would be content if the quote vanished for five years. 0 = the case is the next buyer, a chart or a recent rise.
- **Quote:** "Mr. Market is there to serve you, not to guide you." (S013)
- **Also:** "When investing, we view ourselves as business analysts, not as market analysts, not as macroeconomic analysts, and not even as security analysts." (S013) and "Forget the quotes. Look at the business." (S097) and "If you instead focus on the prospective price change of a contemplated purchase, you are speculating." (S033)
- **Sources:** 20 (S010, S013, S019, S024, S028, S033, S041, S047, S074, S076, S080, S082, S083, S085, S093, S097, S105, S116, S126, S134).

## 7. The management test: owners, candor and the institutional imperative

- **What it is:** judge managers by what they do with owners' money and how straight they talk, not by charisma or EPS growth. The business still comes first.
- **Parts:** (1) honest and able, and for owner-managers, do they love the business or the money (S122); (2) run it as if you own 100%, it is your family's only asset, and you cannot sell for a century (S024, S045, S046); (3) the institutional imperative: resisting change, spending all available funds, rationalizing the boss's whims, imitating peers (S015); (4) the "what will they do with the money" factor, judged by the manager's record, since an outside owner stands by helplessly (S030); (5) repurchase behavior reveals motives (S010); (6) incentive pay tied to the unit's own results with a capital charge in both directions, no lottery tickets (S011, S020, S022, S026); fixed-price options reward a CEO for simply retaining earnings (S025); (7) business first: people come and go, and a great business does not need great management (S083, S116).
- **When to use it:** on every hold review, and immediately after a large acquisition, a stock issuance or a change in pay plan.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = owner-manager with real money in, candid about mistakes, buys back only below value, pay tied to controllable results with a capital charge. 5 = able but empire-minded. 0 = lottery-ticket options, projections trumpeted, deals done with undervalued stock.
- **Quote:** "Institutional dynamics, not venality or stupidity, set businesses on these courses" (S015)
- **Also:** "And we have to decide in that time when we meet them whether they love the business or love money." (S122) and "But the business overwhelmingly is the most important thing. Because people come and go." (S116)
- **Sources:** 20 (S005, S010, S011, S015, S017, S020, S022, S024, S025, S026, S027, S030, S045, S046, S048, S049, S083, S105, S116, S122).

## 8. Look-through earnings

- **What it is:** an owner's real earnings include his share of the earnings his investees retain, not only the dividends that show up in reported income. He wants to own a portfolio that acts like one company with the highest possible look-through earnings a decade out.
- **Parts:** (1) reported operating earnings, plus the share of investees' retained operating earnings, minus the tax that would be due if paid out (he used 14%, S016, S022); (2) for 15% value growth, look-through earnings must grow about 15% (S015, S018); (3) retained earnings at owner-oriented investees are worth at least as much as reported ones; the Big Four's $2.4 billion unreported share in 2011 created at least that much value (S031); (4) over time retained earnings turned into more than $1 of capital gains per $1 retained (S038, S039).
- **When to use it:** on any holding that pays little dividend, and to judge whether a portfolio's real earning power is growing.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = the investee reinvests at high returns and retained earnings have shown up as at least equal market value over five years. 5 = pays out most of its earnings with modest reinvestment. 0 = retains earnings and buries them in low-return projects.
- **Quote:** "The goal of each investor should be to create a portfolio (in effect, a company) that will deliver him or her the highest possible look-through earnings a decade or so from now." (S017)
- **Also:** "our economic well-being is determined by their earnings, not their dividends." (S006) and "earnings retained by our investees (viewed as a group) have eventually delivered capital gains to Berkshire that totaled more than one dollar for each dollar these companies reinvested for us." (S038)
- **Sources:** 19 (S001, S006, S008, S015, S016, S017, S018, S019, S020, S022, S031, S033, S035, S037, S038, S039, S040, S046, S047).

## 9. Fat pitch, punch card and concentration

- **What it is:** there are no called strikes in investing, so wait for the pitch in your best zone, then swing hard. Pretend you have a lifetime card of 20 punches.
- **Parts:** (1) Ted Williams' 77-cell strike zone: swing only at the best cell (S020, S023); (2) the punch card of 20 ideas (S080); (3) when one qualifies, buy a meaningful amount (S022); avoid lukewarm small positions (S004); (4) the know-something investor needs five to ten sensibly priced companies with long-term advantages, or about six (S019, S079); owning 100 stocks is the Noah School of Investing (S067); (5) the partnership limit: up to 40% of net worth in one idea only when the facts are very probably right and the value is very unlikely to change drastically (S067, S069); (6) when opportunity rains, use a bucket, not a thimble (S029, S036).
- **When to use it:** on sizing an add, on a decision to hold cash, and when a user wants to spread money over many names they half know.
- **Scoring guidance:** sizing guidance, not a score: a high-conviction idea inside the circle can carry 10% to 40% of a know-something investor's money; a lukewarm one gets nothing. The 2002 hurdle for owning stocks was a very high probability of at least 10% pre-tax returns (S049).
- **Quote:** "You're not going to get more than 20 investment ideas in a lifetime." (S080)
- **Also:** "waiting for the fat pitch would mean a trip to the Hall of Fame; swinging indiscriminately would mean a ticket to the minors." (S023) and "if you are a know-something investor, able to understand business economics and to find five to ten sensibly-priced companies that possess important long-term competitive advantages, conventional diversification makes no sense for you." (S019)
- **Sources:** 19 (S003, S004, S010, S014, S019, S020, S022, S023, S029, S036, S049, S067, S069, S072, S079, S080, S122, S130, S134).

## 10. The repurchase test

- **What it is:** a buyback is a purchase like any other: smart below intrinsic value, harmful to continuing owners above it.
- **Parts:** (1) two conditions: the company has funds beyond the needs of the business, and the stock sells at a material discount to intrinsic value, conservatively calculated (S031, S046); (2) partnership arithmetic: three partners in a $3 million business; buying one out at $900,000 lifts each remaining stake to $1,050,000, at $1.1 million it costs them (S036, S130); (3) offsetting dilution or having spare cash is no reason (S031); (4) never buy to prop or stem a fall in price (S039, S046); (5) disclose to selling holders what you would want to know in their place (S130); (6) a manager who refuses buybacks that clearly help owners reveals his motives (S010).
- **When to use it:** on any company that buys back stock, and on judging management's capital allocation.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = buying at 25% to 30% below a conservative value, with ample cash left, which justifies very large sums (S130). 5 = a modest discount, worth only token amounts. 0 = buying above value, or on a fixed schedule, or to offset option issuance.
- **How his view changed:** in 2011 Berkshire capped buybacks at 110% of book (S031); in December 2012 the cap rose to 120% because 110% proved unrealistic (S032); by the 2019 letter the test was simply whether he and Munger believe the stock sells for less than it is worth (S039). Newer: a value judgment with no book multiple.
- **Quote:** "The first law of capital allocation, whether the money is slated for acquisitions or share repurchases, is that what is smart at one price is dumb at another." (S031)
- **Also:** "Buying dollar bills for $1.10 is not good business for those who stick around." (S046) and "We will buy stock when we think it is selling below a conservative estimate of its intrinsic value." (S130)
- **Sources:** 18 (S001, S004, S006, S010, S031, S032, S034, S035, S036, S038, S039, S040, S041, S042, S043, S046, S130, S134).

## 11. The retained-dollar test and the capital allocation ladder

- **What it is:** a company should keep a dollar of earnings only if it turns that dollar into more than a dollar of value; otherwise it should pay it out or buy back stock. Where the dollar goes follows a fixed order.
- **Parts:** (1) the $1 test, checked over five years (S001, S008, S122); (2) restricted earnings (needed just to stand still under inflation) versus unrestricted earnings (free to go where they earn most) (S010); (3) the ladder: reinvest in the existing businesses to widen the moat, then acquisitions, then marketable stocks, then buybacks below value, with dividends last (S032, S039, S041); (4) money need not be reinvested where it was earned (S027); (5) if retained dollars become $1.10 or $1.20 of value, owners do better selling a small slice each year than taking a dividend (S032, S126).
- **When to use it:** on any company deciding between growth spending, acquisitions, buybacks and dividends, and to judge whether a high-retention company has earned the right to retain.
- **Scoring guidance:** red lights should flash if the five-year average gain falls much below the return on equity of American industry over the period (S009). See's, which cannot use its cash, should send it all to the parent (S126).
- **How his view changed:** he says he stated the five-year retention test wrongly in 1983 and later corrected it: over five years book-value gain should beat the S&P, and the stock should consistently sell at a premium to book (S001). Newer: the corrected two-part test.
- **Quote:** "We test the wisdom of retaining earnings by assessing whether retention, over time, delivers shareholders at least $1 of market value for each $1 retained." (S001)
- **Also:** "you should wish your earnings to be reinvested if they can be expected to earn high returns, and you should wish them paid to you if low returns are the likely outcome of reinvestment." (S010) and "Reinvestment in productive operational assets will forever remain our top priority." (S039)
- **Sources:** 18 (S001, S004, S007, S008, S009, S010, S012, S021, S027, S030, S032, S036, S039, S041, S045, S079, S122, S126).

## 12. Return on unleveraged tangible capital (the quality screen)

- **What it is:** a business is good if it earns a high return on the tangible capital it actually needs, without leverage or accounting tricks. Return on equity, not EPS growth, is the test of managers.
- **Parts:** (1) earnings on unleveraged net tangible assets, before goodwill amortization (S009); (2) ROE without undue leverage or gimmickry, not steady EPS gains, which retained earnings alone produce (S003, S005, S011); (3) separate return on tangible net worth from return on carrying value: Berkshire's manufacturing and retail group earned 21.7% on tangible net worth but 9.9% on carrying value because it paid premiums (S024, S050); (4) a 12% return from a debt-free company is far better than the same from a leveraged one (S090); (5) do not buy on the assumption that record returns persist (S122).
- **When to use it:** first numerical check on any business, and on any acquirer whose returns on carrying value are collapsing under goodwill.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = 25% after tax to more than 100% on unleveraged net tangible assets (S030, S031), or his Fortune test of average ROE over 20% for ten years with no year under 15% (S013). 5 = 12% to 20% (S031, S035). 0 = 5% to 6% on equity, where time works against the owner however cheap the price (S122), or a return that cannot support the assets employed, as in textiles (S076).
- **Quote:** "The primary test of managerial economic performance is the achievement of a high earnings rate on equity capital employed (without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc.) and not the achievement of consistent gains in earnings per share." (S005)
- **Also:** "Some of the businesses enjoy terrific economics, measured by earnings on unleveraged net tangible assets that run from 25% after-tax to more than 100%." (S030) and "Time is the enemy of the poor business, and it’s the friend of the great business." (S122)
- **Sources:** 17 (S003, S005, S009, S011, S013, S014, S024, S030, S031, S035, S037, S039, S050, S074, S076, S090, S122).

## 13. From cigar butts to wonderful businesses (his biggest change of method)

- **What it is:** the record of how he decides changed once. The partnership bought statistical bargains; Berkshire buys wonderful businesses at fair prices. Both are on record, so the seat must know which applies.
- **Older view (1957 to about 1972):** Graham-style categories: Generals (cheap on a private-owner basis or relative to peers), Workouts (announced corporate events), and Controls (S056, S062, S064). Value balance-sheet items at quick-sale haircuts, as with Dempster (S059); buy 100 such cases and perhaps 70 or 80 work out in one to three years (S062). Generals Private Owner had profits probably fifty times its losses (S074).
- **Newer view (from See's in 1972, stated clearly from 1989):** Munger's lesson: a wonderful business at a fair price beats a fair business at a wonderful price (S015, S032, S034, S043). Time is the friend of the wonderful business and the enemy of the lousy one (S079, S122). Cigar butts do not scale (S034), and Graham's method works only at small scale (S043, quoting Munger).
- **When to use it:** on any stock that looks cheap on book value or earnings but has poor economics. The older method is how he would still advise someone with small sums (S126).
- **Scoring guidance:** a cheap price on a poor business scores low on this seat. Rule he gives himself after relapses: "I have sometimes reverted to my old habit of bargain-hunting, with results ranging from tolerable to terrible." (S032)
- **Quote:** "Forget what you know about buying fair businesses at wonderful prices; instead, buy wonderful businesses at fair prices." (S034)
- **Also:** "A cigar butt found on the street that has only one puff left in it may not offer much of a smoke" (S015) and on how long it took: "always a quick study, required only 20 years to recognize how important it was to buy good businesses" (S013)
- **Sources:** 17 (S004, S005, S013, S015, S018, S032, S034, S043, S056, S059, S062, S064, S067, S074, S079, S122, S130).

## 14. Fear, greed and the net buyer's arithmetic

- **What it is:** a lifetime buyer of businesses should want lower prices, the way a hamburger eater wants cheaper beef. So lean against crowd emotion: fear when others are greedy, greed when they are fearful.
- **Parts:** (1) do not predict the emotional epidemics, just lean against them (S012, S026); (2) the hamburger and IBM arithmetic: a net buyer, directly or through a company buying back stock, is hurt when prices rise (S023, S031); (3) start every analysis from fear, then get greedy when something looks attractive (S105); (4) the falling-price test: a lower price is good news if the business is intact, and a reason to sell and take the loss if you were wrong about the business (S116, his Tesco case); (5) fear is justified only for leveraged firms and weak competitive positions (S087).
- **When to use it:** when a good business has fallen hard on bad news, and when a user wants to add to a winner in a euphoric market.
- **Scoring guidance:** buy more only if the moat and earnings tests still pass after the fall; if the company itself is losing value, the drop is information, not opportunity.
- **Quote:** "we simply attempt to be fearful when others are greedy and to be greedy only when others are fearful." (S012)
- **Also:** "If you are going to be a net buyer of stocks in the future, either directly with your own money or indirectly (through your ownership of a company that is repurchasing shares), you are hurt when stocks rise." (S031) and "stock going down is a good thing unless the company itself is-- is losing value" (S116)
- **Sources:** 17 (S003, S008, S012, S016, S020, S023, S024, S026, S028, S031, S033, S036, S085, S087, S097, S105, S116).

## 15. The per-share value test for deals and share issuance

- **What it is:** every acquisition, merger and stock issue is judged by one number: does per-share intrinsic value go up for the owners who stay? Size and EPS accretion do not count.
- **Parts:** (1) never give more intrinsic value in shares than you receive (S001, S008, S034); (2) using an undervalued stock to buy a fully valued business hurts owners (S029); (3) a takeover premium makes sense only if your stock is overvalued or the combination earns more (S023); (4) evaluate deals on an all-equity basis and never count synergies (S037, S047); (5) would you sell 100% of the business on these terms (S008); (6) boards should hire a second adviser paid only if the deal fails, or one for and one against (S029, S039); (7) run honest post-mortems against the original projections (S034).
- **When to use it:** whenever a holding announces an acquisition, especially for stock, and when judging a CEO's record.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = cash deal, all-equity analysis, value received at least equal to value given, no synergies needed. 0 = undervalued stock issued for a fully valued target, justified by synergies and banker projections.
- **How his view changed:** he admits his own stock deals cost owners (S023, 1998; S025, 2006; Dexter, S047). By 2017 his stance hardened: "Today, I would rather prep for a colonoscopy than issue Berkshire shares." (S036) Newer: avoid issuing stock almost entirely; the BNSF deal used about 30% stock only because he judged value received equal or a bit more (S105, 2010).
- **Quote:** "The intrinsic value of the shares you give in an acquisition must not be greater than the intrinsic value of the business you receive." (S034)
- **Also:** "You simply can’t exchange an undervalued stock for a fully-valued one without hurting your shareholders." (S029) and "Don’t ask the barber whether you need a haircut." (S029)
- **Sources:** 16 (S001, S008, S009, S018, S020, S023, S024, S025, S029, S034, S036, S037, S039, S046, S047, S105).

## 16. Balance sheet first: survive to play tomorrow

- **What it is:** a long string of good results multiplied by one zero is zero, so the first financial check is whether the business can survive the worst case without anyone's help.
- **Parts:** (1) judge debt by acceptable results under extraordinarily adverse conditions, not optimal results in normal ones (S013); (2) three strengths: a large reliable earnings stream, massive liquid assets, no significant near-term cash demands (S034); (3) shun contracts that can force instant collateral calls (S031); (4) for any holding, leverage and a weak competitive position are where fear is warranted (S087, S097); (5) coverage measured as pre-tax earnings over interest (S032).
- **When to use it:** on any leveraged company, any bank or insurer, and in any crisis when a holding may be forced to raise equity at a low price (his Wells Fargo caveat, S097).
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = little or no debt, no near-term cash calls. 0 = a business that could be forced to sell or dilute in a freeze.
- **How his view changed:** Berkshire's cash floor rose with its size: a pledge of at least $10 billion (S030, 2011), a $20 billion working level (S031, S034), then more than $30 billion (S041, 2022). Newer: over $30 billion.
- **Quote:** "acceptable long-term results under extraordinarily adverse conditions, rather than optimal results under a normal range of conditions" (S013)
- **Also:** "a long string of impressive numbers multiplied by a single zero always equals zero." (S025) and "investors are right to be wary of highly leveraged entities or businesses in weak competitive positions" (S087)
- **Sources:** 16 (S001, S006, S013, S025, S028, S030, S031, S032, S034, S041, S043, S087, S090, S097, S130, S134).

## 17. Great, good and gruesome (the incremental capital test)

- **What it is:** judge growth by the capital it eats. Think of three savings accounts: one that pays a rising rate and needs no deposits, one that pays a fair rate on new deposits, and one that demands deposits and pays almost nothing.
- **Parts:** (1) great: See's, bought in 1972 for $25 million with about $8 million of capital; by 2007 it earned $82 million pre-tax on $40 million, needing only $32 million reinvested (S027); (2) good: attractive returns that also apply to added capital, such as FlightSafety, which needs $3.50 of capital per $1 of revenue (S024, S027); (3) gruesome: grows fast, needs heavy capital, earns little; think airlines (S027); (4) always ask how much extra capital produced the extra earnings; the average U.S. business needed about $5 of capital per $1 of added pre-tax earnings (S011, S017); (5) the best business to own can employ large incremental capital at very high returns, which is rare (S018, S043).
- **When to use it:** on any growth story, and on any business where revenue growth is being sold as the thesis.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = rising earnings with tiny incremental capital, or rare high returns on large reinvestment. 5 = decent returns on heavy capital (utilities, railroads). 0 = growth that consumes capital at low returns.
- **How his view changed:** in 1983 he preferred businesses that use "a minimum of tangible assets" (S009); as cash piled up he accepted capital-intensive businesses with decent returns on the increments (S029, 2010; "Asset-heavy companies, however, can be good investments." S040, 2021). Newer still: he calls not anticipating adverse regulatory returns at the utilities a costly mistake (S043, 2024) and says enthusiasm for buying utilities has changed (S134, 2025).
- **Quote:** "The worst sort of business is one that grows rapidly, requires significant capital to engender the growth, and then earns little or no money. Think airlines." (S027)
- **Also:** "it’s always better to make a lot of money without putting up anything than to make a lot of money by putting up a lot of money." (S134) and "For an increase in profits to be evaluated properly, it must be compared with the incremental capital investment required to produce it." (S017)
- **Sources:** 14 (S004, S009, S011, S017, S018, S024, S027, S029, S040, S043, S080, S105, S122, S134).

## 18. Real risk, productive assets and the inflation test

- **What it is:** risk is the reasoned chance of losing purchasing power over your holding period, not price volatility or beta. Productive assets that keep their output's value without much new capital are both the best and the safest holding.
- **Parts:** (1) permanent capital loss, not quotational loss (S059); (2) beta is nonsense for business owners; the cheaper price is the less risky one (S019, S082, S122); (3) three categories: currency-based assets, which look safe but lose purchasing power; assets that produce nothing, such as gold; and productive assets: businesses, farms, real estate (S031, S086); (4) the inflation double test: output that keeps its value, with minimal new capital (S086); (5) the investor's misery index: inflation plus taxes against the business's return on equity (S005, S090).
- **When to use it:** on asset-allocation and cash questions, and on any business during inflation.
- **Scoring guidance:** passes: See's, Coca-Cola, farms, real estate (S086). Fails: regulated utilities, long fixed-rate bonds (S086, S005). His 1977 arithmetic: 12% before tax and inflation, about 7% after tax, and maybe zero after inflation (S090).
- **Quote:** "The riskiness of an investment is not measured by beta" (S031)
- **Also:** "Volatility is far from synonymous with risk." (S034) and "Ideally, these assets should have the ability in inflationary times to deliver output that will retain its purchasing-power value while requiring a minimum of new capital investment" (S086)
- **Sources:** 14 (S005, S006, S007, S019, S030, S031, S034, S037, S059, S082, S086, S090, S097, S122).

## 19. The know-nothing investor and the index fund

- **What it is:** most investors cannot value businesses and should own a low-cost index fund, buy it over time and never sell on bad news. It is also the bogey any active pick must beat after costs.
- **Parts:** (1) know-nothing versus know-something investors (S019); (2) Gotrocks and the Helpers: owners in aggregate earn only what businesses earn, minus frictional costs (S025); (3) active versus passive arithmetic: the lower-cost group wins (S027, S036); (4) the ten-year bet: the S&P index returned 7.1% a year against 2.2% for funds of hedge funds after nine years (S036); (5) his trustee instructions: 90% in a very low-cost S&P 500 fund, 10% in short-term government bonds (S033).
- **When to use it:** when the user cannot pass the circle-of-competence gate, and as the hurdle for any stock pick.
- **Scoring guidance:** if a pick is not expected to beat the index after costs and taxes, the index wins by default (S024, S030).
- **Quote:** "By periodically investing in an index fund, for example, the know-nothing investor can actually out-perform most investment professionals." (S019)
- **Also:** "the most that owners in aggregate can earn between now and Judgment Day is what their businesses in aggregate earn." (S025) and "Put 10% of the cash in short-term government bonds and 90% in a very low-cost S&P 500 index fund." (S033)
- **Sources:** 14 (S019, S022, S024, S025, S026, S027, S030, S033, S036, S050, S079, S093, S116, S126).

## 20. Margin of safety and the value range

- **What it is:** Graham's three words. Because value estimates are imprecise, buy only when the price sits far enough below a conservative value that being wrong still works. Then precision stops mattering.
- **Parts:** (1) protect against estimation error two ways: simple, stable businesses, and a wide price gap (S018); (2) the bridge rule: build for 30,000 pounds, drive 10,000-pound trucks (S082); (3) work from a range and act only when the gap is huge (S080, S126); (4) buy against the bottom of a five-year earnings range (S033); (5) have the purchase price be so attractive that even a mediocre sale gives good results (S059).
- **When to use it:** on every entry price, and when a user asks how precise the valuation needs to be.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = 40 cents for a dollar of value (S082); PetroChina at about $35 billion against value near $100 billion (S126). 5 = around 80 cents, his "hard to go wrong" buyback level (S032). 0 = value only slightly above price (S018).
- **Quote:** "If we calculate the value of a common stock to be only slightly higher than its price, we're not interested in buying." (S018)
- **Also:** "If your range is $300 to $500 and it's selling for $80 you don't need to be more accurate than that." (S080) and "When you build a bridge, you insist it can carry 30,000 pounds, but you only drive 10,000-pound trucks across it." (S082)
- **Sources:** 13 (S016, S018, S023, S033, S047, S056, S059, S062, S064, S080, S082, S105, S126).

## 21. Predictability: can you see the earnings 5, 10 and 20 years out?

- **What it is:** first decide whether you can sensibly estimate an earnings range five or more years ahead. If not, move on, which is the usual answer. If yes, price against the bottom of the range.
- **Parts:** (1) think only about what is important and knowable; macro is important but not knowable (S079, S122); (2) prefer one or two dominant variables: ten independent variables at 90% each give only a 35% chance of success (S024); (3) severe change and exceptional returns rarely mix (S013); (4) the Inevitables over the hopefuls; certainty of a good result over hope of a great one (S022); (5) pet rock versus Barbie: if you cannot tell a fad from a franchise, stay away (S019).
- **When to use it:** on any fast-changing industry, any early-stage company, and any thesis that needs several things to go right.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = he has a pretty good idea where it will be in ten years, as with Coca-Cola (S105). 5 = industry growth visible but margins not (S029). 0 = many independent variables or unproven technology.
- **Quote:** "we will buy the stock (or business) if it sells at a reasonable price in relation to the bottom boundary of our estimate." (S033)
- **Also:** "if ten independent variables need to break favorably for a successful result, and each has a 90% probability of success, the likelihood of having a winner is only 35%." (S024) and "I would rather be certain of a good result than hopeful of a great one." (S022)
- **Sources:** 12 (S013, S019, S022, S024, S029, S033, S069, S079, S105, S116, S122, S134).

## 22. Commodity economics and the low-cost producer

- **What it is:** in an undifferentiated product with overcapacity, returns are poor unless prices are fixed, supply is short, or you are the lowest-cost producer. Good management cannot repeal this.
- **Parts:** (1) undifferentiated goods in capital-intensive businesses earn inadequate returns except in shortage (S004, S011); (2) persistent overcapacity without administered prices equals poor profitability (S008); (3) the low-cost producer must press its edge "foot-to-the-floor" (S024); (4) a bank's cheap deposits work like a low-cost copper mine (S105); (5) brilliant managers on poor economics: the economics win (S006, S015, S026).
- **When to use it:** on any airline, textile, steel, commodity insurer, shoe or retailer case, and when a turnaround depends on a star manager.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = clearly the lowest-cost operator with the gap widening (GEICO, NFM, Wells Fargo deposits). 0 = undifferentiated product, overcapacity, no cost edge; his textile and Dexter Shoe cases (S020, S047).
- **Quote:** "producers of relatively undifferentiated goods in capital intensive businesses must earn inadequate returns except under conditions of tight supply or real shortage." (S004)
- **Also:** "when a company is selling a product with commodity-like economic characteristics, being the low-cost producer is all-important." (S047) and "When an industry's underlying economics are crumbling, talented management may slow the rate of decline. Eventually, though, eroding fundamentals will overwhelm managerial brilliance." (S026)
- **Sources:** 12 (S004, S006, S008, S011, S013, S015, S020, S024, S026, S047, S105, S126).

## 23. The pricing-power test

- **What it is:** the single most important question about a business: can it raise prices without losing customers to a rival? He bought Moody's on this basis with no management meetings.
- **Parts:** (1) raise prices easily, even with flat demand and spare capacity, without losing share or volume (S007, his Category 1); (2) handle inflation-driven volume growth with little new capital (S007); (3) how long does management have to think before a price rise (S080); (4) a brand is a promise in the customer's mind (S126); See's had untapped pricing power when bought (S034, S079).
- **When to use it:** first numerical-free check on any consumer, information or franchise business, and in any inflation question.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = price rises taken routinely with no share loss. 0 = a prayer session before raising the price a tenth of a cent.
- **Quote:** "the single most important decision in evaluating a business is pricing power" (S083)
- **Also:** "how long does the management have to think before they decide to raise prices?" (S080) and "if you have to have a prayer session before raising the price by a tenth of a cent ... then you got a terrible business" (S083)
- **Sources:** 10 (S007, S009, S017, S034, S079, S080, S083, S086, S097, S126).

## 24. Market-level gauges (valued roughly, never timed)

- **What it is:** he does not predict the market but values it roughly, to set expectations and to know when prices are dangerous. Interest rates are gravity; corporate returns on equity are the coupon.
- **Parts:** (1) interest rates act on valuations like gravity (S085, S116); (2) total market value to GNP: buying works well near 70% to 80%, near 200% you are playing with fire; it read 133% in late 2001 (S085); (3) stocks as bonds with a sticky 12% equity coupon; paying 150% of book cuts the owner's return to about 10% (S090); (4) profits grow roughly with GDP, so valuations cannot outrun it for long (S046); (5) stocks are not overvalued only if rates stay put or fall and returns on equity stay high (S023, S122).
- **When to use it:** on macro or "is the market expensive" questions, and to sanity-check return assumptions (his 6.5% pension assumption against the common 8%, S093). Never to time a single stock.
- **Scoring guidance:** guidance only: low GNP ratio and high rates make broad buying easier; a ratio near 200% means demand a much wider margin of safety on any single name.
- **Quote:** "Though we don't attempt to predict the movements of the stock market, we do try, in a very rough way, to value it." (S023)
- **Also:** "If the ratio approaches 200%, as it did in 1999 and a part of 2000, you are playing with fire" (S085) and "interest rates are like gravity in valuations." (S116)
- **Sources:** 9 (S023, S027, S041, S046, S076, S085, S090, S116, S122).

## 25. Float and the cost of float (for insurers)

- **What it is:** an insurer collects premiums now and pays claims later, holding money it does not own. That float is valuable only if its cost, the underwriting loss, is below what the company would pay for funds elsewhere. It is the engine of Berkshire.
- **Parts:** (1) three determinants: the amount of float, its cost, and most critical the long-term outlook for both (S045, S049); (2) cost of float is underwriting loss over float, compared with the long-term government yield; the combined ratio is largely irrelevant (S016, S017, S019); (3) float costing less than zero is better than equity (S021, S122); (4) the four disciplines: understand the exposures, conservatively estimate likelihood and cost, price for a profit, and walk away if the premium cannot be had (S030, S031, S034); (5) avoid aggregation of correlated losses and moral risk; never write without a cap (S047, S048, S049); (6) float is a revolving fund, not just a liability (S031, S034).
- **When to use it:** on any insurer or reinsurer, and on any business that holds customers' money before delivering.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = underwriting profit year after year, so float is free money (S134). 5 = cost of float below the government bond yield (it beat the government rate in 21 of 26 years to 1992, S018). 0 = cost above market rates for money: "the business is a lemon" (S045).
- **Quote:** "An insurance business has value if its cost of float over time is less than the cost the company would otherwise incur to obtain funds." (S022)
- **Also:** "The key determinants are: (1) the amount of float that the business generates; (2) its cost; and (3) most critical of all, the long-term outlook for both of these factors." (S049) and "(4) be willing to walk away if the appropriate premium can’t be obtained." (S031)
- **Sources:** 35 (S001, S012, S014, S016, S017, S018, S019, S020, S021, S022, S023, S024, S025, S026, S027, S028, S029, S030, S031, S033, S034, S036, S037, S038, S039, S041, S044, S045, S046, S048, S049, S050, S122, S130, S134).
