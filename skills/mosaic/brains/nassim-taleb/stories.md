# Stories: Nassim Nicholas Taleb

Built from the six verified batch notes over 56 sources (papers 2012 to 2026, testimonies 2009 and 2011, interviews and transcripts 2010 to 2025). The blowups, trades and case studies he returns to, most-used first. Most of his stories are other people's blowups, used as warnings; his own trades and his own bitcoin mistake are given equal room. Figures are as he reported them.

## 1. LTCM, 1998: the ten sigma excuse
Sources: 7 (S001, S007, S015, S029, S041, S051, S055).
Nobel-decorated partners blew up a hedge fund on risk methods that misjudged the rare event, then called it a ten-sigma move; he counts at least sixty finance PhDs blown up on the same trades and methods (S001). Regulators kept VaR and pushed Basel 2 anyway (S055), and the Fed rescue let exposure to rare events grow (S029). After LTCM, index calls traded below any plausible volatility because capital constraints stopped arbitrage: "With European options, such dislocations can become terminal to a trading desk, that is, they threaten extinction." (S041)
Lesson: apply Wittgenstein's ruler. "it is much more likely that they have the wrong distribution than it is that you really have ten-sigma event" (S001) A model is not safe because of who built it (S029).

## 2. Banks lose all their past profits in one episode
Sources: 7 (S001, S007, S029, S032, S046, S051, S055).
Money center banks "lost in 1982 more money than they ever made in their history" (S001); about 4.7 trillion dollars went in 2008 before bailouts, with similar episodes in 1982 to 1983 and the savings and loan crisis (S007). "Banks lost more money in two episodes, 1982 and 2008, than they made in the history of banking" (S051) In 2007 he could tell they would break because they were net short volatility, without naming the trigger (S046, paraphrase).
Lesson: a business that earns steadily by selling the tail looks profitable until it gives everything back. "Data shows that banks routinely lose everything earned in their past history in single blowups" (S055)

## 3. Fannie Mae: sitting on dynamite
Sources: 3 (S035, S037, S055).
He warned in The Black Swan in 2007 that its losses accelerated as rates moved; a deserter's reports showed losses growing about 20 times as rates moved 200 basis points (S037). Fannie Mae answered that it had PhDs. "whether you've got 15 PhD or 15 trillion PhDs, it's not going to help you with this." (S037) It later booked about 600 billion dollars of losses (S037).
Lesson: the perturbation test finds fragility without a forecast. "Fannie Mae, when I look at its risks, seems to be sitting on a barrel of dynamite, vulnerable to the slightest hiccup." (S055)

## 4. Niederhoffer and Refco: short options destroyed by repricing alone
Sources: 3 (S001, S021, S037).
Options sold far out of the money were bought back at panic prices without ever getting near the money. "options sold for $.05 were purchased back at up to $38, which bankrupted Refco" (S021) Retold in 2023: "He sold them for five, 10 cents and then he had to buy them back and was buying them back up to $40." (S037)
Lesson: selling cheap tails carries ruin even when the event never arrives; a spike in implied volatility is enough (S001, S021).

## 5. Bob Rubin and the $120 million: upside without downside
Sources: 3 (S036, S053, S055).
Rubin took about 120 million dollars over ten years at Citi, offered an apology after 2008 and kept the money (S036). He wants bankers like that clawed back (S053) and calls the arrangement "capitalism for the profits and socialism for the losses." (S055)
Lesson: judge a manager by whether he eats his losses. "you make the upside when you're right, and when you're wrong, who pays for the losses?" (S036)

## 6. Hedge funds in 2008: the best records failed first
Sources: 3 (S001, S007, S025).
The funds with the best Sharpe ratios before the crisis were no protection and, if anything, weakly predicted failure (S001). "hedge funds with the best track records turned out to be the most vulnerable to tail events in 2008" (S025) JPMorgan and Bank of America had perfect quarterly trading records before the 2008 losses (S007).
Lesson: a smooth record under fat tails is a warning, not a credential (S001, S007).

## 7. Bitcoin: his own mistake, then a short
Sources: 3 (S006, S047, S048).
Before 2021 he owned some and hoped it would protect savers from Fed distortions. "I wrongly thought bitcoin would be a bulwark against the distortions of this monetary policy." (S048) He saw it fall further than stocks in March 2020 (S006), published a 2021 paper that it fails as currency, inflation hedge and refuge, exited, and later went short. "So I realized quickly that I made a mistake with Bitcoin." (S047)
Lesson: keep the process, change the action when the test fails. "They don't realize that I'm not selling a recipe. I'm selling a process." (S047)

## 8. The variance swap blowup, fall 2017
Sources: 1 (S001).
A firm bet that realized volatility would be low, forecast correctly, and still went bust, because the same average move can come evenly or all in one day. "They were correct in the prediction, but went bust nevertheless." (S001) and "They were just very concave in the payoff function." (S001)
Lesson: better convex than right.

## 9. 1987 and Lehman: his own tail trades
Sources: 1 (S037).
Before the 1987 crash, on the Plaza Accord announcement, his P&L exploded against a tiny maximum loss, and inspectors were sent to find the hidden risk. "You made too much money, you've got to be taking risk." (S037) He decided then to make a living from the asymmetry. On Lehman day he was on a plane, landed to the news, and the tail trade had paid (S037).
Lesson: a convex position earns little most days and a great deal on the rare one; the rare day is the business.

## 10. The client who overrode the hedge with a correlation trade
Sources: 1 (S037).
A manager who took clients from Empirica overrode the strategy: he bought S&P 500 puts and sold German index puts to collect cash. The German market moved violently and the firm went out of business. "So there was a dependence on correlation." (S037)
Lesson: a hedge that needs a correlation to hold is not a hedge.

## 11. Morgan Stanley: right on direction, hurt anyway
Sources: 1 (S035).
It predicted the credit crisis and still needed rescue, because it did not anticipate the size of the move. Close to 1,000 institutions shut in 2007 and 2008 from underestimating outsized moves (S035).
Lesson: being right on the forecast is not enough when the payoff is open-ended (S035).

## 12. The trader who said up and was short
Sources: 3 (S001, S005, S044).
Asked where the market was going, a trader in Fooled by Randomness said up, and was short. "the market is more likely to go up, but should it go down it will fall much much more" (S001) The manager's anger showed a belief and an exposure are different things (S005).
Lesson: probability of direction is not expected payoff (S044).

## 13. The turkey and the great moderation
Sources: 3 (S003, S029, S033).
A turkey fed for a thousand days grows more confident each day. "On the 1001st day, the turkey has a surprise." (S029) He applies it to Bernanke's great moderation and the banks (S029, S003), and to H. T. Buckle, who in 1860 said wars were declining before the most murderous century in history (S033).
Lesson: a calm stretch in a fat-tailed series is not a trend.

## 14. The casino and day 29
Sources: 2 (S001, S032).
A hundred people gamble for a day; the 28th going bust does not hurt the 29th, and the casino's edge is an ensemble result. One person gambling a hundred days who busts on day 28 has no day 29. "if one of us goes to the casino and on day 28 is ruined, there is no day 29." (S032)
Lesson: the market's average is not your path. Goldman Sachs and Buffett, as survivors, want zero risk of ruin, not small risk (S001, S032).

## 15. Goldman Sachs: 130 years as a partnership
Sources: 2 (S001, S032).
"it ran as partnership with unlimited liability for approximately the first 130 years, but was bailed out once in 2009, after it became a bank" (S032)
Lesson: unlimited liability is skin in the game, and it kept the firm alive; removing it changed the risk taking (S001, S032).

## 16. Silicon Valley Bank: no upside, all downside
Sources: 1 (S047).
SVB bought long-dated bonds paying almost nothing as rates were set to rise; the failure was duration, not credit. "you really have to have deep misunderstanding of finance to invest very long term in bonds that pay you no interest. Because basically you have no upside and all downside from there." (S047)
Lesson: a concave position can look safe because it holds safe assets.

## 17. Dexia passed its stress test
Sources: 1 (S002).
"Dexia went into financial distress a few days after passing a stress test" (S002) A test set at one arbitrary level misses what sits below it.
Lesson: shock the input at several sizes and look for losses that accelerate (S002).

## 18. The Hunt silver squeeze
Sources: 1 (S006).
Hoarding in the 1970s took silver and gold up fivefold to tenfold; then the metals gave back more than half and, 41 years later, had not regained their inflation-adjusted peaks (S006).
Lesson: a squeeze-driven price is not a store of value, and metals failed as inflation hedges after it (S006).

## 19. Greenspan and the forest fires
Sources: 1 (S054).
Easing worked in 1987, so it was repeated in 1991, 2000 and 2002 until rates hit zero. "So he thought he had the magic formula, and had you given him nature he would eliminate seasons." (S054)
Lesson: suppressing small crises uses up the tool and builds a bigger one.

## 20. Universa: the same strategy for nearly two decades
Sources: 3 (S046, S047, S051).
He says the positioning did not change with headlines: "in 2023, the positioning is identical to the one in 2013" (S047) and "We didn't change strategy for a second. And we will not change strategy." (S046) He credits Mark Spitznagel's discipline: "in more than two decades, I never saw him once deviate a micro-inch from a given protocol." (S051) He declines to give returns (S046).
Lesson: a tail strategy is proven by consistency over many years, not by one year's score; the edge is behavioral and in execution (S051).
