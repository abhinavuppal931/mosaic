# Frameworks: Aswath Damodaran

Built from the verified notes on 100 sources (91 blog posts, 2012 to 2026, and 9 NYU Stern papers, 2005 to 2012). Most-used first. Scoring lines are guidance drawn from his own thresholds and numbers, not rules he wrote for a scorecard.

---

## 1. Narrative to numbers *(he calls it story to numbers; also "every valuation tells a story" (S065))*

- **What it is**: his core intrinsic valuation. A business story is turned into a handful of inputs, a DCF turns the inputs into value, and value is compared with price. Every number needs a story and every part of the story has to show up in a number.
- **Steps**: (1) Write the story: what the business is, how it makes money, who it sells to. (2) Run it through the 3P test (framework 5). (3) Turn it into four or five inputs: target revenue (market size times share, or a growth path), target pre-tax operating margin, reinvestment through a sales to capital ratio, cost of capital, and a probability of failure. (4) Clear the loose ends (framework 9). (5) Value per share against price. (6) Publish the spreadsheet and invite people to change his inputs, not argue with his conclusion.
- **Use on a stock decision**: every buy, hold or sell starts here. For young firms the story drives the numbers; for mature firms the numbers constrain the story (S080).
- **Scoring guidance**: check each input against industry data. A target margin above the industry's 90th or 95th percentile needs a stated reason (Tesla's 12.5% target was the 95th percentile of automakers, S005; median auto margin 4.81%, S034). Check the imputed return on capital near steady state against the industry and the cost of capital: well above means reinvestment is too low, below the cost of capital means growth destroys value (S100). Revenue growth is checked in dollars, not percentages (S051).
- **Quote**: "a company valuation without a story to bind it together is just numbers on a spreadsheet" (S018)
- **Quote**: "whatever story you tell about Tesla has to show up in five inputs that drive its value" (S063)
- **Sources**: 28 (S001, S005, S007, S010, S015, S016, S018, S026, S029, S034, S040, S043, S051, S059, S061, S062, S063, S065, S069, S070, S072, S077, S080, S082, S084, S091, S096, S100)

## 2. Probabilistic valuation: simulation, scenarios, decision trees *(unnamed as a group)*

- **What it is**: replace point estimates for the few inputs that drive value with distributions, run the model many times, and read the spread of values, not one number.
- **Parts**: (1) base case with expected, not conservative, inputs; (2) pick driver variables, especially where investors disagree most; (3) set distributions from history, the company and the sector; (4) build in correlations (revenue and margin); (5) run it. Tool choice: decision trees for discrete sequential risk (drug approval), scenario analysis for discrete concurrent risk, simulation for continuous risk (S103). Best, base and worst case without probabilities is sloppy scenario analysis (S088, S103).
- **Use on a stock decision**: place the current price on the value distribution. The width of the distribution sets the margin of safety; the skew says whether upside optionality can tip a fairly valued stock into a buy (S012).
- **Scoring guidance**: his Airbnb decision ladder by percentile of the simulated value: below the 20th percentile a bargain, then a solid buy, then fair value, then too richly priced, then over valued (S059). Musk's Twitter bid at about the 75th percentile of his distribution was a full price (S065). Positive skew favors longs, negative skew favors shorts (S038).
- **Quote**: "my suggestion is that you focus on the variables that not only matter the most, but where you feel most uncertain." (S038)
- **Quote**: "you will be wrong 100% of the time, though you may still make money, if you are wrong in the right direction" (S088)
- **Sources**: 20 (S001, S005, S012, S017, S020, S026, S032, S038, S039, S041, S044, S047, S059, S061, S065, S070, S072, S088, S091, S103)

## 3. Value versus price *(named: the value game and the pricing game)*

- **What it is**: value comes from cash flows, growth and risk; price comes from demand and supply, mood, momentum, liquidity and what others pay for similar assets. Investors buy below value and wait for the gap to close; traders buy low and sell higher on price alone. IPOs, VC rounds and banker comps are pricing.
- **Parts**: the value process (DCF on a story) and the pricing process (a scaled metric, a peer group, a story told after the fact, S091). VCs price off the last round or forward pricing off an expected IPO (S023, S095).
- **Use on a stock decision**: first ask which game the user is playing. If the case rests on the last round, a peer multiple, per-user pricing or momentum, it is a pricing case and his value has little to say about it (S006).
- **Scoring guidance**: a thesis built only on relative pricing earns no credit on value versus price until it is tied back to cash flows, growth and risk.
- **Quote**: "You can value an asset, based upon its fundamentals (cash flows, growth and risk) or price it, based upon what others are paying for similar assets, and the two can yield different numbers." (S023)
- **Quote**: "my advice to you is that you ignore my valuation, if you are playing the IPO game." (S006)
- **Sources**: 19 (S006, S008, S011, S016, S021, S023, S026, S036, S041, S044, S046, S048, S072, S076, S080, S082, S090, S091, S095)

## 4. Accounting cleanup: expense classification *(unnamed)*

- **What it is**: restate the financials so operating, capital and financing expenses are classified correctly before computing margins, returns or multiples.
- **Parts**: capitalize R&D (a 2 to 10 year life by sector, 3 years for most technology and software, S068; 10 years for Amgen, S101); treat all leases and debt-like commitments as debt (S007, S068); treat stock-based compensation as an expense and never add it back (S009, S012, S040); reject adjusted EBITDA that adds back recurring costs (S007, S044); treat cash content spending above the expense as capital spending (S031); capitalize brand advertising or training only with evidence of multi-year benefits (S101).
- **Use on a stock decision**: before judging a margin, a return on capital or a PE, rebuild them. It cuts both ways: Facebook's adjusted operating margin rose to 41.7% after the R&D correction (S068); Amgen's return on equity fell from 23.48% to 18.55% (S101).
- **Scoring guidance**: a bull case that leans on adjusted EBITDA or on adding back stock compensation is marked down until restated.
- **Quote**: "Uncertainty about future benefits should never be the litmus test for whether to treat an expense as a capital or operating expense" (S068)
- **Quote**: "adding back any expense that will persist as part of regular operations is bad practice" (S044)
- **Sources**: 14 (S005, S007, S009, S012, S017, S027, S031, S033, S040, S044, S051, S068, S072, S101)

## 5. The 3P test: possible, plausible, probable *(named; he calls it the 3P test)*

- **What it is**: three rising bars a story has to clear before it becomes numbers. Possible: breakeven revenue does not exceed the total market. Plausible: margins, reinvestment and scaling fit the business economics. Probable: the whole story has a reasonable chance (S087).
- **Use on a stock decision**: probable outcomes go into expected cash flows, plausible ones into growth with caution, and possible ones are upside you do not pay for (S012). A story can move up the scale with news (Uber's new businesses, 2015; Tesla robotaxi, 2023).
- **Scoring guidance**: if the current price needs a story that is only possible, value versus price scores low. If it needs only probable outcomes, it scores high. Scenario writers who will not say where their scenario sits on the scale get no weight (S088).
- **Quote**: "Is it possible? Is it plausible? Is it probable?" (S034)
- **Quote**: "treat the possible as icing on the cake (great if it happens, but you are not paying for it up front)" (S012)
- **Sources**: 11 (S012, S015, S034, S044, S051, S062, S069, S077, S087, S088, S096)

## 6. Breakeven revenue: what the price assumes *(reverse DCF; he also calls it imputed revenue)*

- **What it is**: start from today's market cap, hold cost of capital, target margin and sales to capital fixed, and solve for the revenue the company must reach for a buyer to break even.
- **Steps**: (1) take market cap; (2) fix the other inputs at defensible levels; (3) solve for target year revenue; (4) compare it with the total market and with the short list of companies that have ever reached that size (five companies had $400 billion of revenue in January 2023, S071); (5) for a hot sector, add up the breakeven revenue of every player and compare the sum with the market (online ads priced in $523 billion against a market of at most about $466 billion, S013, S049).
- **Use on a stock decision**: answers the second half of his question, what today's price assumes, without needing a full story. He uses it as a constraint on story telling when data is scarce (S094).
- **Scoring guidance**: highly priced is not the same as overpriced; if breakeven revenue and margin pass possible and plausible, the stock can still be a buy at a very large market cap (S087).
- **Quote**: "By reframing a pricing in terms of breakeven revenues, profitability and reinvestment, it allows you to consider whether a stock, even if priced at $4 trillion, may still be a good buy." (S087)
- **Quote**: "it does provide a framework for reasonableness and a constraint on story telling" (S094)
- **Sources**: 11 (S006, S009, S013, S017, S049, S059, S063, S069, S072, S087, S094)

## 7. Corporate life cycle *(named)*

- **What it is**: companies age through start-up, young growth, high growth, maturity, decline and demise. Cash flows go from burn, to build, to return. Tech companies age in dog years.
- **Parts**: place the company by operating metrics, not by self-image (S095). The stage decides which inputs matter, which pricing metric is used (users, revenue, earnings, book value), which CEO fits (visionary, builder, defender, liquidator, S064, S080), how the firm should finance (young firms with equity, mature with debt), and how concentrated an investor can be (S093).
- **Use on a stock decision**: a young firm whose value is in growth assets gets a story-led valuation and a wider spread of values; a mature firm gets a numbers-led one. A mature company acting young (buying growth, denial) or a young one acting mature (borrowing, buybacks) is a red flag (S080).
- **Scoring guidance**: a single-digit grower with stable margins and positive free cash flow is valued as mature whatever it says about itself (S095).
- **Quote**: "Tech companies age in dog years, and the consequences for how we manage, value and invest in them are profound." (S080)
- **Quote**: "Put simply, if a company has revenue growth in single digits, stable margins and positive free cash flows to the firm, it is a mature company, no matter how it views itself." (S095)
- **Sources**: 11 (S009, S037, S056, S064, S067, S076, S080, S083, S087, S093, S095)

## 8. Story break, story change, story shift *(named)*

- **What it is**: a way to classify news. A break kills or explodes the story (value can go near zero). A change adds or removes a dimension with big value effects. A shift keeps the core story and moves its parameters.
- **Steps**: after each earnings report or big news item, ask whether it changes the story; if not, value barely moves. If it does, re-run the inputs it touches, value again and decide whether to act (S012). To test whether bad news breaks a story, list each group the path to profit depends on and ask whether each has more to lose by walking away (S027).
- **Use on a stock decision**: the re-check discipline for a hold. DeepSeek (January 2025) was classed a story change for Nvidia and cut his AI chip market assumption (S084).
- **Scoring guidance**: news that only moves price (an EPS beat, a big name buying) is not a story change and should not move the value score.
- **Quote**: "the question that I ask is whether these specifics affect my narrative for the company and if yes, in what way" (S018)
- **Quote**: "you learn something about a company that renders your story moot and makes your valuation irrelevant" (S027)
- **Sources**: 10 (S010, S012, S015, S017, S018, S027, S069, S077, S084, S087)

## 9. Loose ends: every claim on equity *(unnamed checklist)*

- **What it is**: the bridge from operating asset value to value per share.
- **Parts**: add cash (including IPO proceeds kept by the firm, net of tax on trapped cash, S002, S020); subtract debt including leases; credit net operating losses; value employee options as options and subtract them (crude share-count fixes are sloppy, S101); count restricted stock units, warrants and acquisition shares; discount non-voting shares; do not add future share issuance already funded in the cash flows, since that double counts (S097).
- **Use on a stock decision**: when a banker or a bull quotes a market cap or per-share value, rebuild the share count first. Twitter's IPO count was 472.6 million in the filing and 574.4 million on his count (S007).
- **Quote**: "There is absolutely no valuation basis for excluding these shares" (S007)
- **Quote**: "Adjusting the number of shares for options outstanding is a very sloppy way of dealing with these options, not reflecting the probability of exercise or the price at which they will be exercised." (S101)
- **Sources**: 10 (S001, S005, S007, S020, S026, S040, S044, S097, S100, S101)

## 10. Failure risk outside the discount rate *(his two-step survival adjustment)*

- **What it is**: a DCF values a going concern. The chance the company does not survive (start-up failure, distress, nationalization) goes in a separate probability, with a distress value, not into a higher discount rate.
- **Steps**: (1) value the firm as if it survives; (2) estimate the probability of failure and the value in failure; (3) take the expected value. Use a decision tree when outcomes are discrete (S047).
- **Use on a stock decision**: young, indebted or politically exposed companies. Aramco's regime change risk took his value from about $1.65 trillion to about $1.5 trillion (S047). Anthropic carried a 10% failure chance with half the value lost (S096).
- **Scoring guidance**: his failure rates: start-ups with no product 50 to 60%, young VC-dependent firms 20 to 30%, lower once public with access to capital, zero once profitable (S026). Costs of capital in a DCF stay in a 12 to 15% band at most even for the riskiest start-ups (S047).
- **Quote**: "Discount rates are blunt instruments and are incapable of carrying the burden of truncation risk, and should not be made to do so." (S047)
- **Quote**: "For start-ups with potential and no product, I use 50-60%." (S026)
- **Sources**: 9 (S005, S026, S041, S043, S047, S096, S097, S100, S103)

## 11. Cost of capital as an opportunity cost *(he calls it the Swiss Army Knife of finance)*

- **What it is**: the cost of equity is what investors can earn elsewhere on equivalent risk, not a personal hurdle. It is one of the least critical inputs in a valuation.
- **Checks**: history, the market-implied cost of equity (8.99% at the start of October 2026), and consistency with the PE it implies (a 15% cost of equity with 5% growth and 20% return on equity means a PE of about 7.5) (S097). Quartiles are enough: half of US firms sit between 7.3% and 10.9% (S071); the median US firm was 8.60% in February 2024 (S079); 80% of tech firms between 9.02% and 12.27% (S097). It drifts toward the median as a firm matures.
- **Use on a stock decision**: when a user's valuation hinges on a 12% or 15% required return, or on fine-tuning beta, push the argument back to cash flows.
- **Quote**: "the cost of equity for an investor should reflect what investors can earn elsewhere in the market on an investment of equivalent risk, i.e. it is an opportunity cost" (S097)
- **Quote**: "you should not spend too much time finessing the cost of capital, since of the many assumptions that drive this valuation, it is among the least critical" (S096)
- **Sources**: 8 (S047, S061, S062, S071, S079, S096, S097, S106)

## 12. Good company versus good investment *(unnamed 2x2)*

- **What it is**: the quality of a business (growth, profitability, moats) is a separate question from the quality of the investment (price against value). Investing is finding mismatches between your view and what is priced in.
- **Use on a stock decision**: a user arguing that the company is great has made half the case. The other half is what the market already prices in (S071).
- **Scoring guidance**: great company priced as great scores neutral; an average company priced as bad can score well; a great company priced as perfect scores poorly.
- **Quote**: "investors need to separate what they think about the quality of a company (great, good or awful) from its quality as an investment (cheap or expensive)" (S079)
- **Quote**: "most great companies are neutral or even bad investments, because the market prices them to be great" (S085)
- **Sources**: 7 (S031, S061, S062, S071, S079, S085, S092)

## 13. Implied equity risk premium and market-neutral valuation *(he calls it the price of risk)*

- **What it is**: back out the expected return on the index from its level and expected cash flows (dividends plus buybacks), subtract the riskfree rate. He has re-estimated it at the start of every month since September 2008, and daily in crises (S089).
- **Use on a stock decision**: use the current implied premium so a company valuation is not a hidden call on the market. Use the premium itself, against history, when the question is whether the market is expensive.
- **Scoring guidance**: his readings: 4.72% on 2021-01-01 and a 7.75% peak in March 2020 (S060); 4.01% at the end of September 2025 against a 1960 to 2024 average of 4.25% and a dot-com low near 2% (S086); 4.23% at the start of 2026 (S089). A premium far below history is a bubble symptom (S073).
- **Quote**: "a company valuation should be market-neutral, and the only way you can get there is by using a current implied equity premium." (S089)
- **Quote**: "one symptom of a market bubble is an equity risk premium that becomes so low that it is disconnected from fundamentals" (S073)
- **Sources**: 7 (S054, S060, S073, S086, S089, S098, S104)

## 14. Pricing done right: multiples and comparables *(the four tests)*

- **What it is**: if you price, do it with discipline. Four tests: definitional (same definition, equity over equity and firm over firm), descriptional (know the distribution across the market, use medians), analytical (what fundamentals drive the multiple), application (comparables matched on cash flows, growth and risk, with differences controlled for).
- **Parts**: each multiple has a companion variable (growth for PE, return on equity for price to book, margin for price to sales, S102). The usable metric moves up the income statement as companies get younger (S080, S095). Run rates and per-user prices are not standardized and are easy to game.
- **Use on a stock decision**: when a user says a stock is cheap against peers, check the definitions, the companion variable and whether the whole peer group is overpriced.
- **Scoring guidance**: cheaper than a peer is not cheap. A low PE or EV/EBITDA screen alone is not a value case (S012).
- **Quote**: "Every multiple, whether it is of earnings, revenues or book value, is a function of the same three variables, risk, growth and cash flow generating potential." (S102)
- **Quote**: "Put simply, desperation drives you up the income statement until you find a positive number." (S095)
- **Sources**: 7 (S003, S006, S029, S080, S095, S100, S102)

## 15. Disaggregated valuation: user-based and sum of the parts *(unnamed)*

- **What it is**: value is additive, so value a company by user, subscriber or business unit when the parts have different economics.
- **Parts**: value of existing users plus value added by new users (net of acquisition cost) minus the drag from corporate costs (S028). Stickiness, profit per user and its growth drive user value. Sum of the parts for conglomerates and young multi-business firms (Amazon retail, AWS and Prime, S033; Tesla auto, energy, software, robotaxi, S077; SpaceX launch, Starlink, xAI, S091).
- **Use on a stock decision**: tests per-user pricing claims and finds the real value driver (Netflix content costs, S031; Prime shipping costs, S030). Linked businesses cannot be spun off at their parts values (S077).
- **Quote**: "Value of user-based company = Value of existing users + Value added by new users - Value drag from corporate expenses" (S028)
- **Sources**: 7 (S028, S030, S031, S033, S040, S077, S091)

## 16. Investment philosophy fit and do no harm *(his five steps)*

- **What it is**: (1) form a view of where markets make mistakes and how they correct; (2) choose a philosophy that fits it; (3) check that strategies survive costs and taxes; (4) check personal fit (capital, horizon, temperament, the sleep test); (5) keep the feedback loop open (S090). His own: intrinsic value matters, markets are mostly right but err in uncertainty and change, do no harm.
- **Use on a stock decision**: before judging a trade, ask whether it fits the user's stated philosophy and horizon. If the user does not enjoy markets or has no edge, he points to index funds (S024).
- **Scoring guidance**: his do-no-harm toolkit as a benchmark: about three dozen stocks, no single position above 15%, act rarely, avoid market timing and sector bets (S090).
- **Quote**: "Markets are for the most part right, but make mistakes during periods of uncertainty and change" (S090)
- **Quote**: "the best investment philosophy for you is the one that best fits you as a person" (S057)
- **Sources**: 7 (S024, S025, S048, S057, S080, S085, S090)

## 17. Big market delusion *(named; first called the macro delusion in 2015)*

- **What it is**: a big market, overconfident founders and investors, and a pricing game together overprice a whole group of companies. Each company looks fine alone; the sum of what they are priced to earn exceeds the market.
- **Use on a stock decision**: when the case rests on TAM, sum the implied revenue of all players and ask why this company wins. Applied to social media (2015), online ads (2019), Indian platforms (2021) and LLM companies (2025, 2026).
- **Scoring guidance**: a TAM claim that does not say whether AI is a tool or a replacement, which pay tier, which industries and which geography is treated as suspect (S094).
- **Quote**: "there are three pieces to this puzzle, and it is when they all come together that you see the most egregious manifestations of the delusion" (S049)
- **Quote**: "In short, each company passed the possible and plausible test, but in the aggregate, you are chasing an impossible target." (S087)
- **Sources**: 6 (S009, S013, S049, S061, S087, S094)

## 18. Flexible margin of safety *(his version)*

- **What it is**: a margin of safety is an add-on to a risk-adjusted value, not a substitute, and it has costs: a bigger margin cuts errors of buying expensive stocks but raises errors of passing on bargains.
- **Parts**: scale it to valuation uncertainty (larger for young firms and crises), portfolio concentration (much larger for four or five stocks), market liquidity (smaller in liquid markets), nearness of a catalyst, and skew (smaller when the upside tail is long, S044). Use the same margin on the way out.
- **Scoring guidance**: his worked example is 15% both ways (S019). 15% may be too large for Apple's tight distribution and too small for Amazon's wide one (S038). Holding more cash than your risk aversion would explain is a sign the margin is too high (S019).
- **Quote**: "The more uncertain you are about your estimated value for an asset, other things remaining equal, the larger the MOS should be." (S019)
- **Quote**: "There can be no one margin of safety for all companies" (S038)
- **Sources**: 6 (S003, S012, S019, S038, S044, S097)

## 19. Real options, only with exclusivity *(unnamed tests)*

- **What it is**: an option to expand, delay or abandon adds value beyond the DCF only when the first investment is a prerequisite for the next, the firm has exclusive rights or a strong advantage, and the advantage lasts. The value comes from excess returns, not revenue.
- **Use on a stock decision**: a small right-skewed option tilt can turn a fair value into a buy (S012); a beaten-down stock with proprietary assets can be bought as a long-odds option, sized small (S004). Platform optionality counts only with intensity and proprietary data (S061).
- **Scoring guidance**: an option premium on top of a DCF that already includes the growth is double counting; anyone using options to justify a premium must value them (S107).
- **Quote**: "Not all investments have options embedded in them, and not all options, even if they do exist, have value." (S107)
- **Sources**: 6 (S004, S012, S032, S061, S100, S107)

## 20. Country risk by exposure *(lambda approach)*

- **What it is**: equity risk premium equals a mature market premium plus a country premium, estimated from sovereign default spreads scaled by relative equity volatility. A company's exposure comes from where it earns revenue and operates, not where it is listed.
- **Parts**: bludgeon (every firm gets the full premium), beta-scaled, or lambda (scaled by actual exposure); he prefers lambda (S104). Democracies add going concern risk to the discount rate; autocracies add regime change risk handled after the DCF (S047). The riskfree rate is a default-free rate in the currency of the cash flows (S106).
- **Use on a stock decision**: emerging market stocks and multinationals. Do not add a premium for a big market in India or China; the danger there is overvaluing (S061).
- **Quote**: "The most general, and our preferred approach, is to allow for each company to have an exposure to country risk that is different from its exposure to all other market risk." (S104)
- **Sources**: 6 (S046, S047, S089, S098, S104, S106)

## 21. The one number: key value driver *(he calls it the one number or driver variable)*

- **What it is**: in every company one input moves value most and is uncertain. Find it by changing each input in turn, or by asking where investors disagree most.
- **Use on a stock decision**: focus research, questions to management and disclosure tracking on that input. Netflix: content cost growth (S031). Facebook in 2018: operating margin (S032). Anthropic in 2026: target revenue, with errors on revenue pulling the other inputs the same way (S097).
- **Quote**: "every company has a value driver, one number that more than any of the others determines value" (S031)
- **Quote**: "If an input has large effects on value, but you feel confident about it, it is not a driver variable." (S032)
- **Sources**: 5 (S020, S031, S032, S061, S097)

## 22. Conviction and position sizing *(three beliefs behind conviction)*

- **What it is**: conviction needs three beliefs: your value is more right than the market's, the market will correct, and it will correct within your holding period. Conviction is a continuum and should set position size and leverage.
- **Use on a stock decision**: size by how many of the three beliefs hold. Pricing is noisier and catalysts rarer for young firms, so spread bets across many young-company ideas and tolerate more concentration in mature ones. Leverage can force a sale before the correction (S093).
- **Scoring guidance**: he would not put a whole stake in one uncertain stock even below value (S097); his cap is 15% of a portfolio of about three dozen stocks (S090).
- **Quote**: "conviction is a continuum, rather than an absolute" (S093)
- **Quote**: "using debt to turbocharge your returns can sometimes shrink your time horizon by forcing you to liquidate your mispriced investments before the market corrects its mistakes" (S093)
- **Sources**: 5 (S018, S019, S090, S093, S097)

## 23. Value, price and gap effects of a trigger *(catalysts)*

- **What it is**: any event (a round-number market cap, a big name buying, an earnings report, a management change) can change value (cash flows, growth, risk), change price (mood, momentum, liquidity), or draw attention that closes the gap between them.
- **Use on a stock decision**: an insider or activist can change value; a famous trader only moves price (S021). Value decides whether to buy or sell; triggers can inform when. A short needs a catalyst more than a long, because the short seller does not control the horizon (S038).
- **Quote**: "even if you are right about your value judgment, you will make money only if the gap between the two closes" (S038)
- **Quote**: "I believe that one of the keys to being a good value investor is finding catalysts that can cause the price correction." (S036)
- **Sources**: 4 (S021, S036, S038, S093)

## 24. Four strands of contrarian investing *(named strands, 2025; four variants, 2012)*

- **What it is**: 2012 version (S004): biggest losers, collateral damage, comeback bet, long odds option, with three questions for a comeback: one time or continuing, how fixable, is the fall disproportionate. 2025 version, newer (S085): knee-jerk (buy anything down), technical (buy only when indicators show a mood shift), constrained (losers that pass quality and safety screens), opportunistic (use a markdown to buy great companies you always wanted).
- **Use on a stock decision**: for a buy-the-dip request, name the strand. His preferred path is opportunistic: keep a list of great companies valued in calm times, and after a crisis revalue them with new cash flows and risk premiums before buying.
- **Scoring guidance**: a stock down a lot is not cheap until the revalued value exceeds the price; knee-jerk contrarianism scores no better than knee-jerk momentum (S079).
- **Quote**: "you use a price markdown as an opportunity to buy companies that you have always wanted to hold, but had not been able to buy because they were priced too high" (S085)
- **Sources**: 4 (S004, S058, S079, S085)

## 25. The IT proposition: intangibles and key people *(named)*

- **What it is**: anything, tangible or not, affects value only through cash flows or risk. Brand shows up in margins, good management in growth, margins and reinvestment, government ties in failure risk. Key person value is the value with the person minus the value without.
- **Use on a stock decision**: when a user credits a brand, a moat or a founder, ask which input it moves and by how much; toggle each on and off to size it (S076). Key person value is highest in small, young, micro-driven firms with transitory moats (S078).
- **Scoring guidance**: a claimed brand with margins below the industry average is not a valuable brand (S076).
- **Quote**: "if it does not affect cash flow or risk, it cannot affect value" (S078)
- **Quote**: "Value of key person(s) = Value of business with key person - Value of business without key person" (S078)
- **Sources**: 4 (S039, S076, S078, S101)
