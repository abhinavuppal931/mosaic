# Principles: Aswath Damodaran

Built from the verified notes on 100 sources (91 blog posts, 2012 to 2026, and 9 NYU Stern papers, 2005 to 2012). Decision rules, most-supported first. Confidence: high = 3 or more sources, medium = 2, low = 1. Where his view moved, both dates are shown and the newer view is marked.

---

1. **Price decides; there is no company he would always buy or never buy.** A money-losing, young or badly governed company can be a buy at the right price, and the best company can be a bad buy at the wrong one.
   - "at the right price, I would buy almost any company, even a risky one with bad corporate governance, and at the wrong price, I would not buy even the very best company." (S012)
   - "At the right price, a money-losing company can be a good investment and at the wrong price, a company with solid and stable profits can be a bad investment." (S092)
   - Sources: 9 (S008, S012, S044, S046, S059, S061, S067, S085, S092). Confidence: high.

2. **Value the company even when the future is very uncertain; uncertainty makes the estimate noisier, not impossible.** Refusing to estimate does not remove the uncertainty.
   - "the fact that you face uncertainty or that you are missing information does not imply that you cannot make estimates, just that the estimates will be noisier" (S091)
   - "Uncertainty is a feature of investing, no matter what you invest in, with the differences across investments lying in how much uncertainty you face, rather than whether you face." (S097)
   - Sources: 9 (S001, S018, S020, S072, S091, S097, S100, S103, S107). Confidence: high.

3. **Stock-based compensation and other recurring costs are expenses; never add them back.** Rebuild margins with R&D capitalized and leases as debt before judging profitability.
   - "Stop adding back stock-based compensation to come up with adjusted EBITDA and acting like acquisitions made with stock really cost you nothing." (S009)
   - "adding back any expense that will persist as part of regular operations is bad practice" (S044)
   - Sources: 9 (S007, S009, S012, S017, S027, S040, S044, S068, S101). Confidence: high.

4. **Own the decision; do not buy or sell because a famous investor did, and do not evangelize your own call.**
   - "buying or selling a company because someone else is doing so, even if that person has legendary investment credentials, is a dereliction of investment due diligence" (S062)
   - "my valuations drive my decisions to buy or sell stocks, they should not determine your choices" (S082)
   - Sources: 9 (S021, S024, S039, S048, S062, S077, S082, S087, S096). Confidence: high.

5. **If you value a company, act on it: buy below value, sell above it, and wait for the gap to close.**
   - "I have always operated on the premise that if you value companies, you should be willing to act on those valuations." (S037)
   - "Investing requires assessing the value of an asset, buying if the price is lower than that value and selling if it is higher, and waiting for the gap to close." (S090)
   - Sources: 7 (S010, S012, S037, S051, S072, S082, S090). Confidence: high.

6. **Change the story when the facts change; stubbornness is the bigger sin than being wrong.**
   - "an unwillingness to change your valuation stories and inputs, especially in a company that delivers as many twists and turns as Tesla, is a far greater sin" (S063)
   - "you have to be willing to revisit your story, admit that you were wrong, and adapt" (S087)
   - Sources: 7 (S010, S015, S063, S077, S084, S087, S091). Confidence: high.

7. **A big market is not a reason to pay up; ask why this company wins and who loses.**
   - "It is therefore dangerous to base your argument for investing in a company and assigning it high value entirely on the size of the market that it serves" (S013)
   - "Not all change is revolutionary, not all revolutionary change translates into big markets, and not all big markets create valuable businesses" (S094)
   - Sources: 6 (S009, S013, S049, S061, S087, S094). Confidence: high.

8. **Spread bets in proportion to uncertainty, with a hard cap on any one position.** His own rule: about three dozen stocks, no position above 15%.
   - "the more uncertain you feel about individual investments, the more you have to spread your bets" (S018)
   - "not only spread my portfolio over three dozen stocks, but also follow rigid rules on not letting any single investment exceed 15% of my portfolio" (S090)
   - Sources: 6 (S004, S018, S019, S090, S093, S097). Confidence: high.

9. **Do not short, or chase a fall, on valuation alone; in the short term momentum beats fundamentals.**
   - "at least in the short term, momentum beats fundamentals, and I have no desire to be caught in the whiplash effect" (S063)
   - "Momentum is a wild card in every investment strategy, and you ignore it at your own peril." (S093)
   - Sources: 6 (S038, S059, S063, S091, S093, S096). Confidence: high.

10. **Never borrow against a story; a young or money-losing firm should fund with equity, and an investor should not lever a macro thesis.**
    - "Borrowing money in anticipation of future profits is not just stupid, but it is dangerous." (S034)
    - "combining a macro story about AI winning with maximal leverage creates a time bomb" (S093)
    - Sources: 6 (S029, S034, S041, S080, S093, S094). Confidence: high.

11. **Sell when price passes value, whatever the holding period; later he sells winners in halves.** In 2015 the rule was to sell once price exceeds value, even after a short hold. In 2023 and 2024 (newer) he sold Nvidia in halves when price ran past value, because his value has error, taxes bite and regret cuts both ways. Doubling down is normally off limits; he made one stated exception (Facebook, November 2022).
    - "you should sell the stock when the price exceeds value (perhaps, using the same margin of safety in the other direction), even if you have held it for only a short period." (S012)
    - "I plan to repeat that process, and sell half of my remaining holdings" (S079)
    - "I made an exception to my rule of not doubling down and doubled my holding of Facebook on November 4, 2022, because its valuation looks compelling" (S069)
    - Sources: 6 (S012, S051, S069, S072, S079, S082). Confidence: high.

12. **Know what you own when founders control the vote; his tolerance for it has narrowed.** In 2012 dual-class control was a reason not to buy Facebook even at a fair price. In 2015 and 2022 he said he would buy at the right price and build distrust in as a discount. From 2021 to 2026 (newer) he worries more than ever about founder voting power and calls concentrated founder power a bad idea.
    - "You may be comfortable with Zuckerberg as CEO for life but I am not." (S001)
    - "you have to build that reality into your valuations, leading to discounts to your value that reflect how much you trust management" (S067)
    - "I would worry more than ever before about giving up voting power to founder/CEOs, even if they are well regarded" (S064)
    - "concentrating power in the hands of founders is a bad idea" (S097)
    - Sources: 6 (S001, S046, S064, S067, S096, S097). Confidence: high.

13. **Keep the chance of failure out of the discount rate; put it in a failure probability.**
    - "you should see 12-15% as costs of capital for even the riskiest start-ups, in a DCF, never 40-50%" (S047)
    - "If your concerns about default are higher, teh failure risk is the number you should be moving, not the cost of capital." (S097)
    - Sources: 5 (S043, S047, S096, S097, S100). Confidence: high.

14. **Spend your effort on cash flow inputs, not on finessing the cost of capital or beta.**
    - "Raising or lowering the cost of capital has an effect on value, but changing my assumptions about risk premiums, betas or debt ratios has a much smaller effect that changing assumptions that alter cash flows." (S061)
    - "contending that the cost of equity for a median US company should be 12% or 15% borders on the delusional" (S097)
    - Sources: 5 (S061, S062, S071, S096, S097). Confidence: high.

15. **Losing money, or being young, is not a reason to reject a stock.**
    - "one of the emptiest, and laziest, is that it is losing money right now" (S061)
    - "I do not have a deep rooted aversion to buying young or money losing companies, if the price is right." (S008)
    - Sources: 5 (S008, S015, S059, S061, S092). Confidence: high.

16. **Set a limit order at your value and move on if it does not fill.**
    - "I have a limit buy at my estimated value of $180, with the acceptance that it may never hit that price in this iteration" (S077)
    - "If it does not drop that low, there are other fish to catch and I will move on." (S039)
    - Sources: 5 (S032, S039, S044, S076, S077). Confidence: high.

17. **Distrust certainty, your own first; humility beats being smart.**
    - "when I believe that I own the high ground in any investment debate, it is a sure sign that I have let hubris get the better of me" (S025)
    - "Humble money beats smart money" (S093)
    - Sources: 5 (S025, S045, S055, S090, S093). Confidence: high.

18. **Aim to be less wrong than everyone else, not right, and be open about being wrong.**
    - "I don't have to be right, just less wrong than everyone else" (S018)
    - "I would rather be transparently wrong than opaquely right" (S062)
    - Sources: 4 (S018, S023, S062, S097). Confidence: high.

19. **A margin of safety must scale with uncertainty and concentration, and must be used on the sell side too.**
    - "There can be no one margin of safety for all companies" (S038)
    - "if MOS is good enough to use when you buy a stock, it should be good enough to use when you sell that stock." (S019)
    - Sources: 4 (S019, S038, S044, S097). Confidence: high.

20. **Do not time the market; use market-level data to reset return expectations, not to trade.**
    - "I steer away from active market timing and sector bets for a simple reason. I am not good at either" (S090)
    - "there is not a single market timing combination (threshold and action) that would have added to annual returns over the last fifty years" (S086)
    - Sources: 4 (S022, S086, S090, S106). Confidence: high.

21. **Keep company valuations market-neutral: use the current implied equity risk premium and keep interest rate views out.**
    - "Using an equity risk premium that is different from the implied equity risk premium makes every valuation a joint judgment on what you think about the company and what you think about the market." (S089)
    - "Rule 3: If you have strong views on interest rates, try to keep them out of the valuation of individual companies." (S106)
    - Sources: 4 (S073, S089, S098, S106). Confidence: high. Note: his 2012 paper also recommends a long-term geometric average premium for valuation and corporate finance (S098); the 2023 and 2026 posts make the current implied premium the rule for any company valuation (S073, S089, newer).

22. **Read earnings and news for changes to the story, not for the EPS beat.**
    - "the least meaningful part of any earnings report" (S070)
    - "the most interesting components of the report are not in the proverbial bottom line" (S082)
    - Sources: 4 (S012, S018, S070, S082). Confidence: high.

23. **After a crash, revalue before you buy; a big fall is a reason to look, not to buy.**
    - "you cannot compare the post-correction price to your pre-crisis valuation of your company" (S085)
    - "Is the market decline disproportionately large?" (S004)
    - Sources: 4 (S004, S039, S079, S085). Confidence: high.

24. **Cheaper than a peer is not cheap; check definitions and what the whole peer group is priced for.**
    - "by comparing the two, you are just finding the less overpriced entity" (S095)
    - "The first step when discussing a valuation based upon a multiple is to ensure that everyone in the discussion is using the same definition for that multiple." (S102)
    - Sources: 4 (S012, S029, S095, S102). Confidence: high.

25. **Push every story for specifics, and do not tweak inputs until the value says what you want.** He picks inputs before opening the spreadsheet to keep price out of value, while admitting that is never fully possible. In 2015 he said he tries not to let price feed into his value; by 2021 (newer) he accepts that value cannot be built in a vacuum.
    - "It is your obligation as an investor to push for specificity" (S069)
    - "I have tried (though not always succeeded) in not letting the price feedback into my value." (S015)
    - "it is almost impossible to value companies in a vacuum, where what the market is doing can be ignored" (S063)
    - Sources: 4 (S015, S063, S069, S085). Confidence: high.

26. **A stock's past run says nothing about whether it is a good investment today; avoid knee-jerk contrarianism.**
    - "a company's past market history, no matter how glorious, has little or no effect on whether it is a good investment today" (S079)
    - "whether it is a good investment today." (S005)
    - Sources: 3 (S005, S079, S085). Confidence: high.

27. **Value decides whether to buy or sell; triggers and catalysts can only decide when.**
    - "while my primary decision of whether to buy or sell a stock is driven by value judgments, the question of when to do it can be affected by market triggers." (S036)
    - Sources: 3 (S021, S036, S038). Confidence: high.

28. **A claimed intangible must show up in the numbers; a brand with below-average margins is not a brand.**
    - "a company that claims that it has a valuable brand name, while delivering operating margins well below the industry average, really does not" (S076)
    - Sources: 3 (S076, S078, S101). Confidence: high.

29. **Buy back stock only when it is cheap; young growth firms should not buy back or borrow at all.**
    - "A company, whose stock is over valued, should pay a special dividend since buying back shares at the inflated price hurts the stockholders who remain after the buyback." (S002)
    - "a young firm that derives much of its value from future growth should not put that at risk by borrowing money or by buying back stock" (S080)
    - Sources: 2 (S002, S080). Confidence: medium.

30. **Treat dividends as expected, not promised; a yield that looks too good usually is.**
    - "these dividend-based strategies become delusional when they treat dividends as obligated payments, rather than expected ones." (S039)
    - Sources: 1 (S039). Confidence: low.
