# Frameworks: Dev Kantesaria

Built from 8 sources (S001, S006, S007, S008, S018, S019, S020, S021; July 2019 to December 2024) through the verified batch notes. Quick depth, so overlapping tools are merged. Most-used first. Scoring lines are guidance drawn from his own thresholds, not his words.

## 1. Compounding machines
Sources: 7 (S006, S007, S008, S018, S019, S020, S021).
What it is: his name for the only kind of business he buys. A monopoly or oligopoly with secular volume growth, pricing power above inflation, operating leverage still ahead, little capital or R&D need, and management that returns free cash flow instead of buying growth.
Parts: (1) entrenched, essential product, first or second in its industry (S008); (2) positive industry volume (S006, S007); (3) pricing power (S007, S018); (4) operating leverage, margins still rising even at large size (S007, S020); (5) capital light, ideally zero reinvestment (S018, S020); (6) predictable earnings not driven by commodities or rates (S008); (7) disciplined capital allocation (S008, S018).
Quotes: "And that is to buy really high quality businesses, what we call compounding machines." (S019) "Such businesses should deliver strong organic growth over a multi-year period, employ pricing power, operate in an industry with positive volume trends and have flexible cost structures to maintain margins during down periods," (S006) FICO is the template: "If you had to design an ideal business from scratch, FICO has most of the elements that you would be looking for." (S020)
When to use: the first gate on any stock. If the business is not one of these, the seat stops and says it is outside the pond.
Scoring (guidance): 10 = all seven parts present with decades of evidence (rating agencies, FICO, card networks as he describes them). 5 = good business missing one leg, such as volume growth without pricing power. 0 = cyclical, commodity-linked, capital intensive or R&D dependent.

## 2. Quality is the intersection of growth and predictability
Sources: 4 (S007, S018, S019, S021).
What it is: quality is not a margin or a moat label. It is a business that grows fast and predictably at the same time. Fast but unpredictable (young software) fails; predictable but slow (2% to 3% staples) fails (S018).
Quotes: "We define quality as finding the perfect intersection between growth and predictability." (S018) "We're happy to give up some growth in exchange for predictability" (S018) "if I'm not comfortable with the industry dynamic in the company remaining in a dominant position for at least the next 10 years, it doesn't meet our bar for predictability." (S018)
Test: if the next earnings release worries you, it fails. "if you're worried about what the next earnings release is going to be because of lack of predictability, then that's probably not a company that you should be owning." (S007)
Target: a weighted portfolio that compounds intrinsic value in the high teens to low 20s over a decade (S018).
Scoring (guidance): 10 = confident in dominance for 10 to 20 years and mid-teens free cash flow growth. 5 = predictable but growing low single digits, or fast but with a real chance of a 90% fall. 0 = cannot say who wins in 10 years.

## 3. The fishing pond and the funnel
Sources: 4 (S007, S008, S018, S021).
What it is: about 40 to 50 businesses worldwide clear the quality bar. A few hundred are tracked, about 100 modeled, 15 to 20 sit on a shortlist, and 8 to 12 are owned (S008). Mid-teens is the ceiling (S007). Only 5 to 7 of 50 to 60 shortlist names are foreign (S018).
Quotes: "our fishing pond for business quality is only 50 or so companies in the entire world." (S007) "By the time we get out to our 15th-best idea, we're not really happy with the risk/reward proposition." (S008)
When to use: on any new name, ask whether it belongs in a pond this small. On a portfolio question, ask whether a 13th or 17th idea dilutes the best ones.
Scoring (guidance): 10 = would be a top-10 name in a 50-name pond. 5 = in the pond but below the current 12. 0 = not in the pond.

## 4. Deserved free cash flow yield against the 10-year Treasury
Sources: 5 (S006, S007, S008, S019, S021).
What it is: a DCF in spirit. Rank hundreds of businesses by quality to decide what free cash flow yield each deserves, with the 10-year Treasury as the risk-free anchor. Compare the yield it trades at (this year and next) with the yield it deserves, and buy only at a large gap. Free cash flow is measured after the cost of stock compensation. This is not a high-yield screen: in 2019 he bought at roughly 3.5% to 4.2% yields on 2021 estimates with a 1.7% risk-free rate, justified by mid-teens growth (S008).
Quotes: "it is a rank ordering process." (S007) "We want to buy businesses at at least 30% discounts to our expected free-cash-flow yields." (S008) "we'll buy them up front at a significant discount, generally 30 to 60 percent below what we think they're worth." (S019) "We actually calculate our free cash flow yields for our company after the cost of buying back stock that is issued in terms of compensation." (S019)
Limits he admits: "intrinsic values are not precise numbers." (S019) "equities trade in a range of reasonableness." (S021)
Scoring (guidance): this sets entry, not quality. 10 = 30% to 60% below his value on SBC-adjusted free cash flow. 5 = around fair value, hold but do not add. 0 = at or above intrinsic value, which forces a justify-or-sell review (see selling.md).

## 5. Pricing power due diligence
Sources: 6 (S006, S007, S008, S018, S020, S021).
What it is: the hallmark test. The product is essential, cheap relative to the value or decision it supports, and has been priced above inflation for a long time without hurting volume. Companies hide pricing power, so he looks for long patterns, not one-year jumps.
Quotes: "Pricing power is the hallmark of a great business." (S007) "we're looking for patterns that have played out over a long period of time" (S007) "because the score is so cheap, relative to the loan decisions being made, there is just a tremendous runway for pricing power" (S020) "So if inflation goes to 10%, you want a company that can raise its prices 13%." (S018)
Warning: price hikes used to hide falling volume backfire. "It can be overused." (S018)
Scoring (guidance): 10 = price is a tiny share of the customer's decision (a score under $1 on a $400,000 mortgage, S020) with decades of above-inflation increases. 5 = pricing power that competition is starting to cap (Monster in 2019, S008). 0 = price taker.

## 6. Natural monopoly or duopoly test
Sources: 5 (S008, S018, S019, S020, S021).
What it is: the moat must be natural: customers choose it because the alternative costs them more, not because they are forced. The rating agency proof: an issuer without a Moody's or S&P rating pays 30 to 50 basis points more, which dwarfs the fee saved (S008, S020, S021). Structure alone is not enough: Broadridge, Boeing and Airbus, UPS and FedEx, Nike and Adidas show a duopoly can still be a mediocre compounder (S018).
Quotes: "And what I mean by natural is that you don't need to twist someone's arm in order to use their products or services." (S019) "Not having a rating from S&P or Moody's brands the offering as inferior" (S008) "There's just no easy way to just have rules around what is investable and what is not." (S018)
Scoring (guidance): 10 = switching away visibly costs the customer more than the product (rating spread, securitization pricing). 5 = duopoly that competes on price. 0 = moat held up by contracts, regulation forcing use, or scale alone.

## 7. The toll collector
Sources: 3 (S008, S018, S021).
What it is: his picture of the ideal compounder. The business takes a small fee on every unit of traffic through an essential road, and the road gets busier. Volume dips are lunch breaks, not threats.
Quotes: "Moody's is the toll collector on an increasingly busy global road." (S008) "sometimes the toll collectors go out to lunch and the cars are just backing up on the highway and we don't care" (S018) On AI risk to ratings: "They're toll collectors in these industries" (S021)
Scoring (guidance): 10 = the fee rides on a growing volume stream that cannot route around it. 0 = the traffic can choose another road cheaply.

## 8. Buy the dislocation: scare or market neglect
Sources: 6 (S006, S007, S008, S018, S019, S021).
What it is: great businesses go on sale only a handful of times a year. About one third of entries come from a sharp drop (40% to 50%) on a regulatory, legal, competitive or earnings scare the market misreads; two thirds come from plain market neglect (S018, S021). He follows a name for years with intrinsic value updated every quarter, so he can act in days.
Quotes: "There are a handful of situations that can arise in a given year where a great business goes on sale." (S008) "Two-thirds of the time, surprisingly, the opportunities we find are what I call market neglect." (S018) "we followed companies for seven years, 10 years before we bought Fair Isaac" (S007) "Great businesses don't go on sale very often." (S019)
When to use: the second half of the seat's question. Ask what the scare is, whether it changes 10-year economics, and whether the work is already done.
Scoring (guidance): 10 = the scare is real news but does not touch the toll (Moody's 2009, Visa and Mastercard after Durbin, Monster FDA reports). 5 = no scare, but neglect leaves a 30% gap. 0 = the scare is the thesis breaking.

## 9. Capital allocation veto
Sources: 6 (S007, S008, S018, S019, S020, S021).
What it is: one bad parameter can make a great business uninvestable. He penalizes defensive acquisitions, serial acquirers, pet projects, dual-class control, heavy stock comp and buybacks that only offset issuance. Best uses of cash: buybacks, then dividends, ideally no cash hoard.
Quotes: "there could be one parameter that is negative that makes the company uninvestable." (S007) "Capital allocation, in general, is usually one of the highest hurdles for us to clear before making an investment." (S008) "capital allocation is absolutely something that can ruin a great business." (S018) "When you're giving out a large amount of restricted stock and then claiming that you have a large buyback, you're essentially running in place." (S019)
Scoring (guidance): 10 = no acquisitions needed, share count falling (FICO retired about 20% in five years, S020). 5 = occasional bolt-ons, buybacks partly offset by comp. 0 = growth plan built on buying five companies a year.

## 10. Exclusion list: what is never in the pond
Sources: 4 (S007, S008, S018, S019).
What it is: his circle drawn by what he refuses. High R&D risk (biotech, pharma, devices, hardware), big upfront bets with unknown payoff, commodity-linked, rate-linked, capital intensive, scale-only moats, most foreign and emerging market names.
Quotes: "we avoid things that have high R&D risk." (S019) "they are not predictable enough for our standards" (S007) "TSMC's advantage today comes from really scale. And that is not a advantage that we prefer." (S021)
Use: anything on this list is TOO HARD for the seat, however cheap.

## 11. The edge is the discount rate
Sources: 4 (S001, S007, S019, S021).
What it is: everyone has the same information; his edge is judging future risk over 10 years or more and folding it into one number. From venture capital he learned to collapse regulatory, patent and management risk into one price. He rejects volatility as risk.
Quotes: "I had to distill all of that into a single pre money valuation" (S001) "I think the real edge comes from determining the discount rate, and you're factoring in a large set of future risk factors." (S019) "We have to assume that everyone has the same information that we do. But we need to interpret that better than others." (S021)

## 12. Win-loss ratio as the margin of safety
Sources: 4 (S001, S007, S008, S019).
What it is: concentration only works if almost every idea works. He wants more than 90% winners against about 60% for a typical manager. His record to 2019: 43 positions with gains or losses above $10,000, six losers (S008).
Quotes: "We want 90% plus of our ideas to work out." (S007) "If you invest in companies that could go down 90% in value, you can't run a concentrated portfolio with that lack of predictability." (S007)
Scoring (guidance): ask whether this idea is one he would expect to win nine times in ten. If not, it does not belong in an 8 to 12 name book.

## 13. The most predictable way to own a theme *(unnamed)*
Sources: 1 (S021).
What it is: when a theme is real but the winners are unclear, own the monopoly supplier rather than the contenders. For AI he chose ASML, 10 to 15 years ahead, over Nvidia, Microsoft, Google or TSMC.
Quote: "we wanted the most predictable way to participate in AI." (S021)
