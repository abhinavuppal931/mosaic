# Stories: Dev Kantesaria

Built from 8 sources (S001, S006, S007, S008, S018, S019, S020, S021; 2019 to 2024) through the verified batch notes. Cases he returns to, most-used first, with repeated tellings merged. Figures are as he reported them at the time. Losers and omissions get equal room.

## 1. Moody's and S&P Global, 2009: buying the toll collectors when nobody would
Sources: 6 (S006, S008, S018, S019, S021, S007).
Bought in the 2008 to 2009 crisis amid bad publicity over mortgage ratings, regulatory threats from Congress and the SEC, and litigation; Moody's traded at a single-digit P/E, about Ford's multiple (S008). Moody's at $16.50 and S&P Global at $17.50 in early 2009 (S021), the S&P purchase made on his honeymoon (S018). Regulation later hardened the duopoly; by 2021 they were up 20 to 25 times from the lows and still his longest-held names (S019). He calls 2008 to 2009 the luckiest event of his career (S007).
"That sort of market disconnect doesn't happen too often." (S008) "The risks that others saw in those businesses we thought certainly were overblown, misunderstood." (S021)
Lesson: a scare that hits headlines but not the toll is the entry point; then hold for decades.

## 2. FICO: market neglect and a price change nobody priced
Sources: 5 (S007, S008, S018, S020, S021).
Followed for seven to ten years (S007). He worried about its organic growth until 2018, when it began special price increases and restructured bureau contracts for annual increases; few investors noticed (S008, S020). Score revenue is tiny against the loan decision, scores margins sit above 85%, and the share count fell about 20% in five years (S020). Later it raised the mortgage score price 100% in one year (S021). When Synchrony moved to VantageScore in 2021 he judged it saved $2 to 3 million while risking securitization pricing, and few followed (S020). The FHFA ruling hurt the bureaus more than FICO (S020).
"We were worried about its organic growth profile." (S020) "In my investment career, I've never seen a company raise the price of its product or service by 100% in a single year." (S021)
Lesson: two thirds of entries are neglect, not crashes; the catalyst can be a pricing decision the market ignores. When the stock doubled two years running he held: "I would much rather be the turtle in the race, so I'll take it when I can get it." (S018)

## 3. Visa and Mastercard after the Durbin Amendment
Sources: 3 (S007, S008, S021).
The debit pricing cap sank both stocks, and that was his entry (S007). By 2019 he saw their role as more solid than a few years earlier and dismissed crypto as a threat (S008). In 2024 he conceded AI-driven unemployment would hurt them: "a weaker consumer certainly is a net negative for those businesses." (S021)
Lesson: regulation that dents economics but leaves the network intact is a buying chance. "it doesn't ultimately matter if you own a very high-quality business." (S007)

## 4. Monster Beverage: bought on a scare, sold when pricing power faded
Sources: 3 (S008, S018, S019).
FDA reports linking deaths to energy drinks dropped the stock about 35% to 40% in days. With the work done and over 10 billion cans sold over decades, he built about a 10% position in a few days; the gap closed within weeks (S008, S019). He sold in early 2019 when competition cut future pricing power (S008); it kept rising after (S019).
"We still consider Monster an excellent business, but increased competition in the energy-drink space has reduced the company's future pricing power and margin potential." (S008)
Lesson: the same pricing power test governs entry and exit.

## 5. Equifax to FICO: selling for a better toll
Sources: 1 (S008).
Bought after the 2017 breach, judging the legal costs manageable. The bureau work led him to FICO, and he sold Equifax about a year before late 2019. "our reason for selling was that we found a better opportunity." (S008)
Lesson: research on one toll road often finds a better toll upstream.

## 6. The big tech omission
Sources: 4 (S008, S018, S019, S021).
He knew Google Search, the iPhone and Windows and Office were great models but stayed out over dual-class control, pet projects and poor capital allocation; he owned only a little Amazon (S021). In 2021 he called it an error (S019); in 2024, "For me, a big omission was the big tech companies." (S018) "But these business models were so strong that they overcame some of the sins that kept me from these businesses." (S018)
Lesson: purism on capital allocation can cost more than it saves; he still applies it (see positions.md, topic 7).

## 7. American Dairy, 2010: the biggest loss
Sources: 2 (S008, S019).
A Chinese infant formula maker; less than a 5% hit to the fund (S008), about a 4% drag (S019). He entered before the winners were clear, misjudged mothers switching to foreign brands, and underestimated execution. "First, we entered the battle too early." (S008)
Lesson: a fragmented market with no clear winner is not predictable, and foreign names carry risks hard to price.

## 8. COVID, March 2020: no sales, one short
Sources: 2 (S007, S019).
No sales and no new names in 2020: "We didn't sell anything. We didn't buy a new name." (S019) A short on medical knowledge from doctor friends added 6% of a 27% year. His holdings posted record margins during the worst GDP drop since the Depression (S019).
Lesson: the portfolio was stress-tested and the right move was to do nothing.

## 9. Valeant and the serial acquirer
Sources: 2 (S007, S018).
Cited as the roll-up that ended in failure, to show why growth by acquisition is uninvestable (S007, S018, paraphrase).
Lesson: "there could be one parameter that is negative that makes the company uninvestable." (S007)

## 10. Intuit: bought after capital allocation improved
Sources: 1 (S008).
Two market-leading franchises moving to subscription, at a 4.2% free cash flow yield on 2021 estimates with mid-teens growth expected. He would not have bought before management stopped failed acquisitions (S008, paraphrase).

## 11. Coca-Cola and Microsoft: obvious in hindsight
Sources: 2 (S018, S019).
Coca-Cola's 1928 report showed a compounder everyone ignored (S018); its stock fell 40% after listing then compounded for 50 years, so entry timing barely mattered (S019). Passing on Microsoft at 35 times earnings for a washing machine maker at 12 is his example of a tragic mistake (S018).

## 12. Leaving venture capital
Sources: 2 (S001, S021).
Eighteen years in venture taught him to price many risks as one number, but he wanted near-certain winners, not lottery tickets. "I wanted my batting average to be nine out of 10." (S001) "I did not find the private markets to be a reliable way to make money." (S021)
