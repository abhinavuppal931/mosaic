# Stories: Stanley Druckenmiller

Built from 16 spoken sources (S001 to S048, 2009 to 2026) through the verified batch notes. These are the trades and mistakes he retells, most-used first, with losers given as much room as winners. Figures are as he told them; where tellings differ, the range is given.

## 1. 1999 to 2000: the internet round trip and the envy trade
Sources: 8 (S001, S004, S005, S006, S009, S010, S026, S047).
In March 1999 he shorted about 200 million dollars of internet stocks and lost 600 to 800 million in two to four weeks (S004, S005). "I shorted 200 million dollars worth of Internet stocks in March of 99 and in three weeks covered them at a 600 million dollar loss" (S047) The companies later went bankrupt, but he was early. He pivoted long on Greenspan easing and finished 1999 up 35 to 42 percent (S001, S004). He sold all tech in January 2000 at about 104 times earnings, then watched two young managers keep making money and bought about 6 billion back near the top, losing 2 to 3 billion (S001, S004, S010). "I think I missed the top by an hour." (S001) and "It was all because I got emotional and dropped every tool of discipline I’ve ever had." (S010) In 2015 he named the Nasdaq buy at 150 times earnings "It was 2000. It was the exact top." (S009) He says he learned nothing because he already knew the lesson (S001, S006).
Lesson: the worst losses come from emotion, not analysis; short losses are open ended, and envy of other people's gains breaks discipline. "bulls make money, bears make money, and pigs get slaughtered. I'm here to tell you I was a pig." (S001)

## 2. Short the pound, 1992: sizing a one-way bet
Sources: 4 (S001, S005, S019, S026).
The cost of being wrong was about half a percent of forward points; the payoff, if sterling broke, was huge. "if they didn’t devalue in the next six months, my fund was going to lose 50 basis points; if they did devalue, I was going to make 2,000 basis points." (S019) He had 1.5 billion short in a 7 billion fund and proposed going to 100 percent; Soros told him to go bigger, to about 200 percent (S001, S026). He made about 20 percent on the pound and more in the second-order trades, gilts and UK equities; the fund made 62 percent that year (S005). "You get a theme and then you look at the concentric circles or the dominoes that fall because of a theme." (S026)
Lesson: size on the payoff ratio, then trade the dominoes. "what I learned from Soros is when you have conviction, you should bet really big." (S026)

## 3. Nvidia, 2022 to 2024: luck, conviction, and the winner he could not hold
Sources: 4 (S006, S016, S026, S047).
A young partner told him in fall 2022 that AI would outweigh blockchain; he bought a company he could not spell, ChatGPT arrived about a month later, and he doubled, then doubled again (S006, S016). "It was just total luck." (S026) In June 2023 he said "Nvidia is something we're going to want to own for at least two or three years not for 10 months and maybe longer" (S047), then sold around 800 to 900 in March 2024 and it went to about 1,400 (S006, S026). "I violated everything I said in the interview. I couldn’t stand success." (S006) and "I’m not used to making 6x my money in an equity in two years, and I’m not Warren Buffett." (S006)
Lesson: buy on the young analysts' trend call and add on confirming news; his weakness is holding a huge winner through a long trend.

## 4. Housing, 2003 to 2008: a loose Fed before the mechanism is visible
Sources: 4 (S001, S004, S009, S027).
In late 2003 nominal growth was about 9 percent and fed funds 1 percent; his analysts guessed fair rates of 3 to 6 percent (S001, S009). He felt trouble but could not see how it would end. In 2005 a sell-side analyst laid out subprime, he shorted everything housing in two days, was wrong for six months in 2006, and made money in 2007 and 2008 (S001, S004). "’06 I stunk. I had made some money but I left the party." (S027)
Lesson: a central bank too loose against strong data is the warning; have the macro conviction first and let the mechanism show up.

## 5. The 2000 sabbatical and the Treasury trade
Sources: 3 (S004, S005, S026).
After the tech loss he quit, went to Africa with no TV or papers, and came back on Labor Day 2000. Oil, rates and the dollar were all up and earnings estimates looked absurd. He put 300 to 350 percent of the fund in 10-year equivalents of Treasuries and made about 40 percent in the fourth quarter (S004, S005, S026). "If you take a break, take a break. Don't sit there and like trade your own account and mess around." (S004)
Lesson: a clean slate after a beating lets you see the next trade and size it.

## 6. Pittsburgh National, 1978 to 1979: too young to be scared
Sources: 3 (S001, S005, S010).
Made director of research at about 25 partly because his mentor wanted someone too young to be scared. After the Shah fell he put 70 percent in oil stocks and 30 percent in defense; the list rose 100 to 300 percent against a flat S&P (S001, S005). The same mentor taught him to watch the Fed and price action: "if all the news is great and the stocks not acting well, get out" (S005) His retail work there found that food and energy prices, not the earnings estimate, moved retail stocks (S010).
Lesson: find what actually moves the stock, then bet on it with conviction.

## 7. 30-year Treasuries at 14 percent, 1981
Sources: 2 (S001, S005).
Starting Duquesne, he put 50 percent of new capital in 30-year Treasuries yielding about 14 percent, betting Volcker would not let inflation run, and was up about 40 percent early (S001, S005). Looking back, he said with Soros's sizing "I probably would have put about 150 percent in the bonds." (S001)
Lesson: bonds, not stocks, launched the record; bet on the central bank's reaction function, and bigger than feels comfortable.

## 8. The Indonesian rupiah, 1996 (as he dates it): frozen by size
Sources: 1 (S005).
His worst memory: after profits shorting the Thai baht and Malaysian ringgit, he lost over a billion dollars in the rupiah and the year was roughly flat. He let others convince him the washout was over. "I was so big, relative to the size of the market, I froze." (S005)
Lesson: size relative to the market you must exit, not to the fund. "I would never get so big in something that I thought I couldn't get out where it would cost the fund maybe 1-2% in execution cost alone to get out." (S005)

## 9. The 1987 crash and the depression that never came
Sources: 3 (S005, S027, S047).
He caught the crash, then was sure a depression would follow. "In 1987, when the market crashed, I was convinced we were going to have a depression." (S027) He threw up two nights a week, fearing the win was luck (S005).
Lesson: a correct trade can sit beside a wrong forecast; he uses it to warn that he can be wrong on the economy. "after the 87 crash I was convinced we're going to have depression so I've been wrong before" (S047)

## 10. The two-year Treasury short, 2021 to 2022: the one he regrets most
Sources: 1 (S026).
After predicting inflation in spring 2021 he built a massive short in two-year notes at about 15 basis points, a pound-style one-way bet, and took most of it off near 150 basis points. Yields went to 500. "I regret deeply not holding that position." (S026)
Lesson: being mesmerized by how far a trade has come is a reason he sold a one-way bet too early.

## 11. The leveraged two-year long, 2023 to 2024
Sources: 1 (S016).
He held a massive leveraged two-year Treasury long because financial conditions were tight and inflation falling, revealed it by accident at a conference, and exited at 415 to 430 once the Fed pivot loosened conditions and companies reported pickups, without getting the low (S016). "financial conditions, which is one of the things that put me in them in the first place were turning" (S016)
Lesson: enter and exit on financial conditions, not on the last tick.

## 12. Right on bonds, wrong on stocks, 2023
Sources: 3 (S043, S047, S048).
He held the hard landing view from 2022 and kept equities near flat (S043). "In some ways being right on the bond market, which I was, and being right on earnings, which I was, made me completely wrong on the stock market." (S048) and "The problem is I didn't believe it, so I didn't have the big allocation like I should have" (S048)
Lesson: being right on the macro inputs does not make the equity call; price is the final test.

## 13. Missing the dollar, 2022
Sources: 1 (S043).
He called it "probably the biggest miss in my career" (S043) and gave a reason that was about distaste, not analysis: "I just couldn't bring myself to own Joe Biden and Jerome Powell." (S043)
Lesson: views on policymakers can block a trade the data supports; know your biases.

## 14. The tariff tweet, 2019: from 93 percent long to flat
Sources: 1 (S044).
Long into an expected trade deal, he was caught when the deal blew up on a tweet. "I went from 93% invested to net flat, and bought a bunch of treasuries." (S044) and "I got caught with my pants down, I didn’t see him doing what he did" (S044)
Lesson: when risk turns political and unreadable, step out first and think later.

## 15. Teva, 2025: buying the change before the re-rating
Sources: 1 (S006).
In mid to late 2025, as AI stocks got heated, his team brought in Teva near 16 dollars and about 6 times earnings with a new CEO moving a generics company into biosimilars; it re-rated to about 32 dollars and about 12 times as the plan proved out (S006). Value holders sold because it had become a growth story, and growth holders had not yet bought. "If you look at today, you’re not going to make any money. If you try and look ahead and what might change and how investors might perceive something ahead" (S006)
Lesson: pay for the change you can see 18 months out, and rely on an expert you trust for the facts.

## 16. Argentina in an afternoon, 2024
Sources: 1 (S016).
He watched Milei's Davos speech, asked Perplexity for the five most liquid Argentine ADRs, and bought them all before doing the work, then added. "I follow the old Soros rule, invest and then investigate. I bought all of them." (S016)
Lesson: get a position on when the concept fits the macro view, then let diligence decide whether to add or cut.

## 17. Commodities in 2008 and Brazil rates
Sources: 1 (S005).
In 2008 he followed the charts long commodities against a bearish economy because of the liquidity coming, and exited around May or June when the charts turned. In Brazil he bought rates at 13 to 14 percent and added up to 19 while the chart said no. "that was one of those rare cases where I ignored the charts I told you that I never ignored." (S005) He calls such a breach "That's like a 1 in 100 for me to violate to that extent." (S005)
Lesson: liquidity can beat a bad economy, and breaking the chart rule needs overwhelming conviction.

## 18. QE1 and the bond market, 2008 to 2009
Sources: 1 (S044).
He argued QE would be bearish for bonds and was early. "And by the way, I was dead wrong for five weeks." (S044) Then it worked, and QE as risk-on became a rule (S044).
Lesson: a sound policy read can still be weeks early; size so you survive the wait.

## 19. Bitcoin: the buy he could not fill
Sources: 1 (S010).
He tried to buy 100 million dollars near 6,200 and took two weeks to get 20 million, stopped, and watched it go to 36,000 (S010). Earlier he had missed it entirely: "I never owned it from like $50 to $17,000, I felt like a moron." (S010)
Lesson: liquidity sets how much you can own, and waiting on a moving asset has a cost.

## 20. The Berlin Wall and the mark, 1989
Sources: 1 (S026).
When the wall fell the mark dropped for two days on fears East Germany would pollute it. He knew German history and its obsession with inflation, bought the mark, and the run, he says, saved his job at Soros (S026).
Lesson: a regime change is the clearest 18-month picture there is; bet on what it does to the currency.
