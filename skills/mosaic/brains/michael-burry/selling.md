# Selling: Michael Burry

Built from the verified batch notes on his MSN journal and Value Investors Club write-ups (S031 to S042, 2000 to 2002), Scion letters and memos (S001, S020 to S027, 2001 to 2008), the FCIC interview and lecture (S028, S029), the 1999 forum posts (S006), the 2019 letters (S004) and the 2025 podcast (S005). His rules for selling, trimming, adding, covering and re-checking, most-used first. One rule changed during 2001: a mechanical stop at new lows gave way to holding or adding when only the price had fallen; both are shown with dates and the newer one is marked.

## Rules

### 1. A falling price with value intact is a reason to add, not to sell
Sources: 6 (S022, S023, S024, S039, S041, S042).
Newer view, from late 2001. "Such volatility in no way impacts the intrinsic value of the portfolio, and rather provides opportunity." (S023) He hoped a holding kept falling so he could buy more: "I will not reveal the name of this company here, as I do hope it continues to fall" (S023). In 2002 he laddered buys into Reuters on the way down: "The value is higher than the current price by a large degree, however, and therefore falling prices are beneficial." (S042) Looking back on 2001: "And once having bought such securities with near-term price risk, I should never have sold them simply because they fell in the near term." (S041) The fund-level version: "The goal here should be neither to take profits when the Fund is up significantly nor to cut losses when the Fund is down significantly." (S024)

### 2. Older rule: cut the loss on a break to new lows
Sources: 3 (S038, S039, S040).
Older view, 2000 to early 2001, for anything but the rare deep-value finds. "And if a stock, other than the rare birds discussed above, breaks to a new low, in most cases I cut the loss." (S038) and "I also generally use broken support as an exit point." (S039) He defended it as practical: "since implementing this rule I haven't had a single misfortune blow up my entire portfolio." (S038) In small stocks he exited as they crossed below $5, ahead of margin calls (S040). Superseded by rule 1 once he saw it had sold good holdings on price alone (inferred from S039, S041, S024); the Caterpillar sale is the example (S039).

### 3. Sell the quick pop and the overvalued winner
Sources: 5 (S005, S006, S031, S038, S041).
"So I am not afraid to sell when a stock has a quick 40% to 50% a pop." (S038) A spike from a technical imbalance, such as an index addition or window dressing, is a sale: "spikes are spikes." (S041) On GTSI he described the old pattern as buy under 3 and sell the spike (S031). Still his view in 2025: "If you own something that has been gone up a lot, you've done really, really well in it, it's shooting straight up, and you think it's kind of overvalued, I think that's something you should sell." (S005) The cost of this rule is selling a compounder early, as with Apple in 1999 (S006).

### 4. Sell when management breaks the thesis
Sources: 5 (S004, S036, S040, S041, S042).
Behaviour ends a thesis faster than price. He sold Senior Housing when management issued equity below value (S041): "You want to issue stock above intrinsic value, not below intrinsic value." (S041) For REITs, "So traditionally the time to sell REITs is when they issue stock in a secondary or tertiary offering." (S041) He sold London Pacific and Spherion when spin-off pricing and CEO comments broke the case (S040). Stock-funded acquisitions and cash spent on diversification instead of buybacks are the same signal (S036, S004).

### 5. When a loss looks permanent, find the reason and own the mistake
Sources: 4 (S036, S040, S041, S042).
"My experience is that when one has losses that look other than temporary, there is usually a reason." (S041) ValueClick was sold near cost: "This stock was a case of good assets, bad business, bad management." (S041) Elan was sold within ten days of purchase after he found accounting leverage: "Time for a mea culpa." (S042) "No investor has a 1.000 batting average, but every mistake deserves scrutiny and this one will get it." (S040)

### 6. Exit an event-driven trade when the event fails
Sources: 1 (S040).
"in event-driven investment if the event does not turn out as predicted, the only prudent thing to do is to exit the position." (S040) Buy one only with a floor: "what you want is a fundamental floor to your valuation in the worst possible case." (S040)

### 7. No single position may ruin the portfolio; trim when the no-brainer price is gone
Sources: 3 (S028, S039, S041).
"One of the tenets of my philosophy is not to let single positions ruin a portfolio." (S039) On American Physicians at 20 to 22: "I took advantage of a no-brainer price when I took such a large position, but at this price I’ll scale it back to a still large but more average-sized position." (S041) Even the credit trade had a ceiling inside the fund (S028).

### 8. Take the gain: taxes and greed are not reasons to hold
Sources: 3 (S021, S024, S041).
"I will not let the prospect of taxes on gains prevent the achievement of those gains." (S021) He broke this in January 2001: "I allowed it to persuade me to hold on to a few extremely profitable positions too long." (S021) He sold the large caps bought after September 2001 once they met two-to-three-year goals in two months (S024, paraphrase). He sold IBP after it ran from 15 to 23.52 because "I am not a risk arbitrageur." (S041)

### 9. Raise the buy limit when value rises, not when price rises
Sources: 2 (S041, S042).
After a buyback lifted Wellsford's value per share: "in this case 18.50 now is cheaper than 16.45 was back before the buyback." (S041) The limit otherwise holds: "I won’t pay more than $7 per share" (S042).

### 10. Recycle into sharper value and keep powder dry
Sources: 4 (S006, S021, S040, S041).
He sold Apple in 1999 to fund names he saw as more acutely undervalued (S006). He raises cash in fast markets for the next slow one (S021, paraphrase). "I’m happy to keep some powder dry for better values later." (S040) and "Don’t worry about missing a rally. Worry about losing your money." (S041)

### 11. Shorts: set a cover target, use buy stops, avoid crowded shorts
Sources: 3 (S040, S042, S024).
"I originally shorted it at roughly 30 with a target cover price of about 9." (S042) In a rally against his shorts he set buy stops: "No need to lose my shirt if the market goes haywire to the upside." (S040) He covered Standard Pacific once it reached book value (S040). He shorts only lightly shorted stocks (S024) and stayed away after watching AmeriCredit's 75% squeeze in 12 days (S042, paraphrase).

### 12. On a contractual short, ignore interim marks and hold to the data
Sources: 4 (S001, S025, S026, S029).
"Again, ultimate performance matters much more than the valuation marks accorded us by our counterparties in the interim." (S025) The payoff grows as the pools decay: "At the end of the day, we have the contractual right to receive cash when these tranches deteriorate to the requisite level, and that is a right that grows more valuable every single day." (S001) He expected no payoff in year one and likely not year two (S025). Through the 2006 drawdown: "But I knew my analysis was correct, and not one of the detractors seemed to be able to get any of the details right." (S029)

### 13. Exit when the rules of the game may change
Sources: 3 (S002, S027, S028).
Late in 2007 he cut corporate credit shorts to the likeliest defaulters: "Late in 2007, I had reduced the positions to include mostly only those that I felt were most likely to default." (S027) Then "By early 2008, I feared the effects of government intervention and exited all our remaining credit default positions" (S002). All mortgage swaps were sold by about March 2008 (S028).

### 14. Do not sell illiquid holdings into a panic
Sources: 3 (S022, S023, S040).
"As a result, the time to exit such positions is certainly not the present." (S023) He expected cheap positions to return to par: "In most cases, I expect many of these securities to move back to par within a reasonable time frame." (S022) He wants falling knives to stop before buying more, not before holding: "I should always wait for my rules to kick in, and that includes waiting for falling knives to lay motionless on the floor before trying to pick them up." (S040)

## Kill criteria this seat would write
Checkable facts that would end a thesis, in his terms (inferred from S004, S005, S021, S025, S040, S041, S042 unless noted).

For a long:
- The company issues stock below intrinsic value, or buys companies with its own undervalued shares (S040, S041).
- Cash above the market cap goes to acquisitions or new ventures instead of buybacks (S004, S036).
- Buybacks spent to offset dilution, deducted from free cash flow, leave little owner cash flow; or buybacks fail to cut the share count (S005, S040).
- Days sales outstanding jumps, profit depends on pro forma items, capitalized costs or interest income, or a big-bath charge resets the base (S040, S042).
- The business needs open capital markets to survive, since common stock is the riskiest layer (S042).
- The event in an event-driven trade fails (S040).
- Insiders sell into strength and none buy on the way down (S040, S031).

For a short or a bear thesis:
- The structural trigger passes without stress: resets arrive, delinquencies and losses stay under the credit support (S001, S025).
- Policy changes the payoff, such as government intervention or a bailout of the counterparty class (S002).
- A counterparty stops posting collateral or cannot pay (S025, S028).
- Short interest gets crowded enough that the shorts control the stock (S042).
- Price reaches his rule-of-thumb value, such as a homebuilder back at book (S040).
- Cumulative carry exceeds the budget he set at entry, about six percent of assets a year for the mortgage short (S025).

## Re-check triggers
- Monthly: pool data from servicer reports, problem loans over credit support, loss severity, not headlines (S001, S025).
- Every quarter: read the cash flow statement first, the options tax benefit and the share count (S021, S040, S042).
- Insider buying or selling, and lock-up expiries (S031, S040, S041).
- Secondary or tertiary stock offerings, and any stock-funded acquisition (S036, S041).
- November to January: tax-loss selling and the January bounce; quarter-end window dressing and Russell additions (S021, S041).
- About one year after a spin-off, when forced selling usually ends (S032).
- A small stock crossing below $5, where margin calls start (S040).
- A takeover bid, a failed buyout or a tender offer: compare the bid with value (S024, S035).
- For a mania: loan terms loosening, and capex less depreciation against GDP (S029, S005).
- Results judged over three to five years, never one quarter (S021, S024).

## Selling mistakes he admitted, with dates
- 1999: sold Apple after a 25 to 30% run-up. "Shame shame. But I make no excuses." (S006)
- 2000-09: sold Caterpillar on a break to new lows. "I pulled the trigger too early in selling Caterpillar." (S039)
- 2001-01: held tax-loss winners into the March selloff. "I did know better, and I did break with a long-standing tax policy that has contributed significantly to my success as a portfolio manager." (S021)
- 2001-04: sold DiamondCluster near 10 after buying near 14; a later deal implied about 21.50 (S040).
- 2001: sold near-term price-risk names because they fell (S041); waited too long to sell IBP, which cost about a dollar a share (S041).
- 2001-Q3: bought too little in the September panic. "In retrospect, one might argue I ought to have rushed to take larger positions at the time." (S024)
- 2006, late: a fire sale of corporate credit shorts to survive possible redemptions gave up billions of potential gains (S029).
- 2007 to 2008: sold most mortgage swaps under investor pressure rather than at the top, then closed the fund (S002).
