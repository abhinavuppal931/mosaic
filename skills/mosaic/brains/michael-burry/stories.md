# Stories: Michael Burry

Built from the verified batch notes on his forum posts, MSN journal, Value Investors Club write-ups, Scion letters and memos, FCIC interview, lecture, speech, 2019 letters, 2025 podcast and Cassandra Unchained posts (S001 to S046, 1999 to 2026). Case studies he returns to, most-used first, with losers and selling mistakes given equal room. Figures are as he reported them at the time.

## 1. The subprime short, 2005 to 2008
Sources: 9 (S001, S002, S005, S025, S026, S027, S028, S029, S030).
From June 2005 he bought credit default swaps on the thin BBB and BBB- tranches of 2005-vintage subprime pools, chosen from the filings, and timed the payoff to the teaser-rate resets about two years after origination (S025, S029). He shorted the subordinate slices "specifically so that the short position would not be dependent on the Armageddon scenario for U.S. residential housing." (S025) Peak short about $8.4 billion notional with a maximum loss under $100 million (S029, S030). The first good month was February 2007 (S028). He later called it "a once-in-a-century type trade." (S005)
Lesson: find the structural trigger and its date, buy the asymmetric instrument, and do not expect it to repeat. "I've basically told everybody I can, you know, ever since that that's not going to happen again anytime soon." (S005)

## 2. Early, angry investors, and closing the fund
Sources: 6 (S001, S002, S005, S026, S028, S029).
In 2006 the funds were down 17.36% for the year while the short bled carry (S001). He side-pocketed the mortgage swaps, faced redemption demands, closed the Hong Kong office and cut staff (S026, S029). "Facing a very angry crowd of investors, many of whom were demanding their money back, I closed our Hong Kong Office, cut salaries, and laid off staff." (S029) His attempt to raise a dedicated vehicle (Milton's Opus) failed: "The effort showed I cannot sell ANYTHING." (S029) Even after the win, "My investors were generally mad at me." (S005) He closed Scion in 2008 (S002).
Lesson: a right bet can be early by more than a year, and the investor base must be able to sit through it. Putting everything in writing protected him: "I think, were the main reason I didn't get sued by my investors." (S005)

## 3. Counterparties and the fight over marks
Sources: 5 (S001, S025, S026, S028, S029).
He negotiated daily collateral and near-equal terms with each dealer (S028). Deutsche Bank dropped him in 2005: "Deutsche bank fired us a client right in 05 because we were too aggressive in prosecuting our marks." (S028) Counterparties marked his book against him and later joined the trade (S029). He suspected Goldman and Bank of America of games at the end of 2006, then admitted "my original logic for there was probably not right." (S028) Two contracts he kept to the end: "we actually held them just for spite. Just to show that they go to zero." (S028)
Lesson: the counterparty is part of the thesis, and letting the other side mark your position was a mistake he admits (S001).

## 4. WorldCom to AIG: how to short a gilded giant
Sources: 4 (S026, S027, S028, S029).
WorldCom going from investment grade to bankrupt taught him the cheap way to short: buy credit protection on leveraged, highly rated firms. "So it hit me, that's the way to short." (S028) In spring 2005 he bought protection on AIG and the AAA monolines because they did not have to post collateral; he reasoned a downgrade below AA would force calls on them (S028, S029). Corporate credit shorts reached $4.27 billion notional, mostly financials (S026).
Lesson: look for "Companies that you think look so gilded now, but you think might tumble, with asymmetric risk taking." (S028)

## 5. From cheap homebuilders to the mortgage pools
Sources: 3 (S028, S029, S040).
Homebuilders looked cheap around 2002, but "I decided that I didn't think they were terribly attractive because they were benefiting tremendously from the increase in land values on their inventory" (S028). That led him to ask how housing was financed, to mortgage insurer filings, then to the pools themselves, then to credit derivatives (S028). "Importantly, because subprime mortgages were being turned into securities, there were mandatory regulatory filings, and this is how I educated myself." (S029)
Lesson: a cheap stock you reject can point at the real trade; follow each answer to the next question.

## 6. September 2001: averaging down into fear
Sources: 2 (S023, S024).
His largest position on September 11 was an airline, and two hotel stocks fell with it; one hotel franchise traded at its free cash, so "an international hotel franchise lacking any recourse debt now goes for free on the stock exchange." (S023) While other value managers sold illiquid names to "upgrade" into popular ones, he averaged down into a stock at three quarters of trailing free cash flow (S023). It tripled by late October and drew bids near seven times its September 30 price. "As it turns out, we did not have to wait five years, or even a year or two." (S024)
Lesson: separate a flow-driven fall from a fall in value, and buy the first. He also admitted he should have bought more (S024).

## 7. Adobe and the stock-option shell game
Sources: 2 (S021, S040).
In 2001 he took Adobe's $125 million tax benefit from option exercises, divided by about 35%, and found about $357 million of compensation missing from the income statement; operating income fell from $408 million to $51 million (S021, S040). "Of course, it is a shell game." (S021) Adobe, Siebel, Oracle and Xilinx paid employees in options close to or above reported net income (S021).
Lesson: if the IRS lets a company deduct it, it is a cost to owners; tech firms that hide it are "private companies in the public domain" (S040).

## 8. Cisco: the big bath
Sources: 2 (S040, S041).
In April 2001 Cisco wrote off over 60% of inventory, then beat on a pro forma basis. He read it as a company built for insiders: "A private company in the public domain, existing to take advantage of shareholders, not to benefit shareholders." (S040) and "In short, Cisco lived and breathed the bubble that is now burst." (S041)
Lesson: a big-bath charge erases the profits that justified the price, and a post-bath beat is not a recovery.

## 9. Magma: the IPO short
Sources: 2 (S041, S042).
In December 2001 he shorted Magma near 30: no profits, accounting changes that pulled revenue forward, 24 of 30 million shares coming off lock-up, and 36 times revenue against 3 to 6 times for peers (S041). He added five times as lock-ups neared and covered near his target: "I originally shorted it at roughly 30 with a target cover price of about 9." (S042) "That’s good enough." (S042)
Lesson: an IPO built to exit venture holders, with a lock-up clock and aggressive accounting, is a short with a date on it.

## 10. Shorts in a bear-market rally, and the AmeriCredit squeeze
Sources: 2 (S040, S042).
In spring 2001 his shorts in Krispy Kreme, Standard Pacific, Kohl's and GE went against him in a sharp rally; he set buy stops so "No need to lose my shirt if the market goes haywire to the upside." (S040) He covered Standard Pacific near book value under his homebuilder rule (S040). In 2002 he watched heavy short interest drive AmeriCredit up 75% in 12 days and concluded to stay away from stocks the shorts control (S042, paraphrase).
Lesson: a short needs a cover rule and a stop, and a crowded short is its own risk.

## 11. Index funds as the new CDOs
Sources: 3 (S003, S024, S029).
Bogle mocked him in Forbes and Vanguard's attorneys threatened his early website (S024, S029). In 2019 he called passive the next bubble: "This structured asset play is the same story again and again, so easy to sell, such a self-fulfilling prophecy as the technical machinery kicks in." (S003) His proof was liquidity: 1,049 Russell 2000 members traded under $5 million a day (S003).
Lesson: when flows set prices instead of analysis, check the size of the exit door against the crowd in the theater.

## 12. Apple 1999, sold too soon; Medusa and BMC from the screen
Sources: 1 (S006).
He bought Apple as a value stock with growth thrown in, at about 10 times earnings ex-cash, then sold. "I bought it as a Buffett pick. And then I sold it after a quick 25-30% run-up. Shame shame. But I make no excuses." (S006) and "I got out. But I sorely want back in." (S006) His midcap screen found Medusa, taken out at a 50% premium within months (S006).
Lesson: "This problem of ultra-quick 30% gains despite Buffetesque intent is vexing, but not unpleasant." (S006)

## 13. ValueClick: good assets, bad business
Sources: 2 (S036, S041).
In November 2000 the market cap sat below cash plus a Japanese stake, so the US business came free (S036). Then management bought a competitor with stock, "the bonehead move of the century." (S036) He sold near cost in 2001: "This stock was a case of good assets, bad business, bad management." (S041)
Lesson: an asset discount does not protect you from managers who spend the assets.

## 14. Elan: the ten-day mea culpa
Sources: 1 (S042).
In 2002 he bought Elan on real cash flow and a scandal discount, then dug into its accounting leverage and capitalized pipeline costs and sold within about ten days. "Time for a mea culpa." (S042)
Lesson: scandal stocks are often over-punished, but only buy one that needs no capital markets, and sell fast when the safety you assumed is not there.

## 15. Huttig, Pillowtex and Wellsford: buying from forced sellers
Sources: 4 (S032, S034, S037, S041).
Huttig was a spin-off dumped by holders who did not want it; he rebuilt the numbers, paid 3.1 times EBITDA and then waited more than a year (S032, S041). "value will indeed out, as Ben Graham once intoned." (S041) At Pillowtex he bought post-bankruptcy equity from creditors at 0.32 times book (S034). At Wellsford he bought real estate at 50 to 61 cents on the dollar once a liquidation plan was visible: "For four years others have done the waiting for me" (S037).
Lesson: a seller who must sell sets a price that is "price risk but not business risk." (S032)

## 16. Caterpillar and Mesaba: selling too early, buying too early
Sources: 2 (S039, S041).
He sold Caterpillar in September 2000 when it broke to new lows and wrote "I pulled the trigger too early in selling Caterpillar." (S039) In August 2001 he bought the airline Mesaba, which became his worst absolute mistake after September 11 (S041, paraphrase).
Lesson: a mechanical stop can cut a good holding, and a cheap cyclical can still be hit by an event no one prices.

## 17. Bachoco: the Mexican chicken company
Sources: 2 (S033, S042).
In October 2002 he wrote up Industrias Bachoco at 2 times free cash flow, no net debt and about half of book: "a statistical anomaly of a valuation" (S033). He named the reason for the price: no one would look at a Mexican chicken company (S033). His target was modest: "I’m not asking for an extravagant valuation; 8-9 times earnings and par with book value would provide tremendous price appreciation from the current level, especially when paired with the dividend." (S042)
Lesson: neglect, not risk, can explain a price; list the real risks up front and check they are already in it.

## 18. GameStop, 2019: cash above the market cap
Sources: 1 (S004).
With the stock under $4, 63% short interest and over $480 million in cash, he wrote to the board demanding the remaining $237.6 million buyback at once, which would retire over 80% of the shares (S004). "GameStop does not need ghosts of the past protecting a legacy of poor capital allocation and thin oversight at this point in time." (S004)
Lesson: when a company trades below its cash, the market is voting on capital allocation, and the fix is a buyback and a board that owns stock.

## 19. Palantir and NVIDIA, 2025: puts, not shorts
Sources: 1 (S005).
He bought puts about two years out, far below the price, on a stock he thought made little; the position cost about $10 million, not the $1 billion the press read from the notional 13F value (S005). He had owned NVIDIA around 2015 to 2016 and now called it lucky: "NVIDIA was lucky. They got lucky once with the crypto mining because crypto mining needed GPUs." (S005)
Lesson: in a mania, cap the cost of being early with long-dated options, and read a 13F as notional, not as dollars at risk.

## 20. Summer 1994: follow the incentives
Sources: 1 (S007).
As a medical student he spent a summer in the American Hospital Association library and wrote that rehab's boom rested on a Medicare payment gap that would close. "The problem is it will not last." (S007) He republished it in 2026 as the root of his method: "Too much of the debate in health care comes from a place of passionate personal anecdote. Much of it is predictable by following the incentives." (S007)
Lesson: a boom tied to a subsidy or a policy gap ends when the gap does; find who is paid for what.
