# Stories: Tom Lee

Built from the verified batch notes on 21 interviews and talks (S001 to S021, 2019-10 to 2026-11). These are the cases he returns to, ordered by how many sources carry them. Misses and early calls count as much as wins. Levels and prices are his dated public numbers, kept only where the lesson needs them; they are not calls on today's markets.

1. **Wireless in the 1990s: the incumbents called it a toy.** He covered wireless from his early twenties (S004, S021) while money managers in their 40s and 50s dismissed cell phones that had 34 million subscribers in 1993 (S001, S017, S019). "But in the investment business, people tend to like the incumbents." (S017) He modeled long-run penetration from teenagers: "if 100% of like teenagers have a cell phone, by the time they're 60, the penetration rate should be whatever it is." (S004) The sub-industries cycled as a group: "they all moved in the cycle together and they all crashed together." (S016) Lesson: the young set the adoption curve, and a theme's pieces rise and fall together before a few pull away. Sources: 6 (S001, S004, S016, S017, S019, S021).

2. **The 2017 Bitcoin call and the clients who left.** He explained Bitcoin through network effects (wallets and activity per wallet) and pitched it to institutions as digital gold (S003, S004, S021). It cost him: "decline in paying clients at Fundstrat because they thought we completely lost our mind" (S009). J.P. Morgan had passed on the idea in 2012 over reputation risk (S017, S019). He says the small 1 to 2% recommendation would later have grown to most of a portfolio (S019, his account). Lesson: an early thematic call carries reputational cost, so size it small and wait. Sources: 6 (S003, S004, S009, S017, S019, S021).

3. **BitMine: an ETH treasury built on equity.** Joined as chairman on 2025-06-30 (S002) with a goal of 5% of ETH supply (S009). Through the 2026 winter: "we bought Ethereum for 60 weeks straight, you know, and we never had to sell it." (S014) Raises were timed above NAV and kept clean: "we wanted to keep the capital structure clean and the whole endeavor was funded with equity, no debt, no convertible instruments." (S014) Near the target: "And now we're done stacking while a bull market is starting." (S015) Lesson: accumulate while a theme is unpopular, avoid forced sellers, stop at the target. It is his own company, so read his ETH views as a holder's. Sources: 5 (S002, S009, S013, S014, S015).

4. **2009 at J.P. Morgan: a month early, then a hated bull.** "Yeah, so we were a month early. So we suffered a drawdown." (S018) He forecast S&P earnings back to 60 while the buy side doubted, and "S&P earnings was over 60 by 2010" (S009); no one expected that for a decade (S019). People called the rally a head fake for years: "It really wasn't until maybe 2012 that people acknowledged that the bottom, in fact, was in." (S005) Lesson: earnings recover faster than a spreadsheet allows, and early-cycle bulls are hated. Sources: 4 (S005, S009, S018, S019).

5. **April 2025: the tariff waterfall.** In March he compared the selloff to 2016: "I think this is actually a lot like Brexit" (S007). He held that it was a waterfall decline that would V while critics doubted: "And we kept saying it's waterfall declines are V-shaped, but no one could really believe it." (S002) The tell was credit: high yield priced about 10% recession odds against economists' 60% (S003). He brushed off estimate cuts: "And I said it didn't matter because it's a waterfall decline." (S009) Lesson: if credit is calm, a fast fall is panic, not a broken cycle. Sources: 4 (S002, S003, S007, S009).

6. **Bitcoin at $200,000 by the end of 2025, then a crypto winter.** In October 2025: "I still think it's possible for Bitcoin to hit $200,000 by the end of the year." (S016) In a debate the same month he put a year-end target range above that (S013). The next year he was describing a crypto winter (S014) and later a four-year cycle bottom in October 2026 (S015), a reversal of his longer-cycle lean. Lesson, recorded as a miss: his dated coin targets were his most fragile calls, and the seat should not repeat the habit. Sources: 4 (S013, S014, S015, S016).

7. **The 1999 internet basket.** "if you bought a basket of internet stocks, 97% went to zero." (S017) Yet "if you bought the internet, the internet basket in 99 okay so you bought it near the peak and you held it to today you actually still outperformed the S&P 500" (S001), and "after the dot com bust of 99, the biggest stock winners were the dot com stocks." (S006) Lesson: in an exponential theme most names die and the basket still wins, so own the theme, not a guess at the winner. Sources: 3 (S001, S006, S017).

8. **March 2020: the V others doubted.** "stock market recoveries are symmetric to the decline. So the faster you fall, the faster you rebound." (S020) Stocks bottomed about six months before vaccines (S021). Businesses that grew through it passed "the ultimate stress test" (S020). He counts the clients he kept invested as his best result: "Tom, you kept me from selling everything in April or you kept me from selling everything in 2020." (S018) Lesson: a shock that does not break earnings power earns a higher multiple, not a lower one. Sources: 3 (S018, S020, S021).

9. **Walmart, Costco and Apple: the label sets the multiple.** "But because they essentially systematize their business, Walmart and Costco trade 35 to 50 times board earnings." (S013) Apple carried a low multiple while it was seen as hardware: "if they're viewed as a hardware company, that's why the multiple was always so low." (S020) Lesson: a rerating comes when the one-line description changes; it is his case for banks becoming tech (S018). Sources: 3 (S013, S018, S020).

10. **1999 from the inside: he knows what a top looks like.** He passed on IPO mandates: "but I passed on so many deals that wanted to do multi-billion dollar IPOs that would have been hundreds of millions of dollars of fees for the bank" (S018). Clients scorned normal upside: "I remember putting a buy rating on a stock and it had 25% upside and they're like, Tom, I can make that in a week." (S004) "So I know what a top looks like." (S018) Lesson: tops arrive with speculation, IPO frenzy and contempt for normal returns, not with skepticism. Sources: 2 (S004, S018).

11. **The 2019 inversion that was not a signal.** Eight of nine earlier inversions came from Fed hikes; this one came from falling rates: "this is an anomalous inversion, just like 98, and it was not an economic signal" (S008). He framed that summer as a test: "if the markets don't break down here, we're about to have a massive breakout in the equity markets." (S017) Lesson: ask why a classic signal fired before acting on it. Sources: 2 (S008, S017).

12. **2022: his worst call.** "I would say my worst call was 2022" (S018). A technician on his team warned and he underweighted it: "And I think if I really appreciated his message, we wouldn't have, we would have played 2022 differently." (S018) Lesson he took: "a real investor, even if they only care about fundamentals, should never ignore technicals because it's really the technicians that get the tops, the turning points correct." (S014) Sources: 2 (S014, S018).

13. **Cisco in 1997: a growth recession, not the end.** "Cisco fell from $9 to $5. A year later, it was at $18." (S010) "exponential growth industries don't have the same rate of change sequentially" (S010) Mobile had about six such cycles in 30 years (S010). He uses Cisco's 1999 multiple as the yardstick for AI leaders (S016). Lesson: a slowdown inside a secular theme is a dip to hold, not a thesis break. Sources: 2 (S010, S016).

14. **Fiber, RCA and the second owner.** After the fiber bust, cheap capacity made Expedia and streaming possible: "the second owner of a hotel made money." (S003) RCA built the ecosystem it sold into: "RCA made radios. Then they had to actually create radio stations across the country." (S010) The 1929 crash was a stock bubble, not a radio bubble (S010). Lesson: circular spending can be ecosystem building, and a bust hands cheap assets to the next wave. Sources: 2 (S003, S010).

15. **Missing gold.** "I kind of regret not recommending gold because it's been so great." (S016) Earlier he had noted gold beating the S&P six of seven years (S007), but Fundstrat does not cover commodities: "I'm not studying it, so I don't know." (S016) Lesson: a theme outside your coverage can run without you; say so rather than improvise. Sources: 2 (S007, S016).

16. **The 1997 Mobile Data Report: right on the theme, wrong on the stocks.** At Salomon he forecast a $40 billion mobile data industry: "turned out to be vastly bigger. But the stocks didn't do that well." (S003) The winners did not exist yet, and ten years into wireless only two groups captured the value (S003). Lesson: being right that a theme is early is not the same as owning who captures it. Sources: 1 (S003).

17. **Domino's over Robert Half.** For a labor-shortage theme, the staffing firm was the obvious pick and the pizza chain the winner: "So it was better to feed the worker than to supply the worker." (S003) Lesson: look for the second-order beneficiary of a theme. Sources: 1 (S003).

18. **Arista, sold too soon.** He traded in and out of a name that later made his timely list: "I probably shouldn't have sold it." (S005) Lesson: trading around a theme winner costs more than sitting through its volatility. Sources: 1 (S005).

19. **Supermicro out of the Granny Shots.** Dropped on auditing and compliance trouble after it had already paid: "Now there was a drawdown with Supermicro, but it already produced a lot of returns for the granny." (S007) Lesson: a broken data trail ends a position even inside a strong theme. Sources: 1 (S007).

20. **Frozen foods and the fear of lost jobs.** Frozen food cut farm labor from about 40% of the workforce to about 2% and food's share of the wallet sharply (S001). "when I look at past episodes of huge labor disruption, it's actually had positive outcomes." (S001) Lesson: he applies it to AI and labor, while admitting the risk that AI goes badly for workers (S001). Sources: 1 (S001).
