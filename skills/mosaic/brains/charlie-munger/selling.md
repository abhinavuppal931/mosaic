# Selling: Charlie Munger

Built from the verified batch notes on his talks (S001 to S021), Blue Chip Stamps and Wesco letters (S026 to S044, S066 to S079), Daily Journal meetings (S022 to S025, S045 to S053) and interviews (S007 to S009, S065, S080 to S083). His rules for selling, trimming, adding and re-checking, most-used first. He sold rarely and said so: "I'm looking for holds." (S048) and "So I'm no good at exits." (S048) Most of his selling rules are really rules for catching his own misjudgment. One rule shifted: in 2021 he had no system for a great company at a nosebleed price; by 2023 Berkshire had trimmed BYD at about 50 times earnings. Both are shown with dates.

## Rules

### 1. Destroy your own best-loved ideas; disconfirming evidence triggers a re-check
Sources: 9 (S002, S003, S004, S017, S021, S023, S046, S049, S025).
- 1986: The trap is "a tendency to process new and disconfirming information so that any original conclusion remains intact." (S003)
- 1995: McDonnell Douglas "ignored the strong disconfirming evidence from the failure of the first test" (S002) and failed again.
- 1996 and 2007: Copy Darwin, "who spent much of his long life thinking in reverse as he tried to disprove his own hardest-won and best-loved ideas." (S017) "We all remember that Darwin paid special attention to disconfirming evidence, particularly when it disconfirmed something he believed and loved." (S021)
- 2003: "If you can get really good at destroying your own wrong ideas, that is a great gift." (S004)
- 2017-02: "I've done so many dumb things that I think I'm very busy destroying bad ideas because I keep having them" (S046)
- 2020-02: "Of course you must be reexamining what you previously thought particularly when disconfirming evidence comes through." (S049)
- 2023-02: The idea he destroyed that year was online retail at Alibaba: "I just got a little out of focus and that made me overestimate the future returns from Alibaba." (S025)

### 2. Sell when you were wrong, and do it fast
Sources: 6 (S007, S016, S023, S049, S065, S048).
- 1996: Commitment and deprival reaction make people throw good money after bad: "People go broke that way because they can't stop, rethink, and say" (S016), I made a mistake. "Failure to handle psychological denial is a common way for people to go broke." (S016)
- 1966, told 2019-05 and 2023-10: Hochschild-Kohn was reversed at once: "We knew we'd made a big mistake and it was going to go to zero eventually." (S065) and "we realized we made a terrible mistake." (S007)
- 2020-02: "A good bit of the Munger fortune came from liquidating things we originally purchased because we were wrong." (S049)
- 2023-10: Berkshire sells only what it cannot fix: "If it's a problem in a business we've never been able to fix, we'll sell it." (S007)

### 3. Watch your own psychology as an owner: endowment, consistency and denial
Sources: 5 (S002, S016, S024, S050, S053).
- 1995: "Once owned, they suddenly become worth more to him than he would pay if they were offered for sale to him and he didn't already own them." (S002) Holders of big winners never sell because "mostly they fear missing out on future gains from stock sold or given away." (S002)
- 1996: "both Warren and I are very good at changing our prior conclusions." (S016)
- 2017-02: Watch for denial in managers whose results fade: "they handle that by denial." (S053)
- 2021-02: He admitted his own bias: "we're likely to be a little too loyal. I don't think we'll change on that." (S050)

### 4. Never sell a great business only because the price is high, but do not add there
Sources: 6 (S025, S048, S051, S052, S009, S024).
- 2019-02: Holding Costco beats selling: "I'd be less rich not more after taxes" (S048)
- 2022-02: "I never even think about selling a share of Costco just because it's selling at a high price." (S051) and "So, I'm not saying I'm buying Costco at this price, but I'm certainly not selling any." (S051)
- 2022-04: Durable compounders are held through drawdowns: "We just ride it out." (S009)
- 2023-02: "I love everything about Costco. I'm a total addict. Never going to sell their share." (S052)
- 2021-02 (older): On BYD at a nosebleed price: "I so rarely hold a company like BYD that goes to a nosebleed price that I don't think I've got a system yet. I'm just learning as I go along." (S024)
- 2023-02 (newer): A very high multiple can justify a trim even in a remarkable company. Berkshire "sold part of ours, by the way, about a year ago at a much higher price than it's selling for now." (S025) and "BYD is talking about 50x earnings. That is a very high price." (S052)

### 5. Sell when a cheaper rival or new technology makes the business doomed
Sources: 9 (S001, S006, S008, S009, S025, S051, S052, S053, S065).
- 1994: Buffett would not put new capital into textiles: "But we're not going to put huge amounts of new capital into a lousy business." (S001) Even IBM "got bounced off the wave after" 60 years (S001).
- 2017-02: Dexter Shoes: "We've learned how awful it is to have somebody who is really way lower priced come in hard and how no amount of managerial skill could protect us." (S053)
- 2017-11: GM's managers did not think like owners, or "they would have seen that it was hopeless." (S008)
- 2019-05: "It happened because the competition and the change got so strong." (S065)
- 2020-12: "They have their little time, and then they get clobbered." (S006)
- 2022-02: Wring cash out of a dying business before it dies: "And yet we wrung enough money out of before they died." (S051)
- 2023-02: Talent does not save the incumbent: "Doesn't help. You die even though you're talented and hard working." (S052) and "Once I understood that better, it made me a better investor." (S025)

### 6. Test whether a decline is cyclical or permanent
Sources: 5 (S031, S036, S037, S038, S042).
- 1981: He named the condition that would end the Buffalo News: hopeless long-term prospects, not low current earnings (S042, paraphrase).
- 2003-03: "CORT would not be making these acquisitions if we believed its furniture rental business prospects were permanently impaired." (S031)
- 2008 to 2010: Precision Steel moved from "may" to "will surely" to permanent: "Because the steel warehouse business may revert to even more difficult conditions, more decline for Precision Steel may lie ahead." (S036) then "the general and ongoing decline in Precision Steel's physical volume is a serious reverse" (S038). Of CORT in 2009: "So far, CORT's business has been melting away faster than CORT can fix it." (S038)

### 7. A falling price is an option, not a verdict
Sources: 7 (S001, S009, S028, S037, S046, S048, S051).
- 1994: Mr. Market gives "the option of deciding whether you want to buy more, sell part of what you already have or do nothing at all." (S001)
- 2000-03: He reported a portfolio fall without promising a rebound: "It's no sure thing that the value of our marketable securities will quickly recover." (S028)
- 2009-03: Net worth fell sharply with no sale: "we are certain that in due course, Wesco will prosper" (S037)
- 2017-02: On 50% declines: "Don't try to avoid it. It will come." (S046)
- 2019-02: "I don't know why Apple stock is going up or down." (S048)
- 2022-02: "I think you just have to learn to live through them." (S051)

### 8. When management or incentives break, judge whether the cause is fixable
Sources: 6 (S046, S047, S050, S065, S082, S020).
- 2000-06: In his fable, one growth disappointment "triggered a careful examination of Quant Tech's financial reporting practices" (S020), and the stock fell again.
- 2017-02 and 2018-02: He held Wells Fargo after the cross-selling scandal because the cause was a fixable incentive error: "The one nice thing about doing something dumb is you probably won't do it again." (S046) and "I think Wells Fargo will end up better off for having made those mistakes." (S047)
- 2019-05: Judge the CEO on what he caused, not the outcome alone; the new CEO "was not responsible for the crazy incentive system that created the trouble" (S082). With a huge tax on a sale and a poor CEO, talk rather than sell (S065, paraphrase).
- 2021-02: He avoided GE on its culture: "I was not surprised when it blew up." (S050)

### 9. Taxes are a real cost of selling, but not a reason to hold a loser
Sources: 6 (S001, S024, S050, S052, S065, S025).
- 1994: "trying to minimize taxes too much is one of the great standard causes of really dumb mistakes." (S001)
- 2019-05: On Costco: "we have a huge gain now that we'd have had to pay a huge tax if we sold it." (S065)
- 2021-02: "On balance, we hold in certain of these position when, normally, we wouldn't buy a new position." (S050)
- 2023-02: On the bank stocks bought at the bottom: "I immediately give the government 40-some-percent, everything we sell out of it" (S052) and "I don't buy it and I don't sell it." (S025)

### 10. Sell the ordinary commodity winner; hold the compounder
Sources: 2 (S009, S053).
- 2022-04: Ballard Petroleum, bought below book: "When it tripled, I sold it." (S009) It then doubled again.
- 2017-02: The cost of selling a cheap find too early: "the stock went from $1 to $40, but of course I sold my stock at $15." (S053)

### 11. Write the mistake down openly and early
Sources: 6 (S026, S044, S076, S077, S078, S079).
- 1994: The USAir preferred was marked down to market once the decline looked other than temporary, rather than carried at cost (S026, S077, S078).
- 1983: A realized loss on mortgage securities was booked and reported separately (S044, paraphrase).
- 1994-03: "Because all failures and faults deserve extra attention in annual reports" (S076)
- 1997: Unrealized gains "subject to market fluctuation, with no guaranty as to its ultimate full realization" (S079) are not money in hand.

### 12. Count the opportunity cost of capital stuck in a poor business
Sources: 3 (S042, S044, S048).
- 1981: Selling the Buffalo News would flatter results: "we could easily improve our consolidated operating earnings and the percentage return we earn on our shareholders' investment by selling the News" (S042)
- 1983: The cost of holding it was the return foregone: "we would have realized a substantial and compounded return if we had invested the money elsewhere" (S044)
- 2019-02: Any new idea must beat the best one already owned: "If we've got one thing we can do more of, we're not interested in anything that's not better than that." (S048)

### 13. Shorten sail before the storm, without forecasting it
Sources: 4 (S042, S044, S050, S051).
- 1980 to 1983: Mutual Savings sold its branches as rates rose, "motivated by a prudent concern for its shareholders and a desire to retain unquestioned financial strength" (S042); it "shortened sail to allow for hurricane conditions, not because a hurricane was clearly foreseen" (S044).
- 2021-02: "Generally, what people do is they have financial reserves so they have some options if trouble comes" (S050)
- 2022-02: Cash builds because nothing is cheap enough, not as a market call (S051, paraphrase).

### Selling mistakes he admits
- Monroe, decades before 2017: sold the stock at $15 on its way from $1 to $40 (S053).
- Ballard Petroleum, told 2022-04: sold at triple, then it doubled again (S009).
- Alibaba, 2021 to 2023: bought on leverage, sold half after it fell; "I regard Alibaba as one of the worst mistakes I ever made." (S052)
- BYD, 2021-02: no sell system at a nosebleed price and admitted loyalty (S050); a partial trim came about a year before 2023 (S025).
- The Buffalo News, 1977 to 1982: held through losses until a rival died, and later called the stewardship "at best, dubious" (S044).
- Exits in general, 2019-02: "So I'm no good at exits." (S048)

## Kill criteria this seat would write
Checkable facts that would end the thesis, in his style (inferred from S007, S020, S025, S038, S047, S053, S065, S071).
- The pay plan rewards a number (sales per employee, reported earnings, assets gathered, deals done) that staff can hit by harming customers or the balance sheet, and leadership responds to the first scandal by punishing staff rather than changing the plan.
- Reported earnings outrun cash for two or more years, or the company leans on EBITDA, adjusted earnings or option accounting to show growth.
- A rival sells the same thing at a much lower price, or a new technology does the job, and the incumbent's response is denial.
- Physical volume falls for several years while price increases hold revenue flat.
- The original reason for owning it no longer holds (for example, the newspaper will not come back), and the holder has quietly swapped in a new rationale.
- The thesis depended on a story (internet position, a founder's charm) and the business turns out to be an ordinary retailer, lender or commodity producer.
- Management issues stock for acquisitions, lets dilution from stock pay go unoffset, or buys back stock only when it is expensive.
- Leverage appears where a lender or counterparty could force a sale at the bottom tick.
- The position is outside the circle of competence once honestly re-examined: you cannot say what the business earns in ten years.

## Re-check triggers
Events and cadences he watches (inferred from S002, S020, S023, S028, S036, S046, S049, S051, S079).
- Any disconfirming fact, especially against a position you love or argued hard for; once a year, name the idea you destroyed.
- A first earnings or growth disappointment, which should open a review of the whole accounting history.
- A change of CEO, especially one who wants public relations, or a new incentive plan.
- A lower-priced entrant, a new technology, or an incumbent losing pricing power to a rival.
- A scandal or regulatory action, judged on whether the cause is fixable.
- Signs of a lollapalooza in the market: social proof, easy credit, short-term gambling and promoters selling new issues.
- Annual: compare price with your own intrinsic value estimate and say the gap plainly; separate cyclical from permanent decline; compare every holding with your best available alternative.
- A 50% fall in a holding: re-check the business, not the quote, and add if it is intact and you have the cash.
