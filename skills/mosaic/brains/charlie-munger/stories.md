# Stories: Charlie Munger

Built from the verified batch notes on his talks, Blue Chip Stamps and Wesco letters, Daily Journal meetings, op-eds and interviews (S001 to S083, 1978 to 2023). The case studies he returns to, most-used first, chosen for what they teach about inversion, incentives and misjudgment. His own losers and admitted errors get as much room as the winners. Figures are as he reported them at the time.

## 1. BYD: the venture bet he tried to talk out of its best move
Sources: 9 (S007, S022, S025, S045, S047, S049, S050, S052, S053).
Found by Li Lu and bought around 2008 for about $230 million, a Graham-style cheap stock that was also a bet on a founder, the eighth son of a peasant who started with a borrowed $300,000 (S025, S045). "I'd rather bet on the man who has some limit to his self-appraisal." (S053) He and Li Lu tried to talk the founder out of making cars and were wrong; it became one of Berkshire's best investments, and he credits "some accident in life" (S025, S052). The stock "did nothing for the first five years we held it. Last year it quintupled." (S050) Berkshire sold part about a year before 2023 at a much higher price, and at about 50 times earnings he called it "a very high price." (S052)
Lesson: an outlier can come from outside the usual method, so call it luck in part and do not build a system on it. Held too long out of affection: "we're likely to be a little too loyal." (S050)

## 2. The savings and loan disaster: a whole industry built on bad incentives
Sources: 9 (S042, S043, S044, S054, S056, S066, S067, S071, S072).
Mutual Savings borrowed short and lent long; he wrote that this structure "destroys shareholder wealth at startling speed whenever interest rates are rising rapidly" (S066). In 1980 it sold all its branches, which he described as "the institution shortened sail to allow for hurricane conditions, not because a hurricane was clearly foreseen" (S044). He then traced the industry's collapse as a chain of incentive errors: deposit insurance, decontrol, brokered deposits and lax accounting, which he called "the financial equivalent of distributing free machine guns in cocaine alley" (S071). He resigned from the trade league: "dominated by crooks and fools" (S056). His own share of blame: "we deserve our share of the beating because we were previously so passive in the presence of obvious error and evil" (S072)
Lesson: when insured, rewarded boldness meets weak accounting, expect bold operators to drive out cautious ones; reduce exposure before the storm can be forecast.

## 3. Everything dies: Dexter Shoes, Kodak, Precision Steel and the newspapers
Sources: 9 (S023, S025, S036, S038, S049, S051, S052, S053, S080).
Dexter Shoes died when imports came in at half the price: "no amount of managerial skill could protect us." (S053) Eastman was brilliant and Kodak still went bankrupt (S080). Precision Steel's volume fell to "less than half the annual volume that Precision Steel had sold thirty years earlier, when it was acquired by Wesco" (S038). Of newspapers: "The revenue goes away and the expenses remain and they're all dying." (S049) "Once I understood that better, it made me a better investor." (S025)
Lesson: invert the moat; ask what cheaper entrant or new technology kills this, because "when a really disruptive technology comes along, the incumbents screw up their reaction to it" (S025).

## 4. Wells Fargo: incentives that rotted a good bank
Sources: 8 (S024, S037, S045, S046, S047, S050, S065, S082).
Berkshire bought because Munger knew the lending officers were not normal bankers and would lose less than the market feared (S045); Wesco's 2007 to 2008 buying had timing that "could not have been much worse." (S037) Then the cross-selling scandal: quotas on poorly paid staff pushed products customers did not need (S024). "It never occurred to them that something like their own incentive system could be a mistake." (S065) Management came down on employees instead of changing the system, and reacted slowly to bad news (S047, S050). He did not sell: "I think Wells Fargo will end up better off for having made those mistakes." (S047) He was more lenient than Buffett: "I expect less out of bankers than he does." (S050)
Lesson: look first at the pay plan, then at how leaders respond to the first bad evidence. "Practically everybody makes those mistakes." (S047)

## 5. The Buffalo News: seven loss years and a bet he calls dubious
Sources: 7 (S007, S039, S040, S042, S043, S044, S053).
Bought in 1977 for about $34 million; a new Sunday edition brought an antitrust suit and injunctions, then years of losses (S039, S040, S044). He stated the risk plainly: "the ultimate security of the enterprise will almost surely remain in doubt for a very extended period" (S039) and "No matter what happens in the future in Buffalo we are about 100% sure to have an economic place lower than we would have occupied if we had not made our purchase." (S043) It worked only when the rival Courier Express failed in 1982. "We were never the weakest in the town. So we were betting that we'd be the survivor." (S053) His verdict: "Our stewardship may have been, at best, dubious in this instance." (S044)
Lesson: a good outcome does not prove a good decision; count the opportunity cost of the years without return (S044).

## 6. Freddie Mac: acting in scale on a simple thing
Sources: 6 (S026, S029, S071, S073, S078, S079).
Mutual Savings bought 7.2 million shares (28.8 million after splits) for about $72 million when only savings and loans could own them (S026, S078). A 1990 loan-loss scare cut the price 27%, which he likened to the 1963 salad-oil swindle that did not impair American Express (S073). It was sold in 2000 at $1.4 billion (S029). He drew the lesson in 1997: readiness to act in scale on something simple and logical dramatically improves a lifetime's results (S079, paraphrase).
Lesson: a legal or structural quirk that blocks other buyers can create a near-cinch; when you see one, buy a lot.

## 7. The bank stocks at the bottom tick
Sources: 6 (S022, S045, S048, S050, S052, S081).
During the foreclosure boom the Daily Journal printed more than 80% of foreclosure notices, "like being an undertaker during a plague year" (S022), and refused to raise prices on people losing homes (S048). It used the cash to buy bank stocks at the 2009 lows. He disclaims the timing, "the exact perfect timing was an accident" (S081), but not the readiness: "It wasn't luck that we had the money on hand when other people didn't and were willing to deploy it when other people were running for cover." (S045) By 2023 the gain was so large that a sale would hand over 40-some percent in tax (S052).
Lesson: you cannot time the bottom, but you can be the one with cash and nerve when others are forced out.

## 8. CORT: bought at the top of the boom
Sources: 6 (S028, S029, S031, S034, S036, S038).
Wesco bought the furniture-rental leader in February 2000 for about $384 million, when "its furniture rental business was rapidly growing, reflecting the strong U.S. economy, phenomenal business expansion and explosive growth of IPOs and the high-tech sector" (S036). Operating income collapsed after the dot-com bust and September 11. "Obviously, when we purchased CORT we were poor predictors of near-term industry-wide prospects" (S031) It recovered with small acquisitions at the bottom (S034), then in 2009 "CORT's business has been melting away faster than CORT can fix it." (S038)
Lesson: a business riding a boom shows boom earnings; ask what it earns when the tailwind stops.

## 9. USAir preferred: a credit bet dressed as safety
Sources: 6 (S016, S026, S027, S074, S077, S078).
Bought in 1989 alongside Berkshire. He called it "a preferred stock with a mandatory redemption." (S016), a credit instrument that went to the brink. Wesco wrote it down to market in 1994 (S077, S078), then it recovered and was sold at a gain after being called (S026, S027). He still calls the purchase a mistake (S016).
Lesson: a senior security in a bad industry is only as good as the industry; a lucky recovery does not make it a good decision.

## 10. New America Electric and the Montecito project: owning his own errors
Sources: 5 (S071, S072, S076, S077, S078).
Wesco bought 80% of an electrical equipment maker in 1988 from a friend; first-year results were "an embarrassment to us." (S072) It was sold at a loss in 1993. "The bad timing of Wesco in entering the electrical equipment field when it did was entirely the result of misjudgment by the writer" (S076) and "The writer caused Wesco's loss, not Glen Mitchel." (S076) Add a Montecito development and boom-time mortgages: "Wesco, under the writer's leadership, has managed to be clobbered in three different ways by the California real estate crash" (S077)
Lesson: name the decision-maker, and do not bury bad results; "all failures and faults deserve extra attention in annual reports" (S076).

## 11. Alibaba: charmed, levered and wrong
Sources: 4 (S025, S051, S052, S080).
He bought Alibaba for the Munger family and the Daily Journal, used a little leverage, and in 2022 defended it as a choice of strength per dollar while conceding "I don't think Alibaba is as entrenched as something like Apple and Alphabet." (S051) It fell, he sold half, and Jack Ma's clash with Beijing and new competition hurt it (S025, S080). "I got charmed with the idea of their position in the Chinese internet and I didn't stop to realize that it's still a goddamn retailer." (S025) "my worst trade was buying a block for the Munger family in Alibaba" (S080)
Lesson: liking an idea is a bias like any other; check what kind of business it really is. "I just got a little out of focus and that made me overestimate the future returns from Alibaba." (S025)

## 12. Mistakes of omission: Belridge, Walmart, Google and the 1970s miss
Sources: 4 (S002, S048, S063, S082).
He declined 1,500 more Belridge Oil shares because of deprival-superreaction, "which made me about $5.4 million poorer than I would have been had I then been psychologically acute." (S002) Berkshire's biggest errors were things not bought: "while mistakes of commission were common, almost all huge errors were in not making a purchase, including not purchasing Walmart stock when that was sure to work out enormously well." (S063) On Google, Berkshire could see from its own subsidiaries' ad results that it worked: "we weren't paying enough attention" (S082). And: "the Mungers would have twice the assets they now have if I hadn't made one mistake of omission back in nineteen 70s" (S048)
Lesson: the costliest misjudgment is often the bet not made on a near-cinch; refusing to pay up a little can cost a fortune.

## 13. The cost-plus mules and the Federal Express night shift
Sources: 4 (S001, S002, S023, S049).
Federal Express could not get its night shift done on time until it paid per shift instead of per hour (S001, S002). A contractor refused cost-plus jobs because even his mules learned to slow down under them (S023, S049); the Daily Journal refuses to bill software by the hour for the same reason. Xerox salesmen pushed an inferior machine because it paid higher commissions (S002).
Lesson: before judging people, read the pay rule; change the rule, not the sermon.

## 14. The Salomon general counsel
Sources: 4 (S005, S007, S016, S021).
When Gutfreund delayed reporting a bond trader's misconduct, the general counsel appealed to duty instead of the CEO's self-interest and lost his career with him (S005, S021, S016). "The correct persuasive technique in situations like that was given by Ben Franklin." (S021) Berkshire nearly lost its Salomon investment (S007).
Lesson: to persuade, appeal to interest, not reason; and expect bosses who delay bad news to compound it.

## 15. The few big chances: oil royalties, Monroe and Grandpa Ingham
Sources: 4 (S022, S045, S046, S053).
In 1962 he and Al Marshall bid at an oil-royalty auction attended only by lowball brokers; about $1,000 each paid $100,000 a year for fifty years (S022, S045). After reading Barron's for decades he found one cheap auto supplier and made about $80 million: "But that's a very funny way to be, to watch for 50 years and act once." (S053) His grandfather bought farms only in panics (S046).
Lesson: opportunities worth a big bet come a few times in a life; the job is to recognize one and act fast and large.

## 16. Hochschild-Kohn: a mistake reversed at closing
Sources: 3 (S007, S048, S065).
In 1966 Diversified Retailing bought a Baltimore department store and saw at closing that it had blundered: "We knew we'd made a big mistake and it was going to go to zero eventually." (S065) They sold at a minor loss, and the cash bought cheap stocks in a panic, including Berkshire's own: "So our stupidity made us rich, buying Hochschild-Kohn." (S065)
Lesson: admit a fresh error at once and redeploy; accept looking foolish to stop the bleeding.

## 17. Henry Singleton and Teledyne: incentives blindside a genius
Sources: 3 (S009, S046, S048).
Singleton was the most rational CEO he knew and bought back stock in gobs when it was cheap (S009). Yet his aggressive incentive plans produced cheating at a few of his subsidiaries at once: "he got a little aggressive in applying the incentives and he got blindsided." (S046) Near the end Singleton offered Teledyne for Berkshire stock; Munger refused to issue stock for a business he did not understand (S009), and Berkshire never owned Teledyne: "It's just one of many mistakes of omission." (S048)
Lesson: strong incentives work, which is exactly why they cause cheating somewhere; audit the pay plan even under brilliant management.

## 18. The 1973 to 1974 crash and the partnership
Sources: 3 (S008, S009, S046).
His partnership lost about half while the market fell about 40% (S046). He closed the fee partnership afterwards because he felt investors' pain: "I have enough of fiduciary gene that pained me greatly" (S008). Berkshire itself fell by half three times: "My Berkshire stock has gone down 50% three times in my lifetime." (S009)
Lesson: a concentrated investor must expect 50% drawdowns and hold capital that can sit through them.

## 19. Long-Term Capital and Niederhoffer: leverage on genius
Sources: 3 (S004, S019, S047).
LTCM "recently collapsed through overconfidence in its highly leveraged methods" (S019). Niederhoffer, who once gamed Harvard's course rules, was wiped out twice, the second time on volatility bets (S004, S047). "Why would you risk what you have and need in order to get what you don't have and don't need?" (S047)
Lesson: intelligence plus leverage plus overconfidence is a lollapalooza toward ruin.

## 20. Quant Tech: a fable of option accounting
Sources: 1 (S020).
A cash-rich, debt-free engineering firm is ruined when new officers use option accounting to inflate earnings; when a growth disappointment drops the stock by half, "This price decline, in turn, triggered a careful examination of Quant Tech's financial reporting practices" (S020). The rule-makers sided with the managers who paid them: "Whose bread I eat, his song I sing." (S020)
Lesson: one flawed accounting rule plus pay tied to reported earnings can corrupt a good institution; read reported earnings against the cash.
