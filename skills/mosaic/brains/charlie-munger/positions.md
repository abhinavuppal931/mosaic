# Positions: Charlie Munger

Built from the verified batch notes on his talks (S001 to S005, S014 to S021, 1986 to 2007), the Blue Chip Stamps and Wesco letters (S026 to S044, S066 to S079, 1978 to 2010), the Daily Journal meetings (S022 to S025, S045 to S053, 2016 to 2023), his op-eds and testimony (S054 to S064) and interviews (S006 to S009, S065, S080 to S083, 2000 to 2023). Each stance carries a date. The seat's own question comes first (how does this fail, whose incentives drive it, what are we fooling ourselves about), then the standard investor topics, most-used first. Where a view changed, both are shown and the newer one is marked. Moat quality is kept thin here; the Buffett seat owns it. These are dated public stances, not calls on today's securities.

## 1. How does this fail: inversion and avoiding standard stupidity
Sources: 9 (S003, S006, S014, S017, S023, S049, S065, S072, S080).
- 1986: Learn how to fail, then do the opposite. "Many hard problems are best solved when they are addressed backward." (S014) His inverted prescription for misery starts: "First, be unreliable. Do not faithfully do what you have engaged to do." (S014)
- 1986: Crowd folly and copied errors are the standard ways businesses die: "business failures through repetition of obvious mistakes made by predecessors, various forms of crowd folly, and so on." (S003)
- 1990: "it is remarkable how much long-term advantage people like us have gotten by trying to be consistently not stupid, instead of trying to be very intelligent." (S072)
- 1996: "it is not enough to think problems through forward. You must also think in reverse" (S017)
- 2019-05: "Some people collect stamps. I collect insanities and absurdities. And then I avoid them" (S065)
- 2020-02: As a wartime weather officer he asked what kills pilots (icing, fuel exhaustion) and cleared flights around those. "Just constantly invert. You don't think about what you want. You think about what you want to avoid." (S023) and "It works better to invert the problem." (S049)
- 2020-12: "I just try and avoid being stupid." (S006)
- 2023-11: Avoid the obvious standard failures (drink, drugs, ruinous risk) before seeking brilliance (S080, paraphrase).

## 2. Incentives at every level: managers, salesmen, advisers and yourself
Sources: 16 (S001, S002, S004, S005, S016, S020, S021, S024, S046, S047, S049, S051, S052, S065, S072, S082).
- 1994: The Federal Express night shift was fixed by paying per shift, not per hour. "what determines the behavior are incentives for the decision-maker." (S001)
- 1995 (revised 2005): "Never, ever, think about something else when you should be thinking about the power of incentives." (S002) Incentive-caused bias is sincere: the surgeon who removes healthy gall bladders believes he is helping (S002, paraphrase).
- 1996: Discount advice that pays the adviser. "You must have the confidence to override people with more credentials than you whose cognition is impaired by incentive-caused bias" (S016)
- 1990: "And who has ever seen a tout who didn't want his backer to make a lot of bets?" (S072)
- 2000-06: The worst blame goes to the rule-makers, while the rank and file commit "low-level cognitive error, much of it subconsciously caused by the standard incentive systems of the world" (S020)
- 2003: "If directors were significant shareholders who got a pay of zero, you'd be amazed what would happen to unfair compensation of corporate executives" (S004)
- 2007: "Incentives are too powerful a controller of human cognition and human behavior." (S005) He would not have lived under billable-hour quotas (S021).
- 2017-02: Even the smartest man he knew was blindsided. "It wasn't that Henry was trying to cheat the government, it's just that he got a little aggressive in applying the incentives and he got blindsided." (S046)
- 2018-02 to 2021-02: Wells Fargo had "incentive systems that were too strong in the wrong direction" (S047), with "a culture of cross-selling where the incentives on the poorly paid employees were too great to sell stuff that customers didn't really need" (S024). "It never occurred to them that something like their own incentive system could be a mistake." (S065) The CEO who inherited it was "not responsible for the crazy incentive system that created the trouble" (S082).
- 2019-05: The commission salesman at month end "would sell a blind man any goddamn thing to get the gross." (S065)
- 2020-02: People with a profit motive convince themselves they do good, so fix the system, not the people: "I think you have to change the incentives." (S049)
- 2022-02: "the people making the decisions care more about their own families than they care about the people whose money they're managing" (S051)
- 2023-02 (newest): "everybody is trying to take what they need and just rationalizing whether it's deserved or not." (S052)

## 3. Misjudgment in markets: bubbles, crowds and lollapalooza
Sources: 15 (S002, S006, S009, S015, S016, S045, S046, S048, S049, S050, S051, S058, S060, S071, S083).
- 1989: "the early phases look like many a speculative bubble which, in due course, was followed by a big bust" (S071)
- 1995: Social proof drives corporate fads. "If one oil company foolishly buys a mine, other oil companies often quickly join in buying mines." (S002)
- 1996: "it's essential that you beware of lollapalooza effects." (S016) In 2017 he defined them: "the Lollapalooza effects came when three or four of these tendencies were operating at once in the same situation" (S046)
- 2000-05: "I worry a little about prosperity that comes from constant pushing of the envelope of credit expansion." (S083)
- 2000-11: "It is an unfortunate fact that great and foolish excess can come into prices of common stocks in the aggregate." (S058) and "I suggest that when the financial scene starts reminding you of Sodom and Gomorrah, you should fear practical consequences even if you like to participate in what is going on." (S058)
- 2006: After the tech bubble, imitation "caused about $90 billion to rush into low-quality, imitative early-stage ventures" (S015)
- 2009-02: "Moderate booms and busts are inevitable in free-market capitalism." (S060)
- 2016-02: "the big busts hurt us more than the big booms help us" (S045)
- 2019-02: On 2008 lending: "When other people were making money in a disreputable stupid fashion, everybody piled in because they didn't want somebody else to be making money and they're not participating." (S048)
- 2020-02: The Nifty Fifty was worse than the tech leaders then: "Nifty Fifty was absolute dementia." (S049)
- 2020-12: "Nobody knows when bubbles are going to blow up" (S006)
- 2021-02: On SPACs and the 2021 frenzy: "Yes, I think it must end badly but I don't know when." (S050)
- 2022-02: "But when it's going to come and how bad it will be, I can't tell you." (S051)
- 2022-04: "The process of capitalism automatically speeds up in both booms and depressions, and it feeds on itself for awhile." (S009)

## 4. The too-hard pile and the edge of competence
Sources: 11 (S001, S006, S016, S045, S047, S048, S049, S050, S052, S053, S082).
- 1994: Staying out is a personal limit, not a law. "The fact that we don't think we're very good at it and have pretty well stayed out of it doesn't mean that it's irrational for you to do it." (S001)
- 1996: "why should it be easy to get rich?" (S016)
- 2016-02: "my attitude is that I have a circle of competence" (S045), and it excludes picking Silicon Valley winners.
- 2017-02: "You got to figure out what your competitive position is in what you're choosing." (S053)
- 2018-02: "I've done so well in life by just using organized common sense" (S047)
- 2019-02: "Everything was the 'too hard' pile, except for a few easy decisions which I make, promptly." (S048) and "I'm good at avoiding subjects which I'm not good at." (S048)
- 2019-05: "my judgment would not be as good on the internet as it is on Coca-Cola" (S082)
- 2020-02: "I don't play in a game where the other people are wise and I'm stupid. I look for a place where I'm wise and they're stupid." (S049) and "Well, if it's complicated technology I tend to leave it to others." (S049)
- 2020-12: "I put them on what I call my too hard pile and I just leave them there." (S006) The key defense is "knowing where you're competent and where you aren't, knowing the edge of your own competency." (S006)
- 2021-02: On hydrogen: "You've reached the limit of my circle of competence. I can't help you." (S050)
- 2023-02: On TSMC and chips: "What the hell do I know about semiconductors?" (S052)

## 5. Opportunity cost and how he values
Sources: 10 (S001, S004, S008, S017, S044, S045, S048, S053, S065, S083).
- 1983: Counted the cost of a slow purchase against the alternative: "we would have realized a substantial and compounded return if we had invested the money elsewhere" (S044)
- 1994: Graham's anchor was value to a private owner, buying at "something that was one third or less of sellout value" (S001)
- 1996: Sizes a business from first principles, then reverses the arithmetic to see what growth the price needs: Coke "is now worth about $125 billion and will have to increase its value at only 8 percent per year until 2034 to reach a value of $2 trillion." (S017)
- 2000-05: Rejects EVA's hurdle as "a cost of capital concept that makes no true economic sense" (S083)
- 2003: "opportunity cost is a superpower, to be used by all people who have any hope of getting the right answer." (S004)
- 2016-02: No single discount rate. "We don't use numeric formulas that way." (S045) and "obviously your opportunity cost should govern your own investment decision making" (S045)
- 2017-02: "We don't have any formulas that are exact or anything like that." (S053) With Li Lu's fund in hand, nothing else qualifies unless it beats it (S048): "If we've got one thing we can do more of, we're not interested in anything that's not better than that." (S048)
- 2017-11: "Everything I've ever done in business could be done with the simplest algebra and geometry" (S008)
- 2019-05: Getting more value than you pay for: "That will never go out of style." (S065)

## 6. Accounting quality, EBITDA and promotional numbers
Sources: 13 (S002, S004, S007, S009, S049, S053, S059, S062, S071, S072, S073, S079, S083).
- 1989: "it is child's play to make any bank or savings and loan association report high profits for a while, thereby rapidly augmenting reported net worth, by making loans" (S071)
- 1990: "Horrible results are obscured, and mediocre results are made to look fine." (S072)
- 1991: "The good figures substitute nicely in the mind for nonexistent underlying institutional good" (S073)
- 1995 (revised 2005): "Perhaps the most important of these antidotes is use of sound accounting theory and practice." (S002)
- 1997: Ignores noise: "We do not regard earnings changes from LIFO accounting adjustments, up or down, as material in predicting future earning power." (S079)
- 2000-05: "I hate with a passion Generally Accepted Accounting Principles as applied to derivatives" (S083)
- 2002-04: "In the long term, huge public benefits are to be gained, with almost no public dangers, from pessimistic accounting, while optimistic accounting is a public menace." (S059) and "Huge vested interests love misleading accounting." (S059)
- 2003: "It's an effective sales technique in America to put a foolish projection on a desk." (S004)
- 2011-07: "Tweakmore turned each glitch into an opportunity to boost profit." (S062)
- 2017-02: Master limited partnerships treat replacement capex as free cash: "There's a lot that goes on in finance that's dishonorable." (S053) The 1960s stock-for-acquisition game: "That was like a chain letter game. It was dishonorable." (S053)
- 2020-02: "I don't like when investment bankers talk about EBITDA which I would translate as bullshit earnings." (S049)
- 2022-04: On GE: "In the end, he was lying about what had been accomplished." (S009)
- 2023-10: "I've never liked John Malone's extreme manipulations." (S007)

## 7. Market timing, macro and the Fed
Sources: 13 (S004, S006, S007, S025, S026, S037, S045, S050, S051, S052, S058, S081, S083).
- 1997: "Well, we have often proved poor prognosticators." (S026)
- 2000-05: "Neither Warren nor I has any record of making large profits by guessing what the Federal Reserve is going to do or in which direction interest rates are going." (S083)
- 2000-11: "I have never taken a single course in economics, nor tried to make a single dollar, ever, from foreseeing macroeconomic changes." (S058)
- 2003: Too much macro, too little micro: "It's like trying to master medicine without knowing anatomy and chemistry." (S004)
- 2009-03: "We will not attempt to prognosticate the effects that Wesco will suffer or when the economy will recover" (S037)
- 2016-02: Scorned Greenspan's Ayn Rand faith that markets are always right (S045, paraphrase).
- 2019-02: "The same damn recipe done a different time gets a different result." (S081)
- 2020-12: "my opinion on that is no better than anybody else's" (S006)
- 2021-02: "I don't think we have any great gift at making macroeconomic predictions." (S050)
- 2022-02: "I figure that I want to swim as well as I can against the tides, I'm not trying to predict the tides." (S051)
- 2023-02: "I think I'm pretty good at long run expectations, but I don't think I'm good at short-term wobbles." (S052) On Powell: "he's about as good as we have any right to expect" (S025)
- 2023-10: Distrusts anyone sure of how the Fed should be run (S007, paraphrase).

## 8. Concentration versus diversification
Sources: 12 (S001, S007, S009, S016, S019, S046, S048, S050, S051, S065, S071, S083).
- 1989: "Through this practice of concentration of investments, better understanding is sought with respect to the few decisions made." (S071)
- 1994: "we're way less diversified. And I think our system is miles better." (S001)
- 1996: With a caveat on temperament: "I don't think there's a one-size-fits-all investment strategy that I can give you." (S016)
- 1998: "It can be a rational choice, in some situations, for a family or a foundation to remain 90 percent concentrated in one equity." (S019)
- 2000-05: "our investments are way more concentrated than is conventional in portfolio management" (S083)
- 2017-02: "The Mungers have three stocks." (S046) and "If you have one cinch, what else do you need in life?" (S046)
- 2019-02: "An idiot could diversify a portfolio! Or a computer for that matter." (S048)
- 2019-05: "Maybe once every two years we had a major opportunity." (S065)
- 2021-02: On owning 100 stocks: "I regard this as insanity. Absolute insanity." (S050) and "I'm way more comfortable owning two or three stocks which I think I know something about and where I think I have an advantage." (S050)
- 2022-02: "I think the great lesson from the Mungers is you don't need all this damn diversification." (S051)
- 2022-04: "the best of them don't expect to find 10 or 20 or 30. They find one or two." (S009)
- 2023-10: "Most people don't teach that in business school. It's insane." (S007)

## 9. Index funds, active managers and fees
Sources: 12 (S001, S008, S009, S019, S025, S046, S047, S048, S049, S051, S053, S058).
- 1994: "on a net basis, the whole investment management business together gives no value added to all buyers combined." (S001)
- 1998: "Indexing can't work well forever if almost everybody turns to it. But it will work all right for a long time." (S019)
- 2000-11: Fund buyers chase performance: "the typical mutual fund investor gained at 7.25% per year in a 15-year period when the average stock fund gained at 12.8% per year" (S058)
- 2017-02: "basically 95 percent of the people have almost no chance of beating it over time" (S046) He likes the Buffett partnership fee: "I did copy the Buffett formula more or less, and I do think it's fair" (S046) Cloning good small managers is fine: "I like cloning." (S053)
- 2017-11: Left the fee partnership after 1973 to 1974: "I have enough of fiduciary gene that pained me greatly" (S008)
- 2018-02: "And of course the high fees will just kill you." (S047) Big staffs over 50 names: "It's delusional. It's not good to face the world in a delusional way." (S047)
- 2019-02: On managers who lag: "Well it's a very simple answer, they're handling it with denial." (S048)
- 2020-02: "I think this indexing thing is going to run and run and run" (S049)
- 2022-02: "We have a new bunch of emperors, and they're the people who vote the shares in the index funds." (S051)
- 2022-04: "95% of the big-time national investing, they're closet indexers." (S009) and "if you get too much indexing, it'll have no chance of working. It'll be a Nifty 50 all over again." (S009)
- 2023-02 (newest): "it's not difficult to just buy an index fund and sit on your ass. That's the great default position." (S025) and "It's a deep moral depravity if some widow comes to you with $500,000 and you charge her one point a year when you could put her in the indexes" (S025)

## 10. Inflation, money printing and government debt
Sources: 12 (S006, S009, S017, S025, S042, S044, S047, S048, S051, S052, S081, S082).
- 1981: "Inflation is a very effective form of indirect taxation on capital represented by holdings of common stock." (S042)
- 1983: "we think the likelihood of future inflation remains high in the United States" (S044)
- 1996: Expects the dollar to depreciate slowly over generations, offset by rising real purchasing power (S017, paraphrase).
- 2018-02: "Of course I'm concerned about the rising level of government debt." (S047)
- 2019-02: "eventually if you try and solve all your problems by printing money there'll be some disaster" (S048) He admitted surprise: "I was surprised when central banks started printing money" (S081) and "nobody knows how much of this money printing we can do" (S081)
- 2019-05: On claims that federal debt does not matter: "if you believe that, you believe in the tooth fairy" (S082)
- 2020-12: "Nobody has gotten by with the kind of money printing we're doing now for a very extended period without some trouble." (S006)
- 2022-02 (softened): "And we're closer to terrible trouble than we've been in the past, but it may still be a long way off." (S051) and, on Japan, "I think we also know that some of our earlier fears were overblown." (S051)
- 2022-04: "a bunch of democratically-elected politicians will eventually print too much money." (S009)
- 2023-02 (newest): "more inflation over the next 100 years is inevitable" (S052) "That's one of the reasons the Daily Journal owns common stocks instead of government bonds." (S025)

## 11. Leverage and margin
Sources: 11 (S007, S015, S019, S025, S044, S047, S051, S053, S065, S071, S080).
- Older view, 1983 to 2019: borrow only for flexibility, never so a lender can force a sale.
- 1983: "We ordinarily have reservations concerning financial leverage" (S044)
- 1989: "Wesco's practice has been to do a certain amount of long-term borrowing in advance of specific need, in order to have maximum financial flexibility to face both hazards and opportunities." (S071)
- 1998: LTCM "recently collapsed through overconfidence in its highly leveraged methods" (S019) LBO funds are margin buying with two layers of fees (S019, S015, paraphrase).
- 2017-02: "Why do you want to strain and (feel like you) have more danger when you're already filthy rich?" (S053)
- 2018-02: "the person whose giving you credit can wipe you out at the bottom tick just because he feels nervous" (S047) and "Why would you risk what you have and need in order to get what you don't have and don't need?" (S047)
- 2019-05: "Even though you're very ambitious and you grab your opportunities and all that, you shouldn't go crazy." (S065)
- Newer view, 2022 to 2023 (marked newer): a little leverage on a rare, ridiculously good opportunity is acceptable.
- 2022-02: "we don't mind a tiny little bit of margin debt." (S051)
- 2023-02: "recently I did use a little bit of leverage here and in another place because the opportunities were so ridiculously good that I thought it was desirable to do that" (S025) and "You're right, it's unusual for us, but we did find a few things." (S025) The Alibaba block bought partly on leverage became his worst trade (S025, S080).
- 2023-10 and 2023-11 (newest): In hindsight Berkshire was too cautious for its trusting shareholders. "If we used a little bit more leverage throughout, we'd have three times as much now" (S007) and "If Warren and I had owned Berkshire without any shareholders that we knew, we would've made more." (S080) He still rejects quant-style intraday leverage: "which gives them this big peak leverage risk, which I would not run myself." (S007)

## 12. Banks and credit
Sources: 11 (S002, S006, S037, S045, S046, S047, S048, S050, S066, S071, S073).
- 1984: Borrowing short to lend long "destroys shareholder wealth at startling speed whenever interest rates are rising rapidly, even when the credit quality of mortgagors or other borrowers is excellent." (S066)
- 1989: Deregulated, insured thrifts were "the financial equivalent of distributing free machine guns in cocaine alley" (S071)
- 1991: Accrual accounting on loans "turns many a banker into a human version of one of B. F. Skinner's pigeons" (S073)
- 1995: The two worst loan classes: "ninety-five percent-of-value construction loans to any kind of real estate developer" (S002) and any construction loan on a hotel.
- 2009-03: On Wells Fargo and US Bancorp bought from late 2007: "The timing of our recent investments could not have been much worse." (S037)
- 2016-02: Bank of America "just got pounded so hard that it was selling for less than it was worth. Way less." (S045)
- 2017-02: Irish banks: "we went crazy when we trusted their damn statements and it was a mistake" (S046)
- 2018-02: "the temptations to do something stupid are way greater in banking than they are in most businesses" (S047)
- 2019-02: "All intelligent investors worry about banks because banks present temptations to their managers to do dumb things." (S048)
- 2020-12: "I like banking when they're trying to avoid losses prudently." (S006)
- 2021-02: "The trouble with banking is that we have more banks than we have bankers." (S050) and "There's a lot of temptation to do dumb things which will make the earnings next quarter go up but are bad for the long term." (S050)

## 13. Private equity, venture capital, SPACs and IPOs
Sources: 11 (S007, S008, S019, S023, S045, S048, S049, S050, S051, S065, S082).
- 1998: "an LBO fund is a better way of buying equivalents of marketable equities on margin" (S019)
- 2016-02: Preference clauses that put new money ahead of old: "It's a disgusting, tricky, dishonorable thing to do." (S045)
- 2017-11: Buying a business whose founder leaves, then recruiting: "that is a tough way to make a buck, and I don't like it" (S008)
- 2019-02: "There is no business so lousy that it can't get a wonderful projection." (S048)
- 2019-05: Private credit: "It's not got a whole lot of safety shock-absorbers in it." (S065) On money-losing IPOs: "I don't have to invest in everything I'm interested in." (S082)
- 2020-02: "It's what I call fee-driven buying, much of it, where people are buying things to get the fees." (S023) "Sequoia had such a wonderful record because it kept itself small" (S049)
- 2021-02: On SPACs: "It's just that the investment banking profession will sell shit as long as shit can be sold." (S050)
- 2022-02: "Private equity buys things so they can have more fees by having more things under management." (S051)
- 2023-10: "Some of the deals get so hot and you have to decide so quickly. You're all just sort of gambling." (S007)

## 14. Gambling, speculation and short-term trading
Sources: 11 (S007, S009, S016, S045, S046, S048, S050, S051, S052, S055, S065).
- 1984: "The practice of playing the stock market for relatively short-term gains has now reached such a high level that it tends to give a bad name to capitalism" (S055)
- 1996: "I don't like legalized gambling." (S016)
- 2016-02: "the public market that operates every day with transactions is an ideal casino" (S045)
- 2017-02: On high-frequency traders: "I always say that those people have all the social utility of a bunch of rats in a granary." (S046)
- 2019-02: Teaching novices to trade is "roughly equivalent to trying to induce a bunch of young people to start off on heroin." (S048)
- 2019-05: "I wouldn't call a man who uses computer science to sift vast amounts of data for correlation, and then starts trading the correlation and if it works, keeps going, and if it doesn't, stops, I wouldn't call him an investor." (S065)
- 2021-02: "Commission-free trading is not free." (S050) On GameStop: "And it's really stupid to have a culture that encourages as much gambling in stocks by people who have the mindset of race track betters." (S050)
- 2022-02: "If I were the dictator of the world, I would have some kind of a tax on short term gains that made the stock market very much less liquid" (S051)
- 2022-04: "It's an absolutely insane thing for the country." (S009)
- 2023-02: Small friendly bets are fine: "I'm all in favor betting with the odds." (S052)
- 2023-10: "I would have a tax on short-term gains with no offset for losses on anything." (S007)

## 15. Management, incentives at the top, and culture

- 1994: "So management matters, too." (S001)
- 1995 (revised 2005): Distrust the polished interview; he thought HP erred in picking a CEO on a dynamic presentation (S002, paraphrase). In 2017-11: "it's a vastly stupid type of overconfidence" (S008)

- 2021-02: Costco and BYD built "a strong culture of fanaticism about cost, quality, efficiency, and honor." (S024) Delegate "just short of total abdication" (S050, quoting Tom Murphy).
- 2023-02: "If you're a lousy manager, you really need a great business." (S025)
- 2023-10: A new CEO who wants public relations can spoil a good company. "So no matter how good the business is, it will be phony." (S007)

## 16. What price to pay and multiples
Sources: 10 (S001, S007, S016, S024, S025, S051, S052, S053, S076, S078).
- 1994: Quality can carry a full price. "if a business earns 18% on capital over 20 or 30 years, even if you pay an expensive looking price, you'll end up with a fine result." (S001) But the Nifty Fifty showed the limit: "Thus, a large investment disaster resulted from too high prices." (S001)
- 1994 and 1996: Published the gap on his own stock. Wesco sold about 30% above intrinsic value in 1994 (S076) and "This comparison indicates that Wesco stock was then selling about 22% above intrinsic value." (S078)
- 1996: On Berkshire: "we wouldn't buy it or recommend that our friends buy it at the prices then prevailing." (S016)
- 2017-02: Apple was bought for a modest edge, not a cinch: "We would have never bought Apple stock in the old days." (S053)
- 2021-02: "I think everybody is willing to hold stocks at higher price-earnings multiples when interest rates are as low as they are now." (S024)
- 2022-02: "I can't bring myself with my habits to pay these big prices, but I never even think about selling a share of Costco just because it's selling at a high price." (S051)
- 2023-02: "if it's really a great business, it's at least 25x earnings and maybe 30x or 35x or something" (S025) "The trouble with Costco is it's 40x earnings." (S052) "BYD is talking about 50x earnings. That is a very high price." (S052)
- 2023-10: Apple "got to about 10 times earnings when Warren bought in." (S007)

## 17. Holding period, turnover and taxes
Sources: 10 (S001, S007, S019, S037, S046, S050, S052, S053, S055, S065).
- 1984: Proposed five years as the line between investing and trading (S055, paraphrase).
- 1994: "if you can find some fairly-priced great company and buy it and sit, that tends to work out very, very well indeed" (S001) But "trying to minimize taxes too much is one of the great standard causes of really dumb mistakes." (S001)
- 1998: Berkshire runs "marketable equity portfolios with virtually zero turnover and with only a very few stocks chosen" (S019)
- 2009-03: "Most equity investments are expected to be held for long periods of time" (S037)
