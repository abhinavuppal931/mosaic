# Frameworks: Charlie Munger

Built from 79 free sources, 1977 to 2023: Blue Chip Stamps and Wesco letters, speeches (including the free Stripe Press online edition of Poor Charlie's Almanack, which is in the corpus and quoted from there), Daily Journal meeting transcripts, interviews, op-eds and one House testimony. This is the inversion, incentives and misjudgment seat, not a second Buffett: the six tools this seat leads with come first (inversion, the misjudgment checklist and lollapalooza, incentives at every level, the too-hard pile, opportunity cost against the best idea, and avoiding standard stupidity), then the rest by number of sources. Moat and business-quality material is kept thin on purpose. The scorecard dimension is **Folly check**: every scoring line below is guidance drawn from his own thresholds and examples, not a scorecard he published. 10 means the decision survives inversion, the incentive map and the misjudgment checklist; 0 means the decision is itself a standard folly.


## 1. Inversion ("Invert, always invert")

- **What it is:** solve a hard problem backward. Ask what would make the thing fail, ruin it or kill it, avoid exactly that, then go back to the forward question and alternate. He borrows the line from the mathematician Jacobi and uses it everywhere: as a wartime weather forecaster he asked how he could kill pilots (icing, running out of fuel) and avoided those; to fix India he would first ask how to hurt India; to guarantee a happy life he lists Carson's prescriptions for misery and does the opposite; the bridge declarer asks what could cause too many losers; the Coca-Cola thought experiment ends with a reality check by reverse (four things that would wreck the brand).
- **Parts:** (1) state what you want; (2) flip it: what would guarantee failure here, for the business, for the thesis and for your own behavior; (3) list how this kind of thing has failed before, from industry history and other people's disasters, since there is little originality in common disasters (S003); (4) test the case against each failure path; (5) avoid what you cannot rule out; (6) only then argue the forward case.
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** first, on every buy, add or hold. Write the position's obituary before its pitch. For a hold, ask what kills this business (technology, a customer that is half the revenue as with stamps in 1981 (S043), leverage, a management that changes what works). Use industry history as the base rate: for a daily paper without a Sunday edition, the record said failure every time (S039).
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = the failure paths are written down, each is absent or priced in, and the historical base rate for this kind of company is known and acceptable; 5 = a strong forward case with a partial failure list; 0 = nobody has asked how it fails, or industry history says the outcome is reliably bad and the thesis ignores it.
- **Quote:** "Just constantly invert. You don't think about what you want. You think about what you want to avoid." (S023) Also: "What could possibly go wrong that could cause me to have too many losers?" (S016) and "It is in the nature of things, as Jacobi knew, that many hard problems are best solved only when they are addressed backward." (S003) The rustic's version: "I wish I knew where I was going to die, and then I'd never go there." (S003)
- **Sources:** 18 (S002, S003, S004, S005, S007, S008, S014, S015, S016, S017, S018, S021, S023, S039, S049, S065, S080, S081).

## 2. The psychology of human misjudgment, run as a checklist

- **What it is:** a list of about 25 standard causes of bad judgment, used literally the way a pilot uses a pre-flight checklist. The list in the 1995 revised talk: reward and punishment superresponse, liking and loving, disliking and hating, doubt-avoidance, inconsistency-avoidance, curiosity, Kantian fairness, envy and jealousy, reciprocation, influence from mere association, simple pain-avoiding denial, excessive self-regard, overoptimism, deprival superreaction, social proof, contrast misreaction, stress, availability misweighing, use-it-or-lose-it, drug misinfluence, senescence, authority misinfluence, twaddle, reason-respecting, and lollapalooza (S002). Before that list he describes a two-track analysis: first the rational interests and real probabilities, then the subconscious forces pushing the conclusion (S001). By 2023 he named denial as the single biggest cause (see entry 18).
- **Parts:** (1) rational track: what are the real interests and odds; (2) psychological track: run every tendency down the list; (3) do it on yourself first, then on management, then on the market crowd; (4) when an outcome looks strange, run the list until one tendency explains it (the slot machine paying out more near misses, S004; the McDonnell Douglas evacuation test, S002).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** on the user before the stock. Common hits: commitment to a stated thesis, deprival superreaction to a paper loss or to selling below cost, liking a charismatic CEO, social proof from famous holders, availability of a vivid story, overoptimism, envy of someone else's gains, authority of a credentialed bull.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = both tracks run, and the case stands without any tendency doing the work; 5 = one or two tendencies plausibly at work (anchored to the buy price, fond of the founder) but the evidence would hold without them; 0 = the case only makes sense through the tendencies (bought because it rose and friends own it, refuses to sell because of the loss).
- **Quote:** "First, what are the factors that really govern the interests involved, rationally considered? And second, what are the subconscious influences where the brain at a subconscious level is automatically doing these things" (S001) Also: "No pilot takes off without going through his checklist" (S016) and "If you know the psychological factors, if you've got them on a checklist in your head, you just run down the factors, and, boom!, you get to one that must explain this occurrence." (S004)
- **Limit he states:** "It also demonstrates that being aware of psychological ploys is not a perfect defense." (S002)
- **Sources:** 16 (S001, S002, S004, S016, S017, S018, S019, S024, S046, S050, S052, S053, S065, S073, S082, S083).

## 3. Lollapalooza effects

- **What it is:** extreme outcomes come when three or four tendencies push the same way at once. The result is nonlinear and can make you rich or kill you. Negative cases: the Milgram experiment (at least six principles), cults, bubbles, the savings and loan collapse. Positive cases: Coca-Cola's brand as a combination of conditioned reflexes, social proof and availability (S017), and Buffett's lifelong narrowing of attention, which he calls a lollapalooza (S063). Mixing forces that explode together is the chemistry analogy (S073).
- **Parts:** (1) list the forces acting on the price or the business; (2) count how many point the same way; (3) if several reinforce each other in demand for the product, that is a durable advantage worth documenting; (4) if several reinforce each other in demand for the stock (social proof, reward, envy, easy money), expect an overshoot both ways.
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** on any stock or asset that has risen fast with a crowd behind it, and on any business whose success looks too good to be one cause.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = no multi-force mania in the price, and any lollapalooza in the business itself is identified force by force; 5 = one or two crowd forces at work in the price; 0 = the price is driven by three or four tendencies at once (social proof, envy, reward superresponse, easy credit), as in the manias he named.
- **Quote:** "the Lollapalooza effects came when three or four of these tendencies were operating at once in the same situation" (S046) Also: "really big effects, lollapalooza effects, will often come only from large combinations of factors." (S017) and "it's essential that you beware of lollapalooza effects." (S016)
- **Sources:** 7 (S002, S004, S016, S017, S046, S063, S073).

## 4. The superpower of incentives (who is paid for what)

- **What it is:** behavior follows the pay of the decision-maker, and every human system gets gamed. He says he always underestimated this even while ranking himself in the top five percent at understanding it. His cases: Wells Fargo cross-selling quotas on poorly paid staff (S046, S050, S065), cost-plus contracts where even the mules go to hell, so the Daily Journal collects only when its software works (S023, S049), savings and loan managers paid for size and recent numbers with little downside (S068), Gresham's law of insured lending (S071), the Quant Tech fable of option-fed earnings (S020), and the Wantmore parable of a whole credit chain rewarded for the same scheme (S062).
- **Parts:** (1) for each actor (management, sales force, lenders, brokers, regulators, counterparties) write down what they are paid for; (2) ask what that pay produces if pushed hard; (3) ask what can be faked and whether faking is rewarded; (4) ask who bears the consequences of each decision; (5) expect the worst practice in an industry to spread when it is rewarded (bad lending drives out good); (6) to fix behavior, change the incentive, not the speech (S049).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** on compensation plans, sales cultures with quotas, lenders and insurers, any industry paid on volume or on reported numbers, and on any regulatory or competitive promise that depends on people resisting a large reward.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = pay tied to long-term owner results, nothing easily faked is rewarded, decision-makers bear the consequences; 5 = mixed (quotas with controls, options partly offset by buybacks); 0 = rewards for volume or reported figures that can be faked while others bear the downside (cost-plus, brokered deposits, quotas on poorly paid staff).
- **Quote:** "Never, ever, think about something else when you should be thinking about the power of incentives." (S002) Also: "When you have tough incentives that are driving everybody with quotas, of course you'll have scandals." (S065) and "Bad lending drives out good." (S071) On goals: "Low goals do cause lower performance and high goals increase the percentage of cheating." (S083)
- **Sources:** 18 (S001, S002, S004, S009, S016, S020, S023, S046, S049, S050, S054, S062, S065, S067, S068, S071, S073, S083).

## 5. Incentive-caused bias in advice, and fee-driven buying

- **What it is:** a professional drifts, without noticing, to the conclusion that what is good for him is good for the client. His three antidotes (S002): fear advice that is especially good for the advisor; learn the basics of the advisor's trade; double check, disbelieve or replace. Find a third, unbiased source when two interested ones disagree (S016). Separate Planck knowledge (people who paid their dues) from chauffeur knowledge (people who learned to recite the talk) (S005, S021). In markets this shows up as fee-driven buying: private equity paying high prices to collect fees, funds raising bigger funds (S049, S051), and products created by professionals and hawked hard (S048).
- **Parts:** (1) who is telling you this, and how are they paid if you act; (2) is the source Planck or chauffeur knowledge; (3) can you check the claim yourself with elementary tools; (4) discount accordingly, or find an unconflicted third source; (5) any product with a big commission and a thick prospectus is a no.
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** whenever the thesis came from a broker, a banker's deal book, a company promoter, a fund that charges on assets, or a structured product. He did not read bankers' projections at all (S004).
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = thesis built from the user's own work or from sources with no stake in the answer; 5 = conflicted sources used but discounted and checked; 0 = the thesis is a pitch from someone paid on the transaction.
- **Quote:** "especially fear professional advice when it is especially good for the advisor" (S002) Also: "any time anybody offers you anything with a big commission and a 200-page prospectus, don't buy it." (S001), "His cognition will often be impaired, for your purposes, by financial incentives different from yours." (S017) and "It's what I call fee-driven buying, much of it, where people are buying things to get the fees." (S049)
- **Sources:** 14 (S001, S002, S004, S005, S016, S017, S018, S019, S021, S025, S048, S049, S051, S052).

## 6. Agency costs and skin in the game (owner versus careerist)

- **What it is:** hired people care more about their own careers than about the firm, so judge a strategy as an owner would, and prefer structures where decision-makers bear the consequences. Careerists deny a hopeless situation that an owner sees at once (General Motors, S008). The best student answer to his shoe factory test was to sell promptly because hired help could not fix it (S018). He paid Daily Journal staff with shares he gave from his own holding, copied from BYD's founder, so the company issued nothing (S025, S052). He calls a director who needs a $300,000 fee not independent (S051). LBO general partners risk little of their own money yet share in gains, so they bid high (S072); the operator who keeps founders at 40 percent aligns them (S053).
- **Parts:** (1) what share of their own wealth do the deciders hold; (2) where does stock pay come from, and is the dilution bought back; (3) are directors dependent on their fees; (4) does any party gain when the shareholder loses; (5) do managers stay long enough to bear the consequences (S063).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** on governance, stock compensation, acquisitive managements, and any fund or partner the user is relying on.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = owner-operators with real wealth at stake, stock pay offset by buybacks or funded by insiders, directors who do not need the fee; 5 = hired managers with some ownership and sane pay; 0 = managers or partners who gain when the owner loses (fees on size, unhedged dilution, deal-hungry executives with little stake).
- **Quote:** "If you're working for somebody else, the truth of the matter is you care more about yourself and your future and your family than you care about the telephone company you're working for." (S051) Also: "requiring that systems be organized, to the extent feasible, so that decision-makers, not others, bear consequences of decisions" (S073) and "But we always buy in enough stock in Costco to pay for the stock we're issuing." (S052) On fees: "he sucked his living out of his own capital for ten long years, because that's what a good money manager should be cheerfully willing to do" (S047)
- **Limit he states:** his own cross-incentive idea for two Berkshire managers did not change their behavior (S053).
- **Sources:** 12 (S008, S018, S025, S042, S047, S051, S052, S053, S063, S072, S073, S078).

## 7. The too-hard pile and the edge of competence

- **What it is:** almost everything goes in the too-hard pile; only a few easy decisions get made, and promptly. Competence means knowing where it ends. Play only where you are wise and the other side is not, and stay out of games where others have a natural edge in their strong suit. A fixable disadvantage gets fixed; an unfixable one gets lived without (S024). He does not study what he cannot buy (S007). His own pile, by date: high technology and Apple (1996, S016), Silicon Valley winners (2016, S045), payment systems ten years out (2017, S046), complicated technology, drug pipelines and crypto (2020, S023, S049), autos (2023, S007), hydrogen (2021, S050).
- **Parts:** (1) can I predict what happens with a high degree of accuracy (S082); (2) where exactly does my knowledge stop; (3) who is on the other side, and do they know more; (4) if any answer fails, the decision goes in the pile with no further work.
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** before valuation. It is the seat's abstain rule: a decision outside the circle gets TOO HARD, not a weak yes.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = the user can explain the business, its ten-year failure modes and the limit of their own knowledge; 5 = understands the business but not one decisive variable (a technology shift, a regulator); 0 = cannot say what happens in ten years, or is competing with specialists in their strong suit. Score 0 here means abstain.
- **Quote:** "I put them on what I call my too hard pile and I just leave them there." (S006) Also: "I don't play in a game where the other people are wise and I'm stupid. I look for a place where I'm wise and they're stupid." (S023) and "It's hardly a competence if you don't know the edge of it." (S048) His boyhood version: "I decided to stay the hell out of businesses where I would compete with people like Eddie Davis, Jr. and Eddie Davis, Sr. in their strong suit." (S080)
- **Sources:** 15 (S001, S006, S007, S016, S023, S024, S025, S045, S047, S048, S049, S050, S053, S080, S082).

## 8. Opportunity cost against your best idea

- **What it is:** every new idea is measured against the best thing you already own or can buy more of, not against cash or a textbook discount rate. He calls opportunity cost, with incentives, a superpower (S004). A bad purchase costs the return forgone on the money, not only its reported losses: the Buffalo Evening News money would have been about $70 million of other assets earning over $10 million a year (S043, S044). When Freddie Mac was a better business than Mutual Savings, capital moved to it (S074). With Li Lu's fund available to add to, nothing worse than that gets considered (S048). Underneath sits his definition of all investing: get more value than you pay (S006, S023, S050, S065).
- **Parts:** (1) name the current best idea, the one you would add to; (2) does the candidate beat it on both expected return and certainty; (3) if not, add to the best idea instead; (4) for a holding, ask what the capital would earn elsewhere; (5) no single formula or discount rate, and different businesses get different treatment (S045).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** on every buy, and on every hold that has drifted to mediocre. It is also the swap test for selling.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = the candidate clearly beats the user's best existing alternative; 5 = roughly equal, so the decision rests on comfort or diversification; 0 = it is worse than what the user already owns and could add to, or it is kept only because it is already owned.
- **Quote:** "opportunity cost is a superpower, to be used by all people who have any hope of getting the right answer." (S004) Also: "If we've got one thing we can do more of, we're not interested in anything that's not better than that." (S048) and "obviously your opportunity cost should govern your own investment decision making" (S045)
- **Dated stance:** 2017, with too much money for old bargains: "if you've got the money and you have to put it somewhere and you can't find what you use to like, you have to put it with what's best available." (S053) 2022, on China: "we got more strength per dollar invested in China" (S051). Newer, 2023: "I regard Alibaba as one of the worst mistakes I ever made." (S052) He said he sold half and that the thesis was wrong because it is still a retailer (S025).
- **Sources:** 8 (S004, S043, S044, S045, S048, S051, S053, S074).

## 9. Consistently not stupid: the asininity checklist

- **What it is:** his main method in practice. Collect the standard ways people fail, keep them as a checklist, and avoid everything on it; avoidance is his general method (S048). The advantage comes from being consistently not stupid rather than very intelligent (S072). The list he names: crooks, crazies, egomaniacs, resentful and self-pitying people, chain-letter operators who must keep expanding to look healthy, drunks, liars, price gougers, bubbles such as bitcoin, big dangerous things that pull you in like a whirlpool (S008, S053, S081). Personal items: envy, resentment, sloth, unreliability, intense ideology (S005, S021).
- **Parts:** (1) keep a written list of asininities from history and from others' disasters; (2) check the decision and the people against it; (3) if one item is clearly present, decline without further analysis; (4) add every new disaster you see to the list.
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** as the final gate before acting, and on management (chain-letter growth, promoters, people who puff).
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = no item on the list is present; 5 = one manageable item (some leverage, a promotional CEO attached to a sound business); 0 = a known asininity is present (chain-letter growth, gambling against the odds, buying a bubble on credit, partnering with people who lie).
- **Quote:** "Some people collect stamps. I collect insanities and absurdities. And then I avoid them" (S065) Also: "my game in life was always to avoid all standard ways of failing" (S080), "I don't like chain-letter operators and I don't like drunks. I don't like people who puff and lie" (S053) and "It's not that we're so smart. It's that we stayed sane." (S023)
- **Sources:** 11 (S006, S008, S023, S025, S048, S052, S053, S065, S072, S080, S081).

## 10. Margin of safety by engineering: a bad day must not kill you

- **What it is:** run finance the way engineers build bridges, with a safety factor against the case you cannot foresee. He applied it to thrift design (S067, S068), to the sheepkeeper's question of how much margin to leave when you do not know what the herd can stand (S071), to super-cat reinsurance written only from a Fort Knox balance sheet (S026, S076, S078), to insurance with a maximum loss on every policy (S083), and to his own portfolio: hold liquidity, avoid any position where a nervous lender can force a sale (S037, S047), and be ready for a 50 percent fall (S046).
- **Parts:** (1) what is the worst plausible case, not the expected one; (2) can anyone force a sale at the bottom; (3) is every exposure capped; (4) are you risking what you have and need for what you do not need (S047, S073); (5) prepare for extremes rather than predicting them (S083).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** on sizing, margin, options and on companies whose balance sheets borrow short and lend long.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = no leverage that can force a sale, every exposure capped, the user could sit through a 50 percent drop; 5 = modest leverage on a clean, cash-producing asset (the kind he accepted for Precision Steel, S043); 0 = a margin call, a covenant or a short-funded book can end the position at the bottom tick.
- **Quote:** "Do not live your life in such a fashion that a bad day can kill you." (S080) Also: "conduct your life so that you can handle a 50 percent decline with aplomb and grace" (S046) and "When you don't know for sure what the sheep can stand, how much safety margin do you leave before you set the shears, shear the whole herd, and send it forth to fare as it will?" (S071)
- **Changed over time (leverage):** 1983: "We ordinarily have reservations concerning financial leverage" (S044). 2018: "the person whose giving you credit can wipe you out at the bottom tick just because he feels nervous" (S047). Newer, 2023: "recently I did use a little bit of leverage here and in another place because the opportunities were so ridiculously good that I thought it was desirable to do that" (S025), and in October 2023: "If we used a little bit more leverage throughout, we'd have three times as much now" (S007). He still told most people to avoid it (S025).
- **Sources:** 15 (S026, S037, S043, S044, S046, S047, S066, S067, S068, S071, S073, S076, S078, S080, S083).

## 11. Darwin's disconfirmation habit: destroy your best-loved ideas

- **What it is:** give the most weight to evidence against the ideas you love most, and reward yourself for dropping a dumb one. Most people process new information so the first conclusion survives (S014). Saying an idea out loud pounds it in rather than enlarging it, which is why Capital Guardian's best-ideas fund failed three times: people are most committed to what they argue hardest (S053). He quotes Feynman on not fooling yourself (S019) and says he rubs his own nose in his own mistakes (S006, S025).
- **Parts:** (1) write the thesis down; (2) list the evidence that would kill it, and go look for it first; (3) weigh it more heavily the better you like the thesis; (4) notice where you have argued the case in public, and discount for it; (5) once a year, name an idea you destroyed.
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** on every hold of a long-owned or publicly defended position, and on any thesis the user has posted, pitched or repeated.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = the user has looked for disconfirming evidence and can name what would change their mind; 5 = aware of the counter-case but has not tested it; 0 = only confirming evidence gathered, thesis defended in public, every new fact explained away.
- **Quote:** "He trained himself, early, to intensively consider any evidence tending to disconfirm any hypothesis of his, more so if he thought his hypothesis was a particularly good one." (S002) Also: "One of the things I do is pat myself on the back every time I get rid of the dumb idea." (S046), "When you pound out an idea as a good idea, you're pounding it in!" (S053) and, quoting Feynman, "The first principle is that you must not fool yourself, and you're the easiest person to fool." (S019)
- **Dated example:** asked in 2023 which idea he destroyed in 2022, he named his enthusiasm for online retailers: "I got overcharmed by the people who are leading in the online retailing." (S052)
- **Sources:** 14 (S002, S003, S006, S014, S019, S024, S025, S046, S047, S049, S050, S052, S053, S065).

## 12. Pessimistic accounting and the phony-earnings red flags

- **What it is:** where big money turns on reported numbers, fools and knaves will fake them, so read accounts as if forced to be pessimistic. His Quant Tech fable (2000) shows dollop-by-dollop inflation through unexpensed options, smooth reported growth above real growth, and a collapse when real growth slows (S020). Other cases: the Texas hurricane insurer that reports good years only because there were no hurricanes (S066, S067), trader-set marks with bonuses on reported profit (S059), EBITDA that drops a real cost (S007), adjusted EBITDA as announced dishonesty (S049), and businesses cash-starved while reporting profits (S042, S044).
- **Parts (red flags):** (1) earnings rising at a suspiciously smooth rate; (2) cash not piling up in line with reported profit; (3) a falling tax rate on reported pre-tax income (S020); (4) invented profit measures; (5) depreciation or maintenance left out or deferred (S053); (6) revenue front-ended before risky bets are won (S073); (7) a good year that only means the bad event has not happened yet; (8) a full-year audit that lowers reported quarterly income (S067); (9) management painting the record instead of the building (S072); (10) critics dismissed as cranks.
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** on any company whose results depend on estimates (insurers, banks, lenders, long-contract firms), any company that leads with adjusted figures, and any stock priced on smooth growth.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = conservative accounting, earnings convert to cash, no invented metrics, losses recognized before profits as at Wesco (S026); 5 = adjusted metrics used but reconciled, one estimate-heavy line; 0 = several red flags at once, or earnings that are good only because the hurricane has not come.
- **Quote:** "The way to get maximum safety from accounting rules is to force a pessimistic outlook." (S059) Also: "Any time you have any new names to sort of mislead people, like adjusted EBITDA, think of the basic intellectual dishonesty that comes with it." (S049), "Thus, each good year reported is a lot like the year when a Texas hurricane insurer reports satisfactory earnings because there have been no hurricanes." (S066) and "Quant Tech's income taxes as a percentage of reported pre-tax earnings kept going lower and lower" (S020)
- **Sources:** 14 (S007, S020, S023, S042, S044, S049, S053, S056, S059, S062, S066, S067, S072, S073).

## 13. Bet seldom, bet big: the pari-mutuel market and one-foot fences

- **What it is:** the market is a betting pool where the odds adjust to the money bet, so the better company is not always the better bet, and the track takes a cut. The few who beat it bet rarely and heavily when they see a mispricing (S001). Look for one-foot fences with big rewards, not seven-foot ones (S016). A lifetime offers only a few invitations to the pie counter (his grandfather Ingham's rule, S046, S080); five or six transactions carried the whole Blue Chip and Wesco record (S022, S045). The wise hitter lets pitches go by (S072, S080). Prepare in advance or you will lack the courage when it comes (S053).
- **Parts:** (1) wait, agnostic about the market most of the time; (2) recognize the rare, obvious mispricing inside your circle; (3) act big and fast with money kept ready by earlier prudence (S079); (4) between those times, do nothing.
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** on whether to act at all, and how big. A marginal idea is a pass, not a small position.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = an obvious, rare mispricing the user understands, with cash ready to act big; 5 = a decent idea that is neither obvious nor rare, sized modestly; 0 = a seven-foot fence (a clever, complicated thesis) or a swing at every pitch.
- **Quote:** "And the one thing that all those winning betters in the whole history of people who've beaten the pari-mutuel system have is quite simple. They bet very seldom." (S001) Also: "we don't leap seven-foot fences. Instead, we look for one-foot fences with big rewards on the other side." (S016), "you're only gonna get three or four of these invitations to the pie counter" (S080) and "Most winners act as a wise baseball hitter would if permitted to pass as many pitches as he wished before swinging." (S072)
- **Sources:** 13 (S001, S006, S016, S022, S045, S046, S048, S053, S065, S072, S079, S080, S007).

## 14. Concentration arithmetic: deworsification, Kelly sizing and the cinch question

- **What it is:** if you know something, concentrate. As a young man he worked out with high school algebra that three stocks with a ten-point yearly edge held three or four years would be about 99 percent safe over 30 or 40 years (S046). Bet more the closer the deal is to a sure winner, Kelly-style, which he calls Fortune's formula (S048). Asking for 20 good holdings is asking for egg in your beer; four is lucky (S051). Coach Wooden gave almost all the playing time to his top seven players, and Munger calls a few picks found by fanatical search the Wooden system of stock picking (S009). The test question he puts to himself: what share of net worth belongs in something you think is a cinch (S025, S052).
- **Parts:** (1) how big is the edge and how sure; (2) size up as both rise; (3) cap by the drawdown you can stand (entry 10); (4) if you have no edge, index instead (S019, S025, S052).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** on add and trim decisions and on portfolio questions.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = size matches a stated, tested edge, and the worst case is survivable; 5 = size set by habit or comfort; 0 = big size with no edge, or a scattered portfolio of 30 names nobody understands, which he would call deworsification.
- **Quote:** "Diversification is a rule for those who don't know anything." (S046) Also: "the bigger your edge, the more close the transaction was to a certain winner, the more you should bet" (S048), "What is the appropriate percentage of your net worth you should put in a stock if you think it's an absolute cinch?" (S025) and "Wesco would cheerfully invest $75 million tomorrow, with a 60% chance of total loss, provided the pay-off for winning was large enough to cause statistical expectation to provide a handsome return." (S072)
- **Limit he states:** the logical answer to the cinch question is near all of it, but few people are right that often (S052, paraphrase). For most people: "it's not difficult to just buy an index fund and sit on your ass. That's the great default position." (S025)
- **Sources:** 12 (S007, S009, S024, S025, S037, S046, S048, S050, S051, S052, S066, S073).

## 15. Latticework of models, the antidote to the man with a hammer

- **What it is:** to a man with a hammer every problem looks like a nail, so carry the big ideas of every big discipline (math, accounting, engineering, biology, psychology, microeconomics) and use them together as a checklist. One or two models make you torture reality to fit. Experts use only models from their own segment (S073). People also overweigh what can be counted (S004). He sets 80 to 100 models as enough to carry most of the freight (S001, S016).
- **Parts:** (1) which disciplines bear on this decision; (2) what does each say; (3) where do they agree or conflict; (4) what is the hard-to-count factor the numbers are drowning out.
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** when an analysis rests on one lens only (a spreadsheet, a chart, a single macro view), and when the user's profession is shaping the answer.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = the case is checked from several disciplines and the uncountable factors are named; 5 = one strong lens plus a partial check; 0 = one model applied to everything, with unmeasurable risks ignored because they cannot be counted.
- **Quote:** "if you just have one or two that you're using, the nature of human psychology is such that you'll torture reality so that it fits your models, or at least you'll think it does." (S001) Also: "You've got to use those tools checklist-style because you'll miss a lot if you just hope that the right tool is going to pop up unaided whenever you need it." (S004) and "practically everybody (1) overweighs the stuff that can be numbered" (S004)
- **Sources:** 12 (S001, S002, S004, S016, S017, S018, S021, S024, S045, S046, S073, S083).

## 16. The past-success audit: talent or niche?

- **What it is:** past success is a trap, so examine it for accidents before betting on it again. Look for dangers in a new undertaking that were absent when it worked before (S002). Ask how much of present prosperity comes from talent that will carry to new activities and how much from the luck of being in a good niche (S073). Expect a run of good years to be followed by bad ones and never extrapolate abnormal results (S029, S032, S033). It can be wise to cut the activity that has recently worked best (S015). Johnson and Johnson makes everyone revisit old acquisitions against their predictions (S002). Read physical volume, not dollar sales: Precision Steel's price rises hid a fall from 69 million pounds to 46 million (S035).
- **Parts:** (1) list what drove past results; (2) mark which drivers were luck, niche or a cycle; (3) check whether they still apply; (4) compare old predictions with outcomes; (5) look at units and volume under the dollar figures.
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** on companies after a record year, on managements expanding into new fields on the strength of the old one, and on the user's own winning streak.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = past results explained driver by driver, with the niche and cycle parts stripped out; 5 = some extrapolation, flagged; 0 = a record year treated as the new base, or a manager's success in one niche assumed to carry to another.
- **Quote:** "you must know how much of your present prosperity is caused by talents and momentum assuring success in new activities, and how much merely reflects the good fortune of being in your present niche" (S073) Also: "to look for dangerous aspects of the new undertaking that were not present when past success occurred." (S002) and "Such weirdly favorable results are not to be expected over the long term." (S032)
- **Sources:** 10 (S002, S015, S028, S029, S032, S033, S034, S035, S068, S073).

## 17. Honest intrinsic value: liquidation value plus a discounted tax float

- **What it is:** each year in the Wesco letters he estimated intrinsic value as after-tax liquidating value plus a heavily discounted value for the interest-free loan from deferred taxes, then told holders plainly whether the stock was above or below it. The deferred tax is not perpetual, so a $24 or $45 paper benefit was valued at about $8 or $15 (S076, S078). Results he printed: about 30 percent above value at yearend 1993 (S076), about 22 percent above in 1995 (S078), about 5 percent below in 1996 (S079), about 14 percent below in 1999 (S028). It belongs in this seat as a self-honesty routine: the manager tells owners when their own stock is too dear.
- **Parts:** (1) book value as rough liquidation value, checked for hidden appreciation; (2) add the tax float at a deep discount; (3) compare with price; (4) say so, even when it argues against buying your own stock.
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** on holding companies and insurers, and as the model for a management that will not whoop up its stock.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = value estimated conservatively and price compared honestly; 5 = value estimated but flattering items counted at face; 0 = paper benefits counted at full value, or no value estimate at all.
- **Quote:** "Therefore, Wesco's shareholders have no perpetual advantage creating value for them of $24 per Wesco share." (S076) Also: "Wesco stock was then selling about 14% below intrinsic value." (S028) and "It has never been the writer's view that the unvarying duty of management is to whoop up the stock price." (S076)
- **Sources:** 9 (S026, S027, S028, S029, S033, S076, S077, S078, S079).

## 18. Denial, and Hardin's rule that bad ideas are born good

- **What it is:** by 2023 he named denial as the one factor that dominates bad decisions: when the truth is painful, the mind rejects it and people keep doing what used to work (S025, S052). Garrett Hardin's principle, which he used to explain the savings and loan disaster (S043, S044): in soft fields bad ideas are born good, work for a while, then stop and reverse, and the mind rejects the danger signal because it contradicts a cherished idea. The cure is to reverse course when the danger flags fly, without needing to foresee the storm. Wells Fargo's real error was not recognizing the bad news when it came (S046).
- **Parts:** (1) list the ideas this position depends on; (2) note how old each is and whether it has stopped working; (3) write down the danger flags in advance; (4) when one flies, act, even if the idea served you for years; (5) ask who benefits from not seeing it.
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** on long-held winners, on businesses whose model is aging (newspapers, borrow-short-lend-long thrifts), and whenever the user explains away bad news.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = danger flags written in advance and checked; 5 = flags known but not written; 0 = flags already flying and being explained away.
- **Quote:** "If I had to name one factor that dominates human bad decisions, it would be what I call denial." (S025) Also: "the tendency of the mind to reject the message from a danger signal which is inconsistent with a cherished idea" (S044) and "tragedy can be averted, partially, only by reversing course when the danger flags start flying" (S044) His own case: on newspapers, "We underestimated the rate of decline." (S047)
- **Sources:** 9 (S002, S019, S025, S043, S044, S046, S047, S052, S058).

## 19. Bonds and Rembrandts: reading speculative mania

- **What it is:** stocks are valued partly like bonds, on rough projections of cash, and partly like Rembrandt paintings, bought because their prices have gone up (S019, S058). When speculation and long-term investing share one liquid market, the result is wretched excess; he wanted a divorce, by taxing short-term gains (S051, S055). Easy money produces regrettable behavior (S045) and ruins nearly everyone in finance (S053). Crowds act like lemmings (S058). The wealth effect can run harder on the way down (S058).
- **Parts:** (1) how much of the price is cash-flow value and how much is momentum; (2) is credit easy; (3) are amateurs crowding in because it is going up; (4) if the Rembrandt part dominates, stay out.
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** on hot sectors, newly listed money-losers, crypto and any asset whose main argument is that it keeps rising.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = the price is explained by cash-flow value; 5 = some momentum premium; 0 = the price is mostly Rembrandt, bought on credit in a crowd.
- **Quote:** "They are valued partly like bonds, based on roughly rational projections of use value in producing future cash. But they are also valued partly like Rembrandt paintings, purchased mostly because their prices have gone up so far." (S019) Also: "It's not a good marriage, and I think we need a divorce." (S051) and "And I think wise people should just stay out of them." (S050)
- **Sources:** 8 (S019, S024, S045, S050, S051, S053, S055, S058).

## 20. Croupier costs and febezzlement

- **What it is:** all investors together bear a disadvantage equal to the croupier costs they pay, so after costs half land below the median (S019). Costs of 3 percent a year can shrink a foundation even in a good market (S019). Paying 2 points out of 5 does not cost 40 percent of your future but about 90 percent once compounded (S048). Galbraith's bezzle extended: wasted fees are the functional equivalent of undisclosed embezzlement, invisible while markets rise because the payer still feels richer (S004, S058, S062). A fair manager charges only above a hurdle and lives off his own capital when underwater (S047).
- **Parts:** (1) total all layers of cost, fees and turnover; (2) compound them over the holding period; (3) compare with Berkshire-style costs of under a tenth of one percent (S019); (4) ask whether the manager has earned a premium with very unusual results (S007).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** on funds, advisers, structured products and high-turnover strategies, and on companies whose profits are other people's fees.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = total costs near index cost; 5 = about one percent with real evidence of skill; 0 = layered fees near the 3 percent he described, with no proof of edge.
- **Quote:** "All the equity investors, in total, will surely bear a performance disadvantage per annum equal to the total croupiers' costs they have jointly elected to bear." (S019) Also: "if you make 5 percent and pay 2 of it to your advisors, you're not losing 40 percent of your future you're losing 90 percent" (S048) and "the functional equivalent of undisclosed embezzlement" (S004)
- **Sources:** 7 (S004, S007, S019, S047, S048, S058, S062).

## 21. Acquisition and bargain-trap skepticism

- **What it is:** a set of warnings about buying businesses, and about managements that buy them. Successful acquirers draw attention while the failures are forgotten (S072). Stock-for-stock deals usually give away more value than they get (S072). The game is crowded with optimists who bid prices up (S069). Buying below liquidating value tends to produce mediocre results unless you turn it fast, because the business swamps the discount; he says this was the error in Berkshire's own Wesco purchase (S070). Cigar butts did not scale, so he pushed Buffett to better businesses (S008, S082). Buying a small company because it is small is "arrant nonsense in method" (S076).
- **Parts:** (1) who is paying for whom, and in what currency; (2) is the success record a survivor sample; (3) is the discount being swamped by a mediocre business; (4) does the acquirer have an acquisition department or bankers paid to do deals.
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** on serial acquirers, deep-value bargains and companies issuing stock to buy growth.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = rare deals, paid in cash, at prices that leave the advantage intact, by people who underclaim expertise (S063); 5 = occasional deals with mixed records; 0 = serial stock-paid acquisitions or a cheap stock in a business that cannot earn its keep.
- **Quote:** "We think undue optimism arises because successful records draw too much attention." (S072) Also: "any attainable stock-for-stock merger will transfer more intrinsic business value than is acquired" (S072) and "the acquirer was overly influenced by a price considered to be moderately below liquidating value" (S070)
- **Changed over time:** 1994: "Over the long term, it's hard for a stock to earn a much better return than the business which underlies it earns." (S001) 2017: "we eventually learned not to buy these cigar butts when they were cheap" (S008), and newer, the same year, on Apple and airlines: "We're doing all kinds of stuff now that we would not have done." (S053)
- **Sources:** 9 (S001, S008, S053, S063, S069, S070, S072, S076, S082).

## 22. Everything dies: capitalism as biology

- **What it is:** companies behave like species, and practically everything dies on a long enough timescale (S006, S025, S052). New technology kills old businesses (S001). Efficiency gains that help civilization often flow to customers rather than to passive owners (S083). The practical value is a standing inversion: ask what kills this moat, rather than assuming it lasts. This seat keeps the moat quality question itself thin; that dimension belongs to the Buffett seat.
- **Parts:** (1) what species is this business, and which close species compete for its niche (Hertz and Enterprise, S083); (2) what technology or cheaper rival could take the position away; (3) how fast did similar businesses die (newspapers).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** on long-hold theses that assume permanence, and on incumbents facing new technology.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = the killers of the moat are named and none is visible; 5 = a known threat moving slowly; 0 = the thesis assumes the moat is permanent while a cheaper or newer species is already in the niche.
- **Quote:** "the companies of America behave more like biology than they do anything else." (S006) Also: "Practically everything dies in business." (S052) and "All kinds of technical interventions that are wonderful for the civilization are not necessarily wonderful for the passive owners of the common stocks" (S083)
- **Sources:** 7 (S001, S006, S024, S025, S050, S052, S083).

## 23. Second-order effects and the flow-through test

- **What it is:** you can never do merely one thing (S072); consequences have consequences (S004). The second step most people skip in a capital decision: how much of the benefit stays home and how much flows through to customers. In a commodity business it all flows through, as with textile looms that paid for themselves while the business still earned about 4 percent (S001, S004).
- **Parts:** (1) what is the first-order effect; (2) what do competitors, customers and regulators do next; (3) who keeps the gain at the end.
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** on capex-heavy plans, cost-cutting stories, new technology adoption and policy changes.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = the gain is shown to stay with owners after competitors respond; 5 = some gain retained; 0 = the gain plainly flows to customers, as in a commodity industry.
- **Quote:** "they don't do the second step of the analysis which is to determine how much is going to stay home and how much is just going to flow through to the customer." (S001) Also: "the consequences have consequences, and the consequences of the consequences have consequences" (S004) and "You can never do merely one thing." (S072)
- **Sources:** 6 (S001, S004, S008, S071, S072, S083).

## 24. Judging people: the record over the interview

- **What it is:** a person's paper record predicts about three times better than an impression from an interview (S008), and face-to-face impressions should be underweighted (S002). Where you cannot judge the work, judge the people doing it, as Carnegie did with steel (S046). Invest in the lengthened shadows of the right sort of people (S041). Prefer the person who knows his limits (S048, S053) and who is moneywise, which cannot be created in someone who lacks it (S065).
- **Parts:** (1) what has this manager actually done, over how long; (2) do they underclaim or overclaim; (3) do they tell bad news promptly; (4) would you be glad to have them as a partner.
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** on management quality, especially after an impressive meeting or call.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = a long, checkable record of honest dealing and capital allocation, with self-limits; 5 = a short or mixed record; 0 = the case rests on charisma or a good meeting.
- **Quote:** "the paper record has about three times the predictive value of your impression interview and of course we're buying great paper records." (S008) Also: "We are judging people because we don't understand what the people do." (S046) and "I'd rather bet on the man who has some limit to his self-appraisal." (S053)
- **Sources:** 7 (S002, S008, S041, S046, S048, S053, S065).

## 25. The iron prescription: state the other side better first

- **What it is:** he does not allow himself an opinion until he can state the case against it better than its supporters can (S005, S021, S050). Courts and good decision-makers force groups to hear skilled counterarguments before deciding (S002).
- **Parts:** (1) write the bear case for a long, or the bull case for a sell, in its strongest form; (2) check that a holder of that view would sign it; (3) only then decide.
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** before any high-conviction call, and whenever the user cannot name a serious argument on the other side.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = the opposing case is stated better than its holders state it, and answered; 5 = stated but weakly; 0 = the user cannot state it, so by his rule they are not yet entitled to the opinion.
- **Quote:** "I'm not entitled to have an opinion on this subject unless I can state the arguments against my position better than the people do who are supporting it." (S005) Also: "I think I'm not really equipped to comment on this subject until I can state the arguments against my conclusion better than the people on the other side." (S050)
- **Sources:** 4 (S002, S005, S021, S050).
