# Selling: Peter Lynch

Built from 13 interviews, talks and TV transcripts (S002 to S035, 1980 to 2025) through the verified batch notes. His rules for selling, trimming, adding and re-checking, most-used first. His selling view is stable from 1980 to 2025: sell on the story, never on the price alone.

## Rules

### 1. Sell when the story breaks, not when the price moves
Sources: 5 (S004, S016, S017, S027, S029).
"If it deteriorates, if the fundamentals slip, you have to say goodbye to it." (S016) For a superior restaurant chain, the thesis ends when the restaurants slip: "once that started to deteriorate, you should have eliminated your holding." (S004) The stock owes you nothing: "One rule you want to remember: the stock does not know you own it." (S016)

### 2. Do not sell the winners to keep the losers
Sources: 5 (S002, S007, S021, S025, S026).
"When you sell your great companies and add to the losers it's like watering the weeds and cutting the flowers." (S026) Add to the ones that keep getting better, as with Stop & Shop: "But the company kept getting better and better and I held onto it." (S021) He notes most professionals do the opposite with conservative stocks and hold a profit forever because it looks good (S025).

### 3. A falling price on an intact story is a chance to add
Sources: 5 (S002, S016, S017, S025, S033).
"If you like a stock at $14 and it goes to $6, that's great." (S016) Only with a sound balance sheet and only if you know why you own it: "This is the problem people have: is they sell stocks because they didn't know why they bought it, then it goes down and they don't know what to do now." (S016) In 1987 Dreyfus fell to $17 with $17 a share in cash (S033).

### 4. A falling price alone is never a reason to buy or average down
Sources: 4 (S016, S025, S026, S027).
"I've tried it myself, and it doesn't work very well. That's known as bottom fishing." (S025) And a low price does not cap the loss: "you lose exactly the same amount of money, everything." (S016)

### 5. When you are wrong, sell; do not hope and pray
Sources: 1 (S027).
"if I make a mistake, I try and sell it." (S027) Waiting to get even can take years: "you just want to just hope and pray because you can wait for years to go on." (S027)

### 6. Swap for something more attractive
Sources: 2 (S025, S027).
"I try and sell stocks because something else is more attractive. Stock A is simply more attractive than Stock B." (S027) In a decline, sell the conservative stock that held up and buy the growth or cyclical name that fell: "So we might sell a conservative stock that we bought at 30 at 29 after the decline" (S025)

### 7. Trim conservative stocks after their move; let growth stocks run
Sources: 1 (S025).
Conservative stocks are bought for 30 to 35 percent, then rotated. "The trick is if you make 30% to 35% with Borden, you can't hold it for 44 years." (S025) Growth companies are held five to ten years or more (S017, S025).

### 8. Exit a growth story by the seventh inning, on saturation, not on gains
Sources: 3 (S017, S029, S007).
Be "in the second inning of the ballgame, and out in the seventh. That could be 30 years." (S029) The Limited and Gap in every mall were the eighth and ninth inning (S017). The fireman's rule from S017: keep buying "until they stop hiring people." (S017)

### 9. Do not judge a holding on its first few months, and do not chase the last eighth
Sources: 2 (S018, S027).
"I've found there's almost no relationship with what a stock does in the first few months I've owned it." (S027) "I don't try and find out the last quarter, the last eighth, the last 10 points." (S027) Some years nobody understands the stock (S018).

### 10. Do not sell because you fear the market
Sources: 4 (S002, S004, S026, S033).
"They should buy, hold, and when the market goes down, add to it." (S002) In 1982 he held companies that were losing money because "These companies are going to do well once the economy comes back." (S002)

## Selling mistakes he admits

- Flying Tiger, 1960s: sold all the way up from 20 to 80; a lucky ten bagger, not a thesis (S002).
- Taco Bell: lost to Pepsi's buyout at 30 when it might have run much further; he repeats the point about private equity in 2025 (S007).
- Textiles, date not given: recommended the stocks all the way down instead of exiting as the industry broke (S026).
- Wal-Mart in 1980 and Sherwin-Williams later: skipped or let go on price or on a guess that it was the last inning, without the research (S021, S029).
- Housing and Fannie Mae: in 1990 he said there would be no wipeout in basic housing (S018); by 2022 he said he had no idea how overdone housing was in 2008 (S017).

## Kill criteria this seat would write

These are checkable facts in his style, inferred from S004, S016, S017, S025, S029.
- Sales growth falls below the rate that defined it as a growth company for two years running, so it is no longer a growth stock.
- Same-store results or unit economics slip in the core formula (the restaurants deteriorate).
- Store or customer count reaches saturation, every mall already has one, with no proven second market.
- A rival product tests as good or better in a hands-on check.
- Debt rises so the company could not survive 18 to 24 months of bad times.
- The recovery needs a large share issue that dilutes it away.
- The written reasons for owning it no longer hold, two of three or worse.
- The price already holds about ten years of growth, the Coca-Cola test, while growth is slowing.

## Re-check triggers

- Every quarterly report, read for the drivers named in the story (Nike inventories, S007).
- About half an hour a month on why you own each company (S033).
- Any drop of 20 percent or more: re-read the written reasons (S017, S029).
- A new competitor or product in the category (S002).
- Monthly industry facts: inventories, used car prices, occupancies, commodity prices (S002, S016).
- A buyout offer or private equity interest (S002, S007).
- A broad market decline of 10 percent or more, used to add, not to sell (S002, S025).
