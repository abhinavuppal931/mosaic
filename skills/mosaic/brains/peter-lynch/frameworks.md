# Frameworks: Peter Lynch

Built from 13 interviews, talks and TV transcripts (S002 to S035, 1980 to 2025) through the verified batch notes. Quick depth. His books (One Up on Wall Street, Beating the Street, Learn to Earn) are copyrighted and not quoted; ideas taught mainly there are paraphrase with the book named. Most-used first; where counts tie, the seat's own question (what kind of stock, what story, does growth pay for the multiple) goes first. Scoring lines are guidance drawn from his own numbers, not a scorecard he published.

## 1. The two-minute story (know what you own)

- **What it is:** before you own a stock, you can say in a minute or two, to a child, what the company does, why you own it and what has to happen for it to work. If you cannot, buy a fund. In One Up on Wall Street he turns this into the two-minute drill (taught in One Up on Wall Street; paraphrase): a short monologue on why the company should succeed and what to watch, shaped by the kind of stock it is (framework 8).
- **Parts:** (1) what the business does; (2) that it is a good business with a good balance sheet (S007); (3) the specific reasons earnings will be higher in two to three years, three to five of them (S007, S017, S029); (4) the story must move the whole company, not one percent of it, as with a plastics story bought through General Electric (S027); (5) what you will watch to see it unfold (S027).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** first, on every buy and every add. It is also the hold test after a fall: if you cannot restate the story you have nothing to act on (S016).
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = the story fits in two minutes, names three to five checkable reasons for higher earnings in two to three years, and the story is most of the company. 5 = a good company, but the reason to own it is vague or is a small piece of a large company. 0 = the only reason is that "the sucker is going up" (S016), a tip, or a story you cannot check.
- **Quote:** "if you can't explain to a ten year-old in two minutes or less why you own a stock, you shouldn't own it." (S016) Also: "If you can't do that, you should buy a fund." (S007)
- **Sources:** 9 (S002, S007, S016, S017, S018, S025, S027, S029, S033).

## 2. How long is the story: the innings test, and you have plenty of time

- **What it is:** a baseball frame for the runway of a growth story. Ask what inning the expansion is in. You want to be in by the second to fifth inning and out by the seventh, which can take decades. You do not need to be there at the start: a proven formula still replicating leaves plenty of time.
- **Parts:** (1) count where the formula has reached (Wal-Mart in 15 to 18 percent of the US ten years after its 1970 IPO, S002, S007, S017; McDonald's with 20 stores in France on the way to 400, S007); (2) ask where it can still go (the Limited and Gap in every mall meant the eighth or ninth inning, S017); (3) confirm with facts that it is being done, not imagined (S007); (4) sell when saturation arrives, not when the stock has risen.
- **When to use it:** on any growth company, and on any claim that a winner is "done" because it has already gone up.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = innings two to five, the formula proven in one region and still replicating, store or customer counts you can see growing. 5 = innings six or seven, growth real but the white space is shrinking. 0 = eighth or ninth inning, every mall already has one, or a growth story with no proof yet (inning zero, a long shot).
- **Quote:** "in the second inning of the ballgame, and out in the seventh. That could be 30 years." (S029) And: "You have plenty of time. Don't have this idea that you have to be there at the moment of conception" (S021)
- **Sources:** 9 (S002, S007, S016, S017, S018, S021, S029, S033, S035).

## 3. Turn over the most rocks: own-industry edge, look at ten and find one

- **What it is:** breadth of homework is the edge. People see turns in their own industry, town or mall years before Wall Street. Look at ten companies and one is interesting; look at twenty and two are; follow five or six and own one or two. Neglected, unfashionable, secondary companies are where mispricing lives.
- **Parts:** (1) start from what you see at work and in shops (Tagamet for druggists, the minivan, aluminum inventories, S016); (2) do not stop at the mall, do the follow-up work on store counts and finances (S026); (3) widen the net to unloved industries with no prejudice (S018, S025); (4) treat heavy institutional ownership and many brokerage reports as a negative (S027, dated 1980).
- **When to use it:** to source ideas, and to judge whether the user has an edge on this stock or is late to a crowded one.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = the user sees the business first-hand or works in its industry, few analysts follow it, and the facts are checkable. 5 = known company, some first-hand knowledge. 0 = a tip with no first-hand facts, or one of the 500 most-followed stocks where "a thousand people just as smart" (S025) look every day.
- **Quote:** "The person that turns over the most rocks wins the game." (S002) Also: "People have incredible edges and they throw them away." (S016)
- **Sources:** 9 (S002, S004, S007, S016, S018, S025, S026, S027, S029).

## 4. Economic facts, not economic predictions

- **What it is:** spend almost no time forecasting the economy, rates or the market. Watch present facts that bear on the companies you own: used car prices, hotel occupancy, ethylene prices, aluminum inventories, home affordability, credit card debt, the savings rate.
- **Parts:** (1) list the two or three industry facts that drive this company's earnings; (2) check them on a cadence; (3) ignore market and recession calls, including your own mood about the market (S025).
- **When to use it:** whenever a decision is being argued from a macro forecast, and on cyclicals, where the facts are the whole case.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = the thesis rests on observable facts that are already improving. 5 = the thesis needs a normal economy but no forecast. 0 = the thesis needs a rate, recession or market call to work.
- **Quote:** "There are economic facts and there are economic predictions, and economic predictions are a total waste." (S016)
- **Sources:** 9 (S002, S004, S007, S016, S017, S018, S025, S026, S033).

## 5. Turnaround by degrees: crappy to semi crappy to terrific

- **What it is:** a stock moves on each step of improvement, not only on full recovery. Losing six, then losing two, then making two can quadruple a stock while the industry still looks bad. The flip side: an industry going bad is not by itself a reason to buy, because it is always darkest before pitch black.
- **Parts:** (1) has the company reached the first step of improvement in facts, not hopes (S026, S029); (2) three turnaround questions: a reasonable chance to turn, good products, solvency (S033); (3) will the recovery be diluted away by issuing a zillion shares (S025); (4) the balance sheet survives 12 to 24 months (framework 7).
- **When to use it:** on any depressed, down and out or cyclical stock (autos, oil and gas, shipping, banks in a crisis).
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = losses already shrinking, products good, cash enough for two to three years (Chrysler had about 2 billion, S007), no big dilution needed. 5 = solvent and cheap but improvement not yet visible: wait. 0 = industry still worsening and the only case is that it has fallen a lot.
- **Quote:** "Then they went from losing two to making two. The stock quadrupled." (S007) And: "It's always darkest before pitch black. Just when you think things are terrible, they get terrible squared." (S026)
- **Sources:** 8 (S007, S016, S017, S025, S026, S027, S029, S033).

## 6. A company behind every stock: earnings and price move together

- **What it is:** over years a stock follows its earnings. Corporate profits have grown about 8 percent a year and the market followed; Coca-Cola and Merck profits up 15-fold and the stocks too; Bethlehem Steel earned less and the stock halved.
- **Parts:** (1) look at the earnings record and where it is going; (2) do not confuse a good company with a stock that has already priced that in (framework 13); (3) expect price and earnings to reconnect.
- **When to use it:** to frame every decision as a bet on the company's future earnings, not on the chart.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = earnings and sales growing with the price lagging. 5 = price in line with earnings growth. 0 = price up while earnings fall or never arrive.
- **Quote:** "Stocks are not lottery tickets. There's a company behind every stock. If a company does well, the stock does well. It's not that complicated." (S016) Also: "It's a direct relationship, what happens to a company's earnings and what happens to their stocks." (S033)
- **Sources:** 7 (S002, S007, S016, S018, S026, S033, S035).

## 7. Balance sheet first: will it be around in 18 to 24 months

- **What it is:** before the earnings story, check the balance sheet to see if the company survives if things get grim. A company with no debt is very hard to bankrupt, and a sound balance sheet is what lets you buy more when the price falls.
- **Parts:** (1) cash against debt and near-term needs; (2) can it last 12, 18 or 24 months in a bad case; (3) for banks, how it is funded (Bank of America was 120 percent retail funded, S029); (4) will recovery require heavy share issuance (S025).
- **When to use it:** first on turnarounds, cyclicals and anything that has fallen hard; second on every other stock.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = little or no debt, cash covers two to three years of bad times. 5 = some debt but no maturity or funding risk in two years. 0 = survival depends on new capital or lenders; he has "no interest" (S029).
- **Quote:** "Will this company survive the next 12, 24 months in case things get grim?" (S017) And: "It's very hard to go bankrupt if you don't have any debt." (S016)
- **Sources:** 7 (S007, S016, S017, S025, S026, S029, S033).

## 8. What kind of stock is this: the stock categories

- **What it is:** sort the stock before judging it, because each kind has its own story, upside, holding period and exit. In One Up on Wall Street he names six kinds (taught in One Up on Wall Street; paraphrase): slow growers, stalwarts, fast growers, cyclicals, turnarounds and asset plays. In the corpus he uses a working version of the same sort: growth companies, conservative stocks, cyclicals, turnarounds and special situations (S025, S027, S029).
- **Parts, in corpus terms:**
  - Growth (the book's fast growers): a small successful company growing into a major one over a long time (S027), held five to ten years (S017), hoping for doubles and triples (S025). Defined by sales growth, not earnings growth (S029); best as a growth company in a non-growth industry (S029).
  - Conservative stocks (the book's stalwarts): large, dividend paying, stable, no losses in recessions; aim for 30 to 35 percent and rotate (S025, framework 14).
  - Cyclicals: buy on improving industry facts, often seen first by people in the industry (S016, S026); own the low-cost producer, never the commodity (S017).
  - Turnarounds: a major company with earnings problems in a depressed industry whose earnings are about to turn (S027), tested by framework 5.
  - Asset plays and special situations: Kaiser Industries at $3 with the whole company worth about one 747 (S016); Dreyfus at $17 with $17 a share in cash (S033).
  - Slow growers: no recorded discussion in the corpus beyond the book.
- **When to use it:** step one on any stock. The error he names is calling a margin rebound "growth" (S029).
- **Scoring guidance:** score against the category's own bar. Growth: sales growing, innings two to five, multiple paid for by growth (framework 13). Conservative: 30 to 35 percent upside, about 10 percent downside if wrong (S025). Cyclical and turnaround: survives 18 to 24 months and facts already improving. 0 = the category cannot be named, or its exit point has already passed.
- **Quote:** "A company can go from losing money to a 2% margin, to a 12% margin, and earnings are up sixfold, but that's not a growth company." (S029) The fallback order: "If you cannot find growth companies in innings three through five of the ballgame, look at turnarounds, special situations, back at the cyclicals." (S029)
- **Sources:** 6 (S016, S017, S025, S027, S029, S033), plus One Up on Wall Street (paraphrase).

## 9. Market decline arithmetic: expect the drops

- **What it is:** declines are normal and unforecastable, so prepare instead of predicting. Over about 95 years there were about 50 declines of 10 percent or more (one every two years) and 15 of 25 percent or more (one every six years). Magellan fell 10 percent or more nine times in 13 years.
- **When to use it:** on any sell-because-the-market-might-fall argument, and to check whether the user can hold through a 25 percent drop.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = money not needed for five years or more, owner knows the drops are coming. 5 = long horizon but shaky stomach. 0 = money needed within a year or two.
- **Quote:** "You need to know the market is going to go down sometimes. If you're not ready for that, you shouldn't own stocks." (S016) And: "If you're prepared for it, you don't panic." (S004)
- **Sources:** 6 (S002, S004, S016, S018, S026, S033).

## 10. Ground truth checks (the L'eggs test)

- **What it is:** check the product and the competition yourself. Carolyn liked L'eggs; he checked where hosiery was sold, and when Kayser-Roth launched No Nonsense he bought dozens of pairs, handed them out at Fidelity and asked for feedback. They said it was not as good, so he held.
- **Parts:** (1) try the product or watch customers; (2) test the rival; (3) check distribution and store counts; (4) then read the numbers.
- **When to use it:** on consumer, retail and local companies, and whenever a new competitor threatens a holding.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = first-hand evidence that customers prefer it and rivals are weaker. 5 = good numbers, no first-hand check. 0 = a mall impression with no follow-up (S026).
- **Quote:** "That's what fundamental research is." (S002) And: "you don't leave the mall and buy that thing. You have to do some more work." (S026)
- **Sources:** 5 (S002, S007, S017, S018, S026).

## 11. A few big winners pay for the mistakes

- **What it is:** portfolio arithmetic. You can lose only what you put in, but you can make ten or fifteen times. Being right six times in ten is good; even three in ten works if you add to the ones that work. One or two good stocks a decade is enough.
- **When to use it:** when sizing the case for a possible ten bagger, and when a user wants to sell a winner to fund a loser.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = upside many times the stake with downside limited to the stake and a story that can compound. 5 = 30 to 50 percent upside. 0 = capped upside with real permanent-loss risk.
- **Quote:** "You only need a few good stocks in your lifetime." (S002) And: "you don't have to be right half the time. If you're right three times out of 10" (S033)
- **Sources:** 5 (S002, S007, S018, S021, S033).

## 12. Write down the reasons, then re-check them

- **What it is:** put on paper why you own it, so that after a fall you can see whether the reasons still hold. For practice, run a paper portfolio of ten stocks for a year or two and compare the reasons to what happened.
- **When to use it:** at purchase, and at every drop of 20 percent or more.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = three written reasons, each still valid. 5 = reasons valid but one weakening. 0 = no written reasons, or the reasons have broken.
- **Quote:** "You ought to be able to write three reasons, are they still valid, on a piece of paper." (S017) And: "Cheap is different from a [good] story." (S029)
- **Sources:** 5 (S007, S016, S017, S027, S029).

## 13. Does growth pay for the multiple *(unnamed in the corpus; PEG in the book)*

- **What it is:** taught in One Up on Wall Street; paraphrase: compare the price-earnings ratio with the company's growth rate. A P/E about equal to the growth rate is fair, well under it is attractive, and around twice it is a warning; the book also offers a variant that adds the dividend yield to growth. In the corpus he never says PEG, but he reasons the same way: Coca-Cola was a spectacular company whose stock had "priced in the next 10 years of growth" (S007); Costco at 55 times earnings drew an "Ooph" (S007); Johnson & Johnson doubling earnings in six years meant the stock would double (S035).
- **The counterweight:** he skipped Wal-Mart in 1980 because he thought it overpriced, and it went on to make 25 times (S021). A high multiple on an early-innings runway can still be cheap; inferred from S021, S007, S029.
- **When to use it:** on every growth stock, after framework 2 sets the runway.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = P/E at or below a sales-backed growth rate, runway in innings two to five. 5 = P/E about one and a half times growth, or several years of growth already priced. 0 = the next ten years of growth already in the price, or a multiple on earnings that do not exist yet (long shots).
- **Quote:** "I mean it's the stocks, you know, priced in the next 10 years of growth." (S007) And: "I didn't own it during this period by the way. I thought it was overpriced." (S021)
- **Sources:** 4 (S007, S021, S033, S035), plus One Up on Wall Street (paraphrase).

## 14. Conservative stock rotation and the portfolio mix

- **What it is:** from the Magellan years (about 1985). Hold about 25 to 40 percent growth companies, 20 to 25 percent cyclicals and special situations, and the rest conservative stocks or bonds. With conservative stocks, take 30 to 35 percent and rotate: six 30 percent moves make fourfold. When the market falls, sell a conservative stock that held up and buy the growth or cyclical name that fell, if fundamentals are fine.
- **When to use it:** on a stalwart that has done its job, and on how to fund an add during a decline.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = a stalwart bought with 30 percent upside and about 10 percent downside if wrong. 0 = a stalwart held for decades on affection.
- **Quote:** "If you have six 30% moves, it is equal to fourfold." (S025)
- **Sources:** 1 (S025).

## 15. Buy the producer, not the commodity; survivors win

- **What it is:** he never bought copper, zinc or nickel, only the best, lowest-cost producers. After a wipeout, look for the companies that still make money if the price falls further (in 2022, crypto miners with low power costs).
- **When to use it:** on any commodity or speculative-asset thesis.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = lowest-cost producer, profitable at a lower price, survivable finances. 0 = a bet on the commodity price itself.
- **Quote:** "I bought the companies that produce it when I thought they were the best with the lowest cost." (S017) And: "And when there's a wipeout, the survivors win." (S017)
- **Sources:** 1 (S017).
