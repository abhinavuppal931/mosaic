# Stories: Peter Lynch

Built from 13 interviews, talks and TV transcripts (S002 to S035, 1980 to 2025) through the verified batch notes. The cases he reuses, most-told first, with the lesson he draws. Misses and losers count as much as winners.

## 1. Wal-Mart: the miss he tells most
Told in 6 sources (S002, S007, S016, S021, S029, S033). Public in October 1970 with about 38 stores; ten years later profits were up about 20-fold and the stock ten-fold, and it was still in under a fifth of the country. He thought it overpriced in 1980 and did not own it; it went on to make 25 times, then 50 to 80 times more. Lesson: a proven formula still replicating leaves plenty of time, and a high price on an early-innings story can still be cheap. "That doesn't take a lot of courage. That's homework." (S002)

## 2. Hanes L'eggs and the No Nonsense test
Told in 3 sources (S002, S007, S017). Carolyn liked L'eggs, sold in supermarkets while 95 percent of hosiery was in department stores. When Kayser-Roth launched No Nonsense he bought dozens of pairs (48, 50 or 65 in different tellings), handed them out at Fidelity, heard it was not as good, and held. It became his biggest position and might have been a thirty bagger but for the buyout by Consolidated Foods (S002). Lesson: test the competitor yourself. "I gave it out to everybody at Fidelity, and said give me some feedback on this." (S007)

## 3. Chrysler: the down-and-out turnaround
Told in 3 sources (S002, S007, S017). His biggest position when he recommended it on Wall Street Week in October 1982 at about 10, while people said it was going bankrupt. It had cash for three years and the minivan and Jeep coming. Lesson: check survival first, then the product turn. "they had 2 billion in cash and they had enough money for the next three years. They weren't going bankrupt." (S007)

## 4. Dunkin' Donuts: the business with nothing to worry about
Told in 3 sources (S016, S026, S033). A local company he could watch fill up, unhurt by recessions and with no Korean imports or money-supply numbers to fret over. Lesson: the best stocks need no big-picture view. "I made 10 or 15 times my money in Dunkin' Donuts." (S016)

## 5. Stop & Shop: expected 30 percent, got 15-fold
Told in 2 sources (S021, S026). Bought as a conservative stock at a 7 percent yield for about 30 percent upside; the company kept improving (Bradlee's, Super Stop & Shops) and he held for 15-fold in 11 years. Lesson: let the category change if the company does; great stocks are a surprise. "But the company kept getting better and better and I held onto it." (S021)

## 6. McDonald's and the innings people miscounted
Told in 2 sources (S007, S029). People said McDonald's was done; it had 20 stores in France on the way to 400, and later 1,400. It was his biggest position and went up ten-fold more. Lesson: count the white space with facts. "They thought they were near the end, and they forgot about the other seven billion people in the world." (S029)

## 7. Taco Bell: a ten bagger cut short by a buyout
Told in 2 sources (S002, S007). One of his first Magellan stocks, a small restaurant chain nobody would look at; Pepsi bought it at 30 when it might have gone far higher. Lesson: takeouts cap the long tail; he repeats it about private equity in 2025 (S007).

## 8. Kaiser Industries: bought on the fall, saved by the assets
Told in 1 source (S016). He bought at 14 and 16 on "how much lower can it go"; it fell to 3. Only then did he see the whole company, with 40 percent of Kaiser Steel and no debt, was worth about the price of one 747; distributions came to about $50 a share. Lesson: a falling price means nothing unless you know the company; he calls himself "a little premature" (S016).

## 9. Polaroid and Avon: the bottom-fishing traps
Told in 2 sources (S016, S027). Polaroid went from 130 to 100, where people bought every share, then to 18 (S016). Avon went from 150 to 90 and then to 18 (S027). Lesson: overpriced great companies can fall a long way with no debt problem at all. "Well, it doesn't have to come back." (S016)

## 10. Long shots: 15 to 30 tries, never broke even
Told in 2 sources (S016, S025). Companies with huge promise and earnings a year or two away. Lesson: bite on results. "I might have done 15 or 20 of those in my life, and I don't think I've broken even in one of them" (S025)

## 11. General Public Utilities after Three Mile Island
Told in 2 sources (S018, S033). Bought two years after the accident, after it had already doubled, and still made about four times. Lesson: you can wait for proof. "I looked at General Public Utilities two years after Three Mile Island, it already doubled and I was able to make four times my money on it." (S033)

## 12. Textiles: recommended all the way down
Told in 2 sources (S026, S033). He followed the industry as it went from terrific to terrible. Lesson: a bad industry is not a buy until it gets better. "I saw the textile industry deteriorate. I was recommending all the stocks all the way down." (S026)

## 13. Sherwin-Williams, Starbucks and the misses from not doing an hour's work
Told in 2 sources (S007, S029). Sherwin-Williams earnings rose 20-fold after he managed the stock; the local painter goes there, not to Home Depot. Starbucks he missed while owning Dunkin. "So staggering, dumb, or just lazy. I thought it was the last inning of the ballgame, without doing the research." (S029) "Brain cramp. I didn't buy it." (S007)

## 14. Fannie Mae: the greatest single stock
Told in 2 sources (S016, S018). Still his largest position in 1994, with Freddie Mac added. Lesson: a great stock found in an unfashionable corner. "that was the greatest single stock of my life." (S016) The 2008 collapse, which he says he did not foresee (S017), sits beside it.

## 15. Apple and the iPod
Told in 1 source (S007). His daughters gave him an iPod; he saw the margins and the stock easily tripled. Lesson: companies change, so re-look at old names. "Something happened at Apple and they were now going to make a lot of money." (S007)

## 16. Bank of America in the financial crisis
Told in 1 source (S029). It fell from 18 to 7; he knew it could not fail because of its funding, and it rose a lot after. "I knew they couldn't go under because they were 120% retail funded." (S029)

## 17. Flying Tiger: right for the wrong reason
Told in 1 source (S002). His first stock, bought in college on an air freight thesis; the Vietnam War sent it up nine or ten-fold and it helped pay for graduate school. Lesson: he credits luck, not the thesis.

## 18. Barbra Streisand and the stocks she could not name
Told in 1 source (S007). She could not sleep because she owned stocks she could not describe. Lesson: if you do not know what you own, you will be shaken out. "If you don't understand what you own, you're toast." (S007)
