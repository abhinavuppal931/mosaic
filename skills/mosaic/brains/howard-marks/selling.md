# Selling: Howard Marks

Built from 105 Oaktree memos (S001 to S167, 1993 to 2026) through the verified batch notes. His rules for selling, trimming, adding and re-checking a position, most-used first. His method is a credit investor's and a cycle reader's: sell on price against value and on a broken case, lighten by degrees as the market heats up, and buy more when the price falls and the case holds. Where he changed his mind, both views are dated and the newer one is marked. Ideas he credits to his son Andrew are labelled; lines from others are not his.

## Rules

### 1. Sell for three reasons only: price reached value, the case broke, or something better
Sources: 5 (S023, S026, S036, S044, S068).
"We usually sell because (a) a holding has reached our target price, (b) the investment case has deteriorated, or (c) we've found something better." (S023) In 2015 he named the two valid reasons as deteriorated fundamentals or a price high enough, and called the rest emotion (S068, paraphrase). Selling is a judgment about price, not a forecast of the top: "I trust you sell because you think the selling price is adequate or more, not because you're convinced the price can never go higher." (S044) In 2022 he put it as holding while the earnings outlook and price attractiveness can be reconfirmed (S026, paraphrase). Watch the common bad reasons, an idea he credits to Andrew: "Two of the main reasons people sell stocks are because they go up and because they go down." (S068)

### 2. A falling price is a conviction test: re-check the case, then buy more if it holds
Sources: 9 (S023, S047, S067, S068, S072, S097, S120, S135, S138).
"If you liked it a month ago at $80, should you sell it now just because it's at $60?" (S068) The investor with alpha does the opposite of the crowd: "They buy with confidence in their analysis, and if the price of the asset falls, they tend to like it more, and buy rather than sell." (S138) Without a firm value estimate this fails: "If there's no conviction, a drop in the price of a holding can weaken the investor's faith in the estimate and make him fail to buy more, or maybe even sell" (S135) He admits it is harder than it sounds: "when prices collapse, the chance to average down is usually a lot less welcome" (S068) In 2002 Oaktree averaged down on Enron bonds as the whole distressed market fell (S120). The question after a drop is whether price now relates well to value, not where it goes next (S047, paraphrase).

### 3. Never become a forced seller
Sources: 8 (S018, S066, S069, S070, S072, S101, S121, S141).
A drop is only a permanent loss if you sell into it or the fundamentals cannot recover (S072, paraphrase). Good downside protection buys "enough resolve to be able to resist selling out at cyclical lows" (S121). Selling at a loss locks in permanent loss when it comes from lost conviction, a shortened time frame, exigency or emotion (S069, paraphrase). Leverage and redeemable capital are how a dip becomes ruin: lenders cut credit, investors withdraw, covenants force sales (S101, S018, paraphrase). In illiquid, levered vehicles, withdrawals force sale of the best assets first and leave holders with the worst (S066, paraphrase). Liquidity leaves just when it is needed: "Usually, just as a holder's desire to sell an asset increases (because he has become afraid to hold it), his ability to sell it decreases" (S070)

### 4. Lighten by degrees as optimism rises; never go all out or short
Sources: 9 (S009, S044, S054, S056, S057, S058, S069, S122, S167).
Trend is the trap: "price gains increase danger and price declines increase opportunity" (S167) His 2025 ladder, which he calls INVESTCON: stop buying, lighten the aggressive holdings, move toward defensives; since overvaluation is never the same as certain decline, he has never gone to selling everything or going short (S009, paraphrase). The cost of trimming early is small: "there may be relatively little to lose from the market continuing to grind higher for a while" (S009) In 2017 his warning meant "It's time for caution, as I wrote in the memo, not a full-scale exodus." (S057) Being cautious and fully invested beat both extremes in 2015 to 2018: "Being fully invested in a cautious portfolio has been an appropriate stance over the last few years." (S056)

### 5. Sell what the crowd now loves: when fear has turned to comfort
Sources: 7 (S070, S119, S130, S135, S139, S141, S143).
Oaktree sold Tumi in 2004 once nobody remembered the post-9/11 fear, sold theater chains as capacity shrank, and sold Chicago land when others decided more skyscrapers were needed (S119). For cyclicals, trim when the market forgets "cyclical companies whose peak earnings deserve trough valuations" (S143). Once a new thing ages, "when newness becomes old, flaws appear and investor ardor cools, the only thing that matters is the stock's price" (S141) The winners' rise itself cuts the forward return: "That bidding-up prolongs the outperformance somewhat, but it also reduces the prospective return and increases the probability of a correction." (S139) A rising price calls for a review of the holding, not a higher target (S135, paraphrase).

### 6. Respect your sell points
Sources: 2 (S057, S135).
"And if you continue to hold past your sell points, what does sell point mean?" (S057) In 2017 critics said to ride the market until a couple of big down days; he called such plans "either (a) absolutely illogical or (b) signs of the investor error and lack of discipline that are typical in bull markets." (S057) Price appreciation tempts holders to raise the target and buy more, which he treats as a trap (S135, paraphrase).

### 7. Expect to be early and to look wrong; do not capitulate
Sources: 12 (S028, S054, S056, S058, S073, S079, S106, S116, S143, S150, S151, S163).
"Most of the time, the hot asset you've sold will keep rising for a while, and sometimes a good while." (S028) "In investing we may have a sense for what's going to happen, but we never know when." (S054) Early has limits: "when you're early by six or more years, it's not clear you can ever be described as having been right" (S058) The danger is giving up at the worst moment: "I often think about the corrosive effect of being on the wrong side of a market judgment for prolonged periods, and the phenomenon through which those who resist trends the longest can finally capitulate at just the wrong time." (S150) "successful contrarianism requires the ability to stick with losing positions" (S079) An adage he was told early in his career (not his line): "being too far ahead of your time is indistinguishable from being wrong" (S163)

### 8. Great compounders are the exception to scaling out (changed in 2021)
Sources: 2 (S036, S068).
Older view: his value background biased him toward mean reversion and scaling out, and he coined a saying for it: "If you sell half, you can't be all wrong." (S036) Newer view, 2021-01, after Andrew's argument that a world-class company's potential cannot be quantified, so one should hold while the evidence supports it (Andrew's view, paraphrase): "cutting back a holding with great potential can be a life-altering mistake." (S036) The same point in 2015, an idea he credits to Andrew: "If you sell just because there's a downdraft (or an updraft), you'll never get that twenty-year winner." (S068) He notes Oaktree's credit asset classes offer few chances to sell mega-winners too early (S036).

### 9. When the price falls fast, ask whether facts or psychology changed
Sources: 7 (S016, S034, S047, S066, S086, S104, S167).
"Market participants have limited insight into what's really happening in terms of fundamentals" (S016) "Price declines cause fear, and thus further price declines." (S104) But a drop can carry information: "we should consider whether the market has discerned something that throws our prior understanding into question." (S034) A bad story falls further only if the story worsens or optimism abates, and with no optimism left the second cannot happen (S167, paraphrase). In January 2016, prices lower and optimism deflated, he moved to less caution (S066).

### 10. Do not sell after the damage is done
Sources: 4 (S044, S047, S094, S121).
In March 2020: "I never urged selling, as I thought a fair bit of the damage had been done." (S044) Selling after a sharp fall is the easy stance, but "there's no way to tell whether that pessimism is appropriate, inadequate or excessive." (S047) After a loss, investors retreat to safety just as the best buying opportunities appear (S121, paraphrase). In late 2008 the cry was to get out at any price; his stance was the opposite (S094).

### 11. The case breaks on things you cannot explain
Sources: 5 (S001, S005, S006, S024, S142).
Enron's holders ignored red flags: "And they remained unperturbed when the CEO quit without explanation." (S142) Can you say where the profits come from, and are the disclosures clear? Oaktree avoids banks partly because their disclosures are impenetrable (S024). With First Brands, Oaktree held a small position, dug deeper when flags appeared in early summer 2025, and checked the picture across its strategies before concluding (S006). On AI, the developments that would break a thesis are chip life, corporate strategy and demand for compute (S001), and a thesis built on continued leadership should be re-examined because new entrants can end a leader's reign (S005, paraphrase).

### 12. Re-underwrite when the regime changes
Sources: 4 (S021, S025, S092, S096).
"If this really is a sea change, meaning the investment environment has been fundamentally altered, you shouldn't assume" (S021) that what worked in 2009 to 2021 will keep working. In 2010 he applied it to himself: "But now that stock prices have drifted lower and bond prices have continued to surge, I find I must reconsider the emphasis on bonds." (S092) In January 2010 he weighed selling long-term bonds and holding cash to reinvest once rates rose (S096, paraphrase).

### 13. Re-check winners: skill, luck, leverage or the tide?
Sources: 6 (S020, S021, S117, S122, S123, S139).
After a great year ask how it was earned and whether the gains are real and realizable (S117, paraphrase). "High past returns don't imply high future returns; more likely, they've borrowed from the future." (S122) Those who profited from ownership and leverage in 2009 to 2021 may mistake the moving walkway for skill (S020, S021, paraphrase); the rule he invokes is an old one, "Never confuse brains and a bull market." (S021) One outcome proves little: "It's impossible to assess the accuracy of probability estimates other than zero and 100 except over a very large number of trials." (S123)

### 14. Do not fire an approach after a bad stretch; consider adding
Sources: 4 (S028, S073, S119, S141).
"highly disciplined managers who hold most rigorously to a given approach will tend to report the worst performance when that approach goes out of favor." (S028) "A lagging year or two doesn't make a manager a bad one ... maybe just one whose market niche has been in the process of getting cheap." (S119) "If you wait at a bus stop long enough, you're guaranteed to catch a bus, but if you run from bus stop to bus stop, you may never catch a bus." (S028)

## Mistakes he admits about selling and timing
- 2000-01, on his 1990s caution: "a single word suffices to describe my recent caution regarding the stock market: wrong." (S151) His 1997 warning took almost three years to come true (S056).
- 2004 to 2007: "I started complaining too soon" (S023); and on his 2004 memo, "That memo may have been too early, but it wasn't wrong." (S079)
- 2008 to 2010: expected investors to stay risk-averse for years; "Just a short while ago, I believed investors had been sufficiently traumatized that the willingness to bear risk would be absent for years." (S094)
- 2009 to 2019: "we paid a price for being cautious." (S044) In August 2020: "it certainly can't be said that caution regarding the damaged economy and elevated p/e ratios has been rewarded of late." (S038)
- On his bias, 2020-03: "On average I may have been more defensive than was necessary (although somehow I was able to shift to aggressive action when crisis lows were reached during my career)." (S045)
- 2021-01: scaling out of winners, now rejected (S036; rule 8).
- 2021-07, on himself: "The old me likely would have latched onto today's high valuations and instances of risky behavior to warn of a bubble and the subsequent correction." (S034) The newer lens does not cut market exposure on predictions (S034).

## Kill criteria this seat would write
Checkable facts that would end or shrink a thesis, in his terms (inferred from S001, S005, S006, S009, S018, S021, S023, S026, S051, S066, S069, S101, S142, S143 unless noted).
- The price has reached the intrinsic value estimate written at purchase, and nothing in the case has raised that estimate (S023, S044).
- The earnings outlook or the price attractiveness can no longer be reconfirmed (S026).
- A clearly better return for the risk is available elsewhere in the same book (S023).
- Holding at today's price requires believing "it's different this time": the multiple assumes the good times continue indefinitely, or a cyclical is priced on peak earnings as if they were permanent (S051, S143).
- You cannot say where the profits come from; obligations exceed disclosed debt; off-balance-sheet vehicles or receivables financing appear; the CEO leaves without explanation (S006, S142).
- Debt no longer fits the volatility of cash flows, or refinancing at today's rates leaves a hole (S018, S021).
- The holding or its vehicle could be forced to sell in a decline: margin, redemptions or covenant tests (S066, S069, S101).
- For a technology thesis: useful life of the key assets shortens, demand fails to justify capex, or the leader's position erodes (S001, S005).

## Re-check triggers
- Temperature readings: euphoria, meme stocks, "no price too high" talk, FOMO, unprofitable IPOs and SPACs (S009, S013, S030).
- Credit gauges: spreads near lows, CCC issuance, covenant-lite, PIK toggles, dividend recaps, hung bank debt (S009, S030, S079, S114).
- Lending standards and capital flows: too much money chasing too few deals; deals that would not get done in a prudent market (S054, S088).
- A sharp price fall: classify it as fundamentals or psychology before acting (S016, S066).
- A sharp price rise: review the holding; do not lift the target (S135).
- A rate-regime shift or a sea change in financing costs (S021, S025).
- Cockroaches: several idiosyncratic frauds or defaults in one corner of credit (S003, S006).
- A great year in a holding or manager: ask how it was achieved (S117).
- Red flags in a holding: dig deeper and cross-check before adding (S006).
- Before acting on a macro view: count how many forecasts must all be right (S027).
- Cadence: judge on the next five or ten years, not the last quarter (S026).
