# Frameworks: Howard Marks

Built from 105 Oaktree memos, February 1993 to November 2026 (see sources.md). Most-used first, weighted toward this seat's job: where we are in the cycle, what is priced in, and whether the investor is paid for the risk. Scoring lines are guidance drawn from his own numbers and thresholds, not a scorecard he published. Lines he quotes from others (Buffett, Graham, Galbraith, Templeton, Twain, his son Andrew, clients) and output he reports from AI tools are not quoted as his words.

## 1. The pendulum of investor psychology *(his name, first used in his April 1991 memo)*

- **What it is:** investor mood swings between greed and fear, optimism and pessimism, credulousness and skepticism, risk tolerance and risk aversion. It spends little time at the midpoint, and the move toward an extreme is what powers the swing back. Because feelings about risk swing so much, prices swing far more than fundamentals. Reality moves between pretty good and not so hot; perception moves between flawless and hopeless.
- **Parts:** (1) list the polar pairs (euphoria and depression, urgency to buy and panic to sell, overpriced and underpriced) (S114); (2) note which column today's behavior fits; (3) remember it is a swing, not a machine: "It's certainly not mechanical." (S029); (4) lean against the end it is near, without guessing when it turns.
- **His evidence:** over 40 years the standard deviation of annual change was 1.8% for GDP, 9.4% for corporate profits and 13.1% for the S&P 500 price (S017, using Conrad DeQuadros's data); ten high yield bonds yielded 45% in the 2002 meltdown and 6% a year later (S088).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** first, on every buy, add, hold or trim. Ask which end of the arc the stock, its sector and the market are near before asking anything about the company.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = the pendulum sits at the fearful end (pessimism, skepticism, sellers setting prices) and the price reflects it. 5 = near the midpoint, mixed mood. 0 = at the euphoric end: everyone sure, no skeptics, FOMO driving buyers.
- **Quote:** "The mood swings of the securities markets resemble the movement of a pendulum." (S016) Also: "whenever the pendulum is near either extreme, it is inevitable that it will move back toward the midpoint sooner or later." (S066) And: "it is the movement toward the extreme itself that supplies the energy for the swing back." (S114)
- **Why it ranks first:** "I probably write more about the pendulum of investor psychology than I do anything else." (S076)
- **Sources:** 20 (S003, S006, S009, S016, S017, S025, S029, S051, S066, S076, S079, S086, S088, S092, S097, S112, S114, S130, S143, S161).

## 2. Taking the temperature: know where we are *(his name)*

- **What it is:** a diagnosis of the present, not a forecast. Gather observable evidence about valuations, deal quality, terms and attitudes, and decide whether the market is in an extended state. It tells you the stage is being set, never the date.
- **Parts:** (1) the poor man's guide: paired columns (vibrant or sluggish economy, eager or reticent lenders, loose or tight capital markets, narrow or wide spreads, crowded or starved funds, GPs or LPs holding the cards) and count the checkmarks (S122, S029); (2) the four-part environment test he used for years: big uncertainties, low prospective returns, high prices, pro-risk behavior (S056, S058, S044); (3) the checklist for market sanity: are prospective returns adequate, are investors risk-averse and skeptical, are risk premiums present, how do valuations compare with history, are structures fair, are deals being declined, are there limits on faith in the future (S058); (4) the Martian test: describe today as a visitor would (S122); (5) anecdotes that only get done in a hot market (S023, S054).
- **Swap the question:** do not ask what inning it is, since nobody knows how long the game runs; ask whether things are depressed, average or euphoric (S060).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** before sizing. The same stock deserves a different weight when the checklist reads hot than when it reads cold.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = most checkmarks in the cautious column: spreads wide, lenders reticent, deals declined, investors fleeing. 5 = mixed. 0 = most checkmarks in the eager column: "hold on to your wallet" (S122).
- **Quote:** "we never know where we're going, but we ought to know where we are." (S025) Also: "Knowing where you are in a cycle and what that implies for the future is very different from predicting the timing, extent and shape of the next cyclical move." (S143) And: "I'd suggest investors ask whether things are or are not in an extended state." (S060)
- **Sources:** 15 (S023, S025, S029, S044, S054, S056, S057, S058, S060, S088, S094, S097, S114, S122, S143).

## 3. Excesses and corrections: cycles as a chain reaction *(his name)*

- **What it is:** cycles are not ups and downs around a trend but excesses and corrections, each event caused by the one before. Price rises above fair value, corrects through it, undershoots, and corrects again. Psychology supplies the excess.
- **Parts:** (1) the causal chain: rate cuts and easy money lift markets, which lowers prospective returns, which raises willingness to bear risk, which brings unwise decisions and losses, which brings fear and tight money, which brings rate cuts (S020); (2) the family of cycles: the economic cycle is gentle, the profit cycle is exaggerated by operating and financial leverage, the credit cycle swings between wide open and slammed shut, and the market cycle swings most because psychology magnifies all the others (S130, S114); (3) boom size sets bust size (S104, S116); (4) cycles also run in the long-term trend itself, not only around it (S100).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** to judge whether today's earnings and multiple are a peak, a trough or a norm. Cyclicals at peak earnings do not deserve peak multiples (S143).
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = the correction has run its course: losses taken, credit shut, prospective returns high. 5 = near trend. 0 = deep in the excess: returns far above profit growth, easy credit, the belief that cycles have ended.
- **Quote:** "a series of events, each of which causes the next." (S023) Also: "if there's one thing I believe in, perhaps more strongly than anything else, it's the fact that cycles will prevail and excesses will correct." (S110) And: "If you have a boom, eventually you'll have a bust. And the further the boom goes, the worse the bust is likely to be." (S104)
- **Sources:** 14 (S020, S023, S029, S030, S056, S079, S100, S104, S110, S114, S116, S130, S141, S143).

## 4. The three stages of a bull market, and of a bear market *(adage he learned in the early 1970s)*

- **What it is:** a gauge of psychology, not events. Bull: (1) a few forward-looking people think things will improve; (2) most investors see improvement under way; (3) everyone concludes things will get better forever. Bear: (1) a few prudent investors see the bullishness won't last; (2) most see deterioration; (3) everyone is convinced things can only get worse. Nothing in fundamentals has to change between stage one and stage three.
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** place the stock and its theme in a stage. Buying in bull stage one or bear stage three is high return and low risk; buying in bull stage three carries you over the cliff with the herd (S108).
- **His dated placements:** late 2008, bear stage three, buy (S104, S023); March 2012, contrarian bullishness (S167); March 2013, "somewhere in the first half of stage two" (S077); he notes the 2020 bull market had almost no first stage, so history does not repeat exactly (S030).
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = bear stage three, prices reflecting little or no optimism. 5 = bull stage two. 0 = bull stage three: everyone sure, too much confidence and too little cheapness.
- **Quote:** "it's great to be a buyer in the first stage and essential not to be in the last." (S066) Also: "The essential raw material for a bull market is cheapness" (S076) And: "there's too much confidence and too little cheapness." (S076)
- **Sources:** 12 (S013, S023, S030, S051, S066, S076, S077, S104, S108, S143, S163, S167).

## 5. Price versus intrinsic value, and the favorite versus the proposition *(the calculus of value, his name)*

- **What it is:** value is what you get, price is what you pay, and a good investment is one whose price is right for the value that turns out. Value is earning power discounted to today. Price swings far more than value because of psychology, so judge both the numbers and the mood. In betting terms, handicapping who will win is step one; whether the odds on offer are fair is the step that pays.
- **Parts:** (1) estimate value from earning power (earnings, growth, steadiness, assets, management, moat, balance sheet) (S009); (2) compare price with value, never price alone; (3) remember assets with no cash flow (gold, art) cannot be valued intrinsically (S009, S090); (4) value works like a magnet only in the long run; cheap can get cheaper (S009); (5) ask whether the odds are fair, not whether the company is great (S048).
- **His yardsticks:** a p/e of 16, the post-war average, pays for more than 20 years of earnings (S013); in J.P. Morgan's data a forward p/e near 22 to 23 was followed by ten-year returns between plus 2% and minus 2% a year every time (S013, S009); stocks rising 20% a year against roughly 7% profit growth become risky (S013).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** always. A great company at too high a price is a bad investment; a poor one at a low enough price can be a bargain.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = price well below a conservatively derived value, with the odds clearly generous. 5 = fair price for fair value. 0 = price assumes perfection; you are picking a favorite and ignoring the odds.
- **Quote:** "The most important thing, above all, is the relationship between price and value." (S135) Also: "It's not what you buy, it's what you pay that counts." (S013) And: "the goal is to figure out who the favorite is and whether the odds are fair or not." (S048)
- **Sources:** 17 (S001, S009, S013, S016, S036, S047, S048, S057, S067, S081, S088, S090, S092, S100, S135, S138, S151).

## 6. How much optimism is in the price: what is priced in *(unnamed)*

- **What it is:** the one fact he would want about any security. Price equals fundamentals plus how people view them. An event moves a price only by how it compares with what was expected, so a coming negative is no reason to cut risk unless the price ignores it.
- **Parts:** (1) state the consensus; (2) ask what the price assumes; (3) compare the event with expectations, not with zero (S026); (4) after a fall, ask whether the move is proportional to the change in fundamentals (S047); (5) do not price a good event twice, once when it is rumored and again when it happens (S016); (6) a stock priced for perfection is hurt most by a catalyst nobody saw (S047, S086).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** whenever the case is "the news is good" or "the news is bad". Good news already in the price is not a reason to buy; bad news already in the price is not a reason to sell.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = little or no optimism in the price; the bad case is mostly reflected. 5 = price fair for the optimistic case but little room for worse news (his March 2020 reading, S045). 0 = price incorporates unlimited virtue.
- **Quote:** "If I could know only one thing about an investment I'm contemplating, it might be how much optimism is embodied in the price." (S066) Also: "it's not whether the event is simply positive or not, but how the event compares with what was expected." (S026) And: "It's not what's going on; it's how it's priced." (S057)
- **Warning sign:** "when prices incorporate unlimited virtue, the eventual result is bound to be disappointment, disillusionment and depreciation." (S086)
- **Sources:** 12 (S001, S016, S026, S028, S029, S045, S047, S057, S060, S066, S086, S167).

## 7. Second-level thinking *(his name; taught in his book The Most Important Thing and in memos)*

- **What it is:** thinking that is different from the consensus and better. First-level thinking says a good company is a good buy. Second-level thinking asks what everyone else thinks, what is in the price, and what happens if they are right or you are.
- **Parts (his question list):** (1) what is the range of likely outcomes; (2) which do I think will occur; (3) what is the probability I'm right; (4) what does the consensus think; (5) how does my expectation differ; (6) how does the current price fit the consensus view and mine; (7) is the consensus psychology in the price too bullish or too bearish; (8) what happens to the price if the consensus is right, and if I am (S028, S068).
- **The everyone-knows test:** if everyone believes it is a bargain, the crowd has already bought it up (S067).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** to test any thesis built on widely known facts. In credit, superior analysis is second-level thinking built on a mosaic of small inferences, not one aha moment (S006).
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = a specific, reasoned difference from the consensus that the price does not reflect. 5 = agrees with consensus at a fair price. 0 = first-level reasoning: good company, so buy.
- **Quote:** "In order to outperform, your thinking has to be different and better." (S028) Also: "Different and better: that's a pretty good description of second-level thinking." (S068) And his example of the second level: "It's a good company, but everyone thinks it's a great company, and it's not. So the stock's overrated and overpriced; let's sell." (S068)
- **Sources:** 8 (S006, S023, S028, S048, S067, S068, S106, S141).

## 8. Risk is the probability of permanent loss, not volatility *(his definition)*

- **What it is:** investors fear losing money or earning too little, not price wiggles. Volatility is at most a symptom. Permanent loss comes two ways: a temporary dip locked in by selling (lost conviction, short time frame, need for cash, emotion), or an asset that cannot recover. Within that, fundamental risk (the asset performs badly) and valuation risk (you paid too much) are the two main ways to fail, and valuation risk is under your control.
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** ask how this position could become a permanent loss, and whether you or your circumstances (leverage, redemptions, a need for cash) could turn a dip into one. A missing mark is not missing risk (S003).
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = a low price relative to value and staying power, so a dip stays a dip. 5 = fair price, some way to be forced out. 0 = overpaid, and a fall could force a sale.
- **Quote:** "I define risk as the probability of a bad outcome, and volatility is, at best, an indicator of the presence of risk. But volatility is not risk." (S026) Also: "We can ride out volatility, but we never get a chance to undo a permanent loss." (S069) And: "At bottom, the riskiest thing is overpaying for an asset (regardless of its quality), and the best way to reduce risk is by paying a price that's irrationally low (ditto)." (S069)
- **Latest restatement (April 2026, private credit):** "I insist strenuously that risk and volatility aren't the same thing." (S003)
- **Sources:** 8 (S003, S026, S069, S072, S079, S092, S121, S123).

## 9. Are we paid for the risk: is the risk premium adequate? *(unnamed; his "essential bottom line")*

- **What it is:** riskier assets must only appear to offer more, or nobody would buy them. Whether you are paid for bearing risk depends on how risk-averse others are. When the risk-free rate falls, the whole capital market line drops; when risk aversion is low, the line flattens and extra risk earns little extra return.
- **Parts:** (1) set the risk-free rate; (2) compare the asset's prospective return with it (earnings yield versus Treasury yield, spread versus default losses); (3) ask which risks you are compensated for and which you merely assume (S001); (4) judge in absolute terms, not relative to peers (S058); (5) demand premiums but do not count on receiving them (S124).
- **His yardsticks:** high yield spreads normally 350 to 550 bp; 242 bp at the June 2007 peak; 1,773 bp at the December 2008 trough (S088). Earnings yield over the risk-free rate: 325 bp at a p/e of 16 and a 3% risk-free rate; 112 bp in 2000 at a p/e of 32; 525 bp in March 2013 (S077).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** after valuation. A stock can be fairly valued and still not pay enough over safer alternatives for the uncertainty it carries. Uncertainty is acceptable if price is low enough (S001).
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = prospective return well above the risk-free rate and above what the uncertainty demands; spreads wide. 5 = premium near historical norms. 0 = premium thin or gone because risk aversion has vanished.
- **Quote:** "The essential bottom line in all investing is simple: is the risk premium at least adequate?" (S058) Also: "Prospective return exists to compensate for perceived risk, and when there isn't much perceived risk, there isn't likely to be much prospective return." (S079) And: "In my view, when uncertainty is high, asset prices should be low, creating high prospective returns that are compensatory." (S037)
- **Sources:** 13 (S001, S019, S037, S058, S077, S079, S088, S111, S118, S123, S124, S130, S161).

## 10. The future as a probability distribution *(unnamed; "more things can happen than will happen" is Elroy Dimson's line, which he adopts)*

- **What it is:** view the future as a range of outcomes with probabilities, not a point forecast. Knowing the probabilities is not knowing the outcome, and only one thing will happen. Riskier assets have wider distributions with worse bad outcomes, not just higher averages. Risk cannot be measured even after the fact.
- **Parts:** (1) sketch the range, including the left tail; (2) attach rough probabilities; (3) reject any path with an outcome you cannot survive, even if its expected value is highest; (4) prepare for once-in-a-generation events, not the absolute worst case (S101).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** before sizing. The skydiver rule applies: a high expected value does not justify a small chance of ruin.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = the bad outcomes are survivable and the range skews right. 5 = symmetric range, tolerable left tail. 0 = an unacceptable outcome sits in the range, however unlikely.
- **Quote:** "Even though many things can happen, only one will." (S072) Also: "I always say I have no interest in being a skydiver who's successful 95% of the time." (S069) And: "The fact that something happened doesn't mean it was likely, and the fact that something didn't happen doesn't mean it was improbable." (S123)
- **Sources:** 10 (S069, S072, S073, S086, S096, S097, S101, S123, S137, S167).

## 11. The perversity of risk *(his name)*

- **What it is:** risk rises when people think it is low and falls when they think it is high. A market that feels safe becomes unsafe because of the risk-taking the feeling invites; a frightening one becomes safer because caution lowers prices.
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** when the case is "there is nothing that could go wrong" or "everyone knows it is a safe compounder". The absence of a visible catalyst is a reason for more care, not less.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = risk consciousness high, investors cautious, prices discount the danger. 5 = normal worry. 0 = widespread belief there is no risk.
- **Quote:** "the riskiest thing in the world is the widespread belief that there's no risk." (S068) Also: "Simply put, risk is low when risk aversion and risk consciousness are high, and high when they're low." (S072) And: "But it's precisely when people can't see what it is that could make things turn down that risk is highest, since they tend not to price in risks they can't see." (S058)
- **Sources:** 10 (S013, S058, S068, S069, S072, S076, S079, S081, S094, S097).

## 12. The twin risks: losing money versus missing opportunity *(unnamed)*

- **What it is:** every investor faces two risks. You can eliminate either one, but only by taking on the other fully. Which one the crowd fears tells you where the pendulum is. In bull markets the real risk is losing money; in bear markets it is missing opportunity.
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** ask which risk the investor is reacting to. A decision driven by fear of missing out at high prices is the classic late-cycle error; a refusal to buy after a crash is the classic bottom error.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = the crowd is obsessed with losing money and prices reflect it. 5 = balanced. 0 = the crowd thinks the worst outcome is missing the gains.
- **Quote:** "You can completely avoid one or the other, or you can compromise between the two, but you can't eliminate both." (S094) Also: "In other words, should we worry more today about losing money or about missing opportunity?" (S058) And: "When most people think the worst imaginable outcome is failing to participate fully in gains, the result is risky behavior." (S097)
- **Sources:** 9 (S044, S058, S072, S077, S081, S088, S094, S097, S098).

## 13. Calibrating offense and defense: the dial, not the switch *(includes INVESTCON, his name, and the normal risk posture)*

- **What it is:** for the intermediate term, the key decision is the balance between aggressiveness and defensiveness, set along a continuum by current conditions, not by forecasts. Each investor has a normal risk posture (Oaktree's leans defensive) and moves from it only when conditions are extreme and the odds of being right are high.
- **Parts:** (1) set your normal posture from your finances, needs and tolerance (S026); (2) move toward offense as prices fall relative to value and others turn prudent, toward defense as prices rise and others embrace risk (S044); (3) adjust gradually, never all in or all out; (4) INVESTCON ladder as valuations rise: 6 stop buying; 5 reduce aggressive holdings and increase defensive ones; 4 sell the remaining aggressive holdings; 3 trim defensive holdings; 2 eliminate all holdings; 1 go short. He says he has never gone to the extremes (S009).
- **His dated settings:** "move forward, but with caution" from mid-2011, eased to "a little less caution" in January 2016 (S066); the same caution through 2017 and 2018 (S056, S057); a turn toward buying in March 2020 as prices fell (S044); INVESTCON 5 in August 2025 (S009).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** to turn a cycle reading into a size. The answer is rarely buy or sell; it is more or less, and which flavor (aggressive or defensive version of the same exposure).
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = conditions justify leaning to offense: low prices, chastened investors. 5 = normal posture. 0 = conditions call for INVESTCON 5 or lower.
- **Quote:** "The most important job is to strike the appropriate balance between offense and defense." (S044) Also: "this process is one of gradual readjustment, not a matter of all-or-nothing." (S044) And: "I consider tactical actions in terms of the spectrum that runs from aggressiveness to defensiveness, and when valuations are high, I consider becoming more defensive." (S009)
- **Sources:** 13 (S009, S023, S026, S028, S034, S037, S044, S054, S056, S057, S058, S066, S081).

## 14. Asymmetry and defensive investing *(unnamed; his test of real skill)*

- **What it is:** excellence is not high return but asymmetry: capturing more of the upside than the downside. Defensive investing means insisting on value at a bargain price, diversifying, avoiding macro bets and market timing, and aiming to be average in good times and far better in bad ones. Avoid the losers and the winners take care of themselves.
- **Parts:** his Manager A to G table with the market up or down 10%: A (+10/-10) adds nothing; B and C are just low or high beta; D (+17/-12) is aggressive with alpha; E (+9/-3) is defensive with alpha; F (+20/-5) beats both ways; G (+20/+5) is so rare it needs extra due diligence (S026). Signs of asymmetry: more made in good markets than given back in bad, more and bigger winners than losers, surprises mostly on the upside (S026).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** ask what the position does in the bad case relative to the good one. Pay up, in fees or in price, only for asymmetry (S129).
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = clear upside with a limited, identifiable downside. 5 = symmetric bet. 0 = high return only if everything goes right, heavy loss if not.
- **Quote:** "The goal in investing is asymmetry: to expose yourself to return in a way that doesn't expose you commensurately to risk" (S073) Also: "If we avoid the losers, the winners will take care of themselves." (S135) And Oaktree's two-part goal: "There is a time when it's essential that we outperform, and that's in falling markets." (S121)
- **Sources:** 10 (S019, S026, S054, S069, S073, S121, S129, S135, S138, S161).

## 15. The "I know" school versus the "I don't know" school *(his names, from 2001 and 2004 memos)*

- **What it is:** the first choice an investor makes is whether the future is knowable. The "I know" school forecasts the economy, rates and markets, invests aggressively and concentrated, and rarely checks its record. The "I don't know" school thinks the macro future is important but not knowable, studies the micro, and invests defensively. He also applies Buffett's test that information must be important and knowable; he judges the macro important but not knowable (S034, S060).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** when the thesis depends on a macro call (rates, recession, a policy outcome). Strip out the macro, ask what you know about the company that others don't, and invest on that.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = the case rests on knowable, company-level facts and a price that allows for macro surprise. 5 = some macro dependence, hedged by price. 0 = the case only works if a macro forecast is right.
- **Quote:** "you can't know the future; you don't have to know the future; and the proper goal is to do the best possible job of investing in the absence of that knowledge." (S034) Also: "Thus one key question each investor has to answer is whether he views the future as knowable or unknowable." (S097) And: "If you know what lies ahead, you'll feel free to invest aggressively" (S135)
- **Sources:** 10 (S023, S027, S034, S060, S097, S135, S137, S141, S157, S163).

## 16. Forecasting arithmetic: forecast plus probability, compounding odds, consensus payoff *(unnamed; "single-scenario forecasting" is his name)*

- **What it is:** a forecast is worth only its payoff times its chance of being right. A thesis built on several links needs all of them right, and the odds multiply down. Consensus forecasts are usually right but already priced; forecasts of big deviation would pay but are rarely right. Know which layer you are on: facts, inferences or guesses (his version of Marc Lipsitch's hierarchy, S042).
- **Parts:** (1) list every link the thesis needs; (2) give each a probability; five calls at 67% leave about 13% (S163); four at 60% leave about 13% (S001); (3) ask whether your view differs from the consensus in prices and why you would be right; (4) ask why a valuable forecast is being given to you (S137); (5) his 2026 test for AI: guess a company's EPS in five years and ask whether you would bet it lands within plus or minus 50% (S001).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** on any story stock, any top-down thesis, and any long-duration growth case where most of the value lies years out.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = few things have to go right, and the probabilities are known with some confidence. 5 = a handful of links, each likely. 0 = a long chain of guesses, or a non-consensus forecast held as certain.
- **Quote:** "a forecast isn't enough. In addition to a forecast, you also need a good sense for the probability your forecast is correct, since not all forecasts are created equal." (S011) Also: "If they have a 60% probability of getting each one right, which is probably generous, what's the probability of getting all four right?" (S001) And: "To bring above average profits, a forecast generally must be different from the consensus and accurate." (S157)
- **Sources:** 13 (S001, S004, S011, S017, S026, S027, S042, S047, S082, S137, S141, S157, S163).

## 17. The credit cycle and the supply of capital *(includes "the race to the bottom", his title)*

- **What it is:** small changes in the economy produce big swings in credit availability. When capital is plentiful, money is a commodity, and providers compete by accepting lower returns, weaker terms and less safety; the deal goes to whoever accepts the least. Too much money chasing too few deals means low returns and rising risk. When the window slams shut, even good borrowers are refused, and bargains appear.
- **Parts:** (1) watch supply and demand for capital in your field before the economy (S143); (2) watch terms: covenant-lite share, PIK toggles, dividend recaps, CCC issuance, EBITDA add-backs (S054, S088, S094); (3) the race to the bottom in yields: 8% with strong covenants, then 7% with fewer, then 6% with none (S030); (4) read a wave of debt-for-dividend deals as a cycle signal (S130).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** for any company that depends on refinancing, any lender, and any sector being flooded with capital. Ask whether the business needs the window open to survive.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = the window is shut, lenders demand safety, and the company does not need them. 5 = normal terms. 0 = covenants gone, anyone can raise money for anything, and the thesis depends on that continuing.
- **Quote:** "The longer I'm involved in investing, the more impressed I am by the power of the credit cycle." (S020) Also: "Prosperity brings expanded lending, which leads to unwise lending, which produces large losses, which makes lenders stop lending, which ends prosperity, and on and on." (S143) And: "That's why the level of covenant protection is a good barometer of the market climate." (S116)
- **Sources:** 16 (S003, S006, S020, S030, S054, S088, S094, S107, S114, S116, S118, S124, S129, S130, S143, S151).

## 18. Volatility + Leverage = Dynamite, and the mismatch *(his formula, first proposed April 1994)*

- **What it is:** leverage magnifies gains and losses and adds no value, but it carries a risk of ruin with no matching upside. The prudent amount is set by how volatile the assets are, not by what lenders will give. The deadliest combination is leverage plus an asset/liability mismatch: borrowing short to hold long or illiquid assets. Survival on average is useless.
- **Parts:** (1) risk equals what you buy plus how you finance it (S108); (2) optimize leverage, do not maximize it (S018); (3) separate permanent capital from capital that can be withdrawn or margin-called (S101); (4) the four dials for any debt: share of debt in the capital structure, quality of the assets or cash flows lent against, the borrower's other sources of liquidity, and the lender's safety margin (S005); (5) liquidity is situational: ask who else holds the asset and whether they could all sell at once (S070).
- **His numbers:** at 25 times equity, a 4% price fall wipes you out (S153); highly levered companies get into trouble when growth falls below 2% (S110).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** on any levered company, any financial, and any investor using margin. Ask whether the structure survives the low point, not the average.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = little or no debt, or debt sized to stable assets with long maturities. 5 = moderate leverage on cautious assumptions. 0 = high leverage on volatile assets, or short funding against long assets.
- **Quote:** "The more stable the assets, the more leverage it's safe to use. Riskier assets, less leverage. It's that simple." (S101) Also: "There's no source of meltdown - in any sector - as potentially toxic as the combination of high leverage and an asset/liability mismatch." (S024) And: "Surviving on average is a useless concept; you have to be able to survive all the time, including - no, especially - in bad times." (S024)
- **Sources:** 16 (S005, S018, S020, S024, S045, S055, S070, S097, S100, S101, S108, S110, S112, S117, S135, S153).

## 19. Diagnosing a bubble: psychology first *(unnamed; includes "the seeds for a boom" and "lottery-ticket thinking", his names)*

- **What it is:** a bubble is a state of mind more than a calculation. It starts with something new and real (a grain of truth), early investors win, envy and FOMO pull in the rest, possibility becomes probability and then certainty, and nobody asks what price is safe. Newness removes the tether of history. Lottery-ticket thinking says a tiny chance at an enormous payoff compels participation.
- **Parts:** (1) the nine seeds for a boom: benign environment, grain of truth, early success, more money than ideas, willing suspension of disbelief, rejection of valuation norms, pursuit of the new, a virtuous circle, FOMO (S058); (2) the hallmark phrases: no price too high, this time it's different (S037, S051); (3) separate company behavior from investor behavior (S005); (4) for a new technology ask whether the technology is overhyped, whether spending will earn a return, and whether investors are paying too much (S001, S004).
- **His dated AI stances:** January 2025, frothy but not nutty, with a p/e high but not insane (S013); December 2025, no bubble would be a first, a moderate position applied with selectivity (S005); February 2026, the technology is very real but infrastructure returns cannot be judged yet (S004); November 2026, view little changed, and he calls AI the most consequential and least predictable development for investors in a generation (S001).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** on any stock carried by a new theme. A real technology does not make the stock cheap.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = the theme is out of favor and priced for failure. 5 = enthusiasm with valuations high but not insane. 0 = no price too high, every contender assumed to win, new metrics invented because earnings don't exist.
- **Quote:** "So, to discern a bubble, you can look at valuation parameters, but I've long believed a psychological diagnosis is more effective." (S013) Also: "Market bubbles aren't caused directly by technological or financial developments. Rather, they result from the application of excessive optimism to those developments." (S005) And: "Buying into a fad regardless of price is the absolute hallmark of a bubble." (S058)
- **Lottery tickets:** "the dream of an enormous payoff justifies, no, compels, participation in an endeavor with an overwhelming probability of failing." (S005)
- **Sources:** 14 (S001, S003, S004, S005, S013, S023, S037, S051, S057, S058, S092, S097, S122, S151).

## 20. Interest rates, the moving walkway and the sea change *(his names)*

- **What it is:** the risk-free rate resets the return on everything. Forty years of falling rates (his bank loan went from 22.25% in 1980 to 2.25% in 2020, S003) lifted asset prices and carried investors along like a moving walkway, rewarding owners and leveraged buyers and starving lenders and bargain hunters. In December 2022 he called a sea change: rates more normal, credit offering equity-like contractual returns.
- **Parts:** (1) compare earnings yield with the Treasury yield; an earnings yield of 6.5% means a p/e of 15.4 and 4.5% means 22.2 (S037); (2) ask how much of a past return came from falling rates; (3) the leveraged buyer's arithmetic: a 10% asset yield, 75% borrowed at 8%, gives 16% on equity, and refinancing at 5% gives 25% before any business gain (S025); (4) low rates push savers out the risk curve against their will (S020); (5) long-duration assets, including growth stocks, rise most with easy money and fall most when it ends (S020).
- **Dated stances:** July 2021, prices reasonable relative to rates, but only if rates stayed low (S034); December 2022 and October 2023, sea change, reallocate toward credit, since high yield above 8% and leveraged loans near 10% approached the stock market's century-long return of about 10% (S025, S021); January 2024, rates unlikely to return to ultra-low (S020).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** to ask whether a valuation needs low rates to hold, and whether the stock's record was made on the walkway.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = the valuation works at normal rates and the return is not a gift of falling rates. 5 = some rate dependence. 0 = only works if rates fall or stay ultra-low.
- **Quote:** "I consider it nearly impossible to overstate the influence of declining rates over the last four decades." (S025) Also: "Changing the risk-free rate has the potential to reset the returns on everything." (S037) And: "you shouldn't assume the investment strategies that have served you best since 2009 will do so in the years ahead." (S021)
- **Sources:** 12 (S003, S020, S021, S025, S034, S037, S038, S077, S079, S094, S096, S161).

## 21. Intelligent contrarianism and the 2x2 of conventional behavior *(his names)*

- **What it is:** conventional behavior gives average results, good or bad; only unconventional behavior can beat the average, and only superior judgment makes the difference favorable. At extremes most people are wrong, so success lies in diverging. But contrarianism is not doing the opposite by reflex: understand what the herd is doing, why, what is wrong with it, and what to do instead.
- **Parts:** (1) the four steps above (S023, S028); (2) dare to be different, to be wrong, and to look wrong for a while (S073); (3) it works best at extremes, and most of the time there is nothing dramatic to do (S068); (4) over three to five years, whether you were countercyclical or procyclical may matter more than selection or allocation (S097).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** when a stock is loved or hated by everyone. A contrarian position needs a reason the crowd is wrong, not just that it is a crowd.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = the position diverges from a crowd at an extreme, with a clear reason. 5 = no strong crowd view either way. 0 = joining the herd near its peak, or opposing it with no reason.
- **Quote:** "You can't take the same actions as everyone else and expect to outperform." (S028) Also: "you have to understand (a) what the herd is doing, (b) why it's doing it, (c) what's wrong with it, and (d) what should be done instead and why." (S023) And: "Contrarianism is most effective at the extremes, and then only for those who understand what the herd is doing and why it's wrong." (S068)
- **Sources:** 12 (S023, S028, S042, S066, S068, S073, S079, S097, S112, S119, S130, S141).

## 22. Efficiency as a rebuttable presumption, and active investing as the search for mistakes *("rebuttable presumption" is Bruce Karsh's term; the rest his)*

- **What it is:** start by assuming a market is efficient: price is as close to right as participants can get. The burden of proof is on anyone who claims a bargain nobody else sees. For one side of a trade to win big, the other must have erred, so name the mistake. Few markets are structurally inefficient now, but many are cyclically inefficient when emotion takes over.
- **Parts:** (1) what mistake is the seller making, and why is it not you (S082, S109); (2) is the inefficiency structural or cyclical (S074); (3) where will an edge survive cheap data and AI: in judging what information means, assessing qualitative factors, and divining the future (S004, S036); (4) go where others won't, after making sure you know the hazards (S139).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** on any claim that a widely followed large cap is mispriced. The more followed the stock, the more specific the claimed mistake has to be.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = a named mistake by sellers, in a neglected or forced-selling corner. 5 = a plausible but common view. 0 = no account of why the price is wrong.
- **Quote:** "The burden of proof should be on anyone who thinks a market provides underpriced investments that no one else is smart enough to detect and pursue." (S074) Also: "Active management has to be seen as the search for mistakes." (S082) And: "while few markets demonstrate great structural inefficiency today, many exhibit a great deal of cyclical inefficiency from time to time." (S074)
- **Sources:** 13 (S004, S029, S036, S048, S055, S074, S082, S109, S121, S135, S138, S139, S163).

## 23. Judge the decision, not the outcome *(credited to C. Jackson Grayson and Nassim Taleb's alternative histories)*

- **What it is:** a good decision is the best one available when it was made, with the future unknown. Missing information and luck distort outcomes, and over a few trials the skilled and the lucky look alike. Judge by process, the a priori odds, the alternatives, and the other things that could reasonably have happened.
- **Parts:** (1) quality of the process; (2) probability it would work; (3) the alternatives; (4) alternative histories; (5) which choice had the best odds (S067). Being early looks the same as being wrong for a while (S073, S079).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** when a past winner is cited as proof of skill, or a past loser as proof of error. Also when a bull market makes everyone look smart.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = the reasoning holds at the time of decision, whatever the next quarter does. 5 = sound reasoning with untested assumptions. 0 = the main evidence is that it has worked lately.
- **Quote:** "you can't tell the quality of a decision from the outcome." (S048) Also: "In the long run, superior skill will overcome the impact of bad luck. But in the short run, luck can overwhelm skill, and the two can be indistinguishable." (S048) And: "A good decision is one that's optimal at the time it's made, when the future is by definition unknown." (S138)
- **Sources:** 12 (S017, S020, S021, S026, S048, S056, S067, S068, S074, S117, S123, S138).

## 24. Five questions for any investment *(his closing list, November 2026)*

- **What it is:** his newest checklist, offered for AI but, in his words, for all investments. It gathers the seat's main tests in one place (inferred from S001, S058, S066, S069, S163).
- **Parts:** (1) how many things have to go right for an acceptable return; (2) if the central assumptions are wrong, how much could I lose; (3) which risks am I paid for and which am I merely assuming; (4) how much optimism is already in the price; (5) does this amplify or diversify the risk the portfolio already carries (S001).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** as the closing check before any buy or add.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = few things must go right, the loss if wrong is bearable, the risks are paid for, little optimism is in the price, and it diversifies. 0 = the reverse on most counts.
- **Quote:** "How many things have to go right for me to earn an acceptable return?" (S001) Also: "Which risks am I being compensated for, and which am I simply assuming?" (S001)
- **Sources:** 1 (S001), drawing on ideas used across the corpus.
