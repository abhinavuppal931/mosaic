# Stories: Howard Marks

Built from 105 Oaktree memos (S001 to S167, 1993 to 2026) through the verified batch notes. These are the episodes he retells, most-used first, with his mistakes and early calls given as much room as his wins. Figures are as he told them; where tellings differ, the range is given. Stories about other people (Buffett, Prince, Robertson, Paulson) are his retellings, and their words are not his.

## 1. The Nifty Fifty, 1969: great companies, terrible investments
Sources: 17 (S005, S009, S013, S025, S028, S033, S036, S037, S038, S048, S068, S069, S072, S079, S092, S121, S141).
He started at First National City Bank in 1969, when the bank owned the one-decision growth stocks on the belief that nothing bad could happen and no price was too high, at 60 to 90 times earnings (S009, S013, S068). Multiples fell to single digits in 1973 to 1974 (S028, S079). "if you bought these stocks on the day I started work and held them tenaciously for five years, you lost well over 90% of your money" (S013) Coca-Cola at 46 times earnings in 1972 fell 65% in 18 months (S036). The bust cost him his job as director of equity research and pushed him into convertibles and high yield, which he calls a lucky break (S036). Nuance he adds in 2021: about half the group later compiled respectable 25-year returns even from the highs (S036).
Lesson: price beats quality. "Perceived quality, it turned out, wasn't synonymous with safety or with successful investment." (S025)

## 2. bubble.com, January 2000: right, and right fast, after years of being wrong
Sources: 11 (S005, S009, S013, S020, S023, S056, S058, S143, S150, S151, S029).
After ten years of memos with no reply, he wrote bubble.com on the first business day of 2000, having just read Chancellor's Devil Take the Hindmost (S020, S023, S058). "The memo had two things going for it: it was right, and it was right fast." (S013) Oaktree had little tech exposure; the S&P 500 fell 46% and the NASDAQ 80% into 2002 (S023). The same memo opened with an admission about his 1990s caution: "a single word suffices to describe my recent caution regarding the stock market: wrong." (S151) His 1997 warning took almost three years to be proved right (S056). He had no tech expertise: "All I did was render observations regarding the behavior taking place around me." (S005)
Lesson: read behavior, not the technology; and the warning is usually early. He watched holdouts like Tiger capitulate near the top (S150).

## 3. 2004 to 2007: too early, then right, on the race to the bottom
Sources: 10 (S003, S023, S029, S054, S057, S058, S060, S079, S107, S116).
From 2004 he and Bruce Karsh saw deals that "wouldn't be doable in a market marked by appropriate levels of caution" (S054), so they raised standards, sold assets, liquidated large funds and organized small ones (S023). "I started complaining too soon" (S023) His 2004 memo on lower prospective returns: "That memo may have been too early, but it wasn't wrong." (S079) A 2006 FT article on UK lenders moving from 3.5 to 5 times salary led to The Race to the Bottom in February 2007 (S029, S116). Oaktree formed a distressed fund of nearly $11 billion and held it in reserve; the pain began in September 2008 (S054). He did not foresee the trigger: "I can assure you we had no idea that sub-prime mortgages and sub-prime mortgage backed securities would go bad in huge numbers" (S054)
Lesson: you do not need to name the catalyst. "Thus the imprudent deals that were getting done in 2005-06 were reason enough for us to increase our caution." (S054)

## 4. After Lehman, late 2008: buying while nobody knows
Sources: 11 (S011, S023, S029, S042, S044, S047, S057, S088, S094, S097, S104).
Four days after Lehman failed he wrote Nobody Knows and urged buying at deep discounts although, in his words, "Nobody knew, especially me" (S011) whether the spiral would stop. Karsh's team invested about $400 to $450 million a week over the last 15 weeks of 2008, some $6 to $7 billion, worrying "too slow" one day and "too fast" the next (S023, S042, S044). Prices kept falling into early 2009, but supply dried up after year-end, so waiting for the bottom would have missed it (S044). High yield yielded about 19% and first lien loans 18% with few buyers (S094). The panic window lasted only about fifteen weeks (S088). "that period represented one of the greatest buying opportunities I've ever seen." (S097)
Lesson: when risks are on the table and priced in, buy without waiting for clarity; the bottom is only visible afterward.

## 5. 1978: high yield, and owning what nobody wanted
Sources: 12 (S003, S005, S021, S025, S028, S037, S048, S074, S079, S119, S123, S137).
Citibank asked him to start convertible and high yield funds in 1978, when institutions were barred from sub-investment grade bonds and the market was about $2 billion (S025, S119). Leaving equity research, he told Citibank "I'll do anything but spend the rest of my life choosing between Merck and Lilly." (S074) A dollar invested at the end of 1979 grew to more than $23 and was never in the red (S079). Asked how he could buy bonds of issuers that would go bankrupt, he pointed to life insurers, who know everyone will die (S123). He admits defaults every year: "we've had defaults in our high yield bond portfolios nearly every year since I started the effort 48 years ago" (S003)
Lesson: it is not what you buy but what you pay; the unseemly and the shunned are where risk is overpaid (S048, S028).

## 6. The Death of Equities, 1979, and the re-read in 2012
Sources: 6 (S023, S077, S092, S100, S163, S167).
BusinessWeek's August 1979 cover marked the bottom of a lost decade; the S&P 500 then returned roughly 18% a year for twenty years (S023, S167). He became a portfolio manager in mid-1978 near that sentiment low: "In the investment world, lows in sentiment usually coincide with lows in price, and the late Seventies were no exception." (S092) He admits he did not call it then, after only a decade in the business (S023). Re-reading the article on a sleepless night in 2012 led to Deja Vu All Over Again (S167).
Lesson: "The extrapolator threw in the towel on stocks, just as the time was right for the contrarian to turn optimistic." (S077)

## 7. March 2020: four memos in four weeks
Sources: 8 (S011, S023, S030, S034, S037, S044, S045, S047).
The S&P 500 fell from 3,386 to 2,237 in a month (S023). On March 3 he said it was "probably a time to buy" and to spend part of the cash (S047); on March 19 he firmed up; on March 31, after the best three days since the 1930s, he expected prices to fall again (S044, S045). He admits the message varied as prices did (S044). The Fed and Treasury ended the crisis so fast that bargains were few and the bull market skipped its early stages (S030, S037).
Lesson: calibrate to price day by day, buy some, keep some, and accept that "the probability distribution governing future events is much wider and the tails much fatter." (S044)

## 8. Long-Term Capital Management, 1998: genius is not enough
Sources: 9 (S055, S069, S072, S104, S108, S117, S123, S141, S153).
LTCM had $4.6 billion of equity, about $150 billion of positions and $1.25 trillion of notional derivatives; about 90% of equity was gone between August and late September 1998 (S153). It bought small mispricings with huge leverage and added as they diverged (S108). "A relationship's failure to hold often comes just when faith in it has reached an excessive level and huge sums have been bet on it." (S153) He turned it on Oaktree: "hubris is one of the few things that could make us fail." (S153)
Lesson: the danger is leverage plus confidence in a model, not security selection.

## 9. Safe assets that were not: Bank X, Carlyle Capital, SVB, and Oaktree's own funds
Sources: 7 (S024, S097, S101, S108, S109, S111, S112).
A bank with $10 billion of capital buying $100 billion of triple-A paper loses everything on a 10% writedown (S101). Carlyle Capital held $21.7 billion of agency paper on $670 million of equity, so a 3% decline wiped it out (S108). Silicon Valley Bank put deposits into Treasurys and agency mortgages with the wrong duration: "I find it hard to believe it was a conscious, considered decision, as opposed to an example of mindlessly chasing yield, perhaps abetted by wishful thinking." (S024) His own error: three Oaktree evergreen funds that borrowed to buy bank loans, and one in Japanese small caps, were hurt in 2008 (S101). Personally, in 2007 he moved his Northern Rock deposits despite a 2% penalty, which he calls peanuts against risking principal (S024).
Lesson: safety lives in the fit of asset, leverage and funding, not in the label.

## 10. Chuck Prince and the dance, 2007
Sources: 7 (S003, S020, S054, S058, S094, S097, S106).
Citi's chief said in July 2007 that as long as the music plays you have to dance (Prince's words). Marks reads it as a career trap, not ignorance: "The problem wasn't that he overlooked the danger; the problem was that he felt he had to participate anyway." (S097) He adds that sitting out from 2005 would likely have got Prince fired sooner (S106), and in 2017 he saw investors who knew the good times would end dancing anyway (S058).
Lesson: abstaining is the hard part; "Abstaining demands uncommon strength when doing so means departing from herd behavior." (S020)

## 11. The limits to negativism, October 2008
Sources: 1 (S029).
A client of a levered senior loan fund, delevered from four to two times, asked "What if it's worse?" again and again. His numbers showed the fund still earning 25%, 22%, 19% and 13% under default rates of 1%, 4%, 7.5% and 13%, against a base case of 26%. When he asked about her equities, the conversation became The Limits to Negativism (S029).
Lesson: at bottoms no scenario seems too dark; skepticism means doubting the pessimism too (inferred from S029, S104).

## 12. Distressed cycles, 1988 to 2002: the shock you did not list
Sources: 9 (S037, S073, S082, S110, S112, S114, S119, S143, S153).
In 1988 he and Karsh found it hard to raise the first distressed fund; then recession came and the 1988 to 1992 funds found plenty to buy (S073, S143). The 1990 funds earned around 50% gross from the closed credit window and LBO defaults (S153). Summer 2002, with recession, a credit crunch, 9/11, the telecom collapse and Enron arriving together, was the best distressed buying ever, none of it predictable a year before (S114). Karsh's record of about 23% a year gross over more than two decades, all funds profitable: "I consider this record nothing short of aberrant." (S082)
Lesson: "distressed debt investors are bargain hunters whose ardor rises as prices fall" (S082)

## 13. Enron and Opportunities Fund IVb, 2001 to 2002
Sources: 3 (S120, S142, S137).
"We averaged down while Enron bonds slid and continued to draw capital and invest it as the whole distressed debt market tanked in June and July" (S120) The fund's time-weighted return was about zero, but it made $195 million at a 61.4% IRR (S120). On the equity side, ten of fifteen analysts still rated Enron a buy while its debt traded near 60 (S142), and holders stayed in "although they couldn't possibly have thought they understood the financial statements" (S142)
Lesson: buy more when the analysis holds and the price falls; own nothing whose profits you cannot explain.

## 14. Amaranth and the great year, 2006
Sources: 1 (S117).
Amaranth lost most of its $9.5 billion on natural gas in September 2006; its 2005 profits had come from deep out-of-the-money options and hurricane luck, and a trader's own buying in thin markets had set the marks behind his gains (S117). "But he seems not to have asked whether the gains were real and realizable." (S117)
Lesson: "Surprisingly good returns are often just the flip side of surprisingly bad returns." (S117)

## 15. Tumi, the theater chains and Chicago land: buy fear, sell its absence
Sources: 1 (S119).
Oaktree bought Tumi in 2002 when people believed no one would travel after 9/11, and sold in 2004 when no one remembered the fear. It bought theater chains when overexpansion was thought to have killed the industry, and sold as capacity shrank and profits rebounded; it sold Chicago land when others decided more skyscrapers were needed (S119).
Lesson: buy when the consensus says there is no way it can work, and sell when that has turned into no sweat (S119, paraphrase).

## 16. First Brands, 2025: the cockroach Oaktree caught
Sources: 2 (S003, S006).
First Brands had $11.6 billion of obligations against $5.9 billion of disclosed debt, receivables factored more than once, and off-balance-sheet vehicles (S006). Oaktree held a small position, dug deeper when red flags appeared in early summer 2025, and used contacts across strategies to build the picture (S006). He is candid about selection bias: "I'm writing about our experience with First Brands because we reached the correct conclusion." (S006) and "We don't always do this as well as we did in this case" (S006)
Lesson: opacity plus off-balance-sheet financing is the red flag, and long good times let it grow.

## 17. Argentina's century bond, 2017
Sources: 3 (S020, S054, S058).
Argentina issued a 100-year bond at about 7.85% against 2.77% on 30-year Treasurys, with $9.75 billion of bids despite eight defaults in 200 years (S020, S058). Within a year it went to the IMF, within three it defaulted, and holders recovered about 54.5 cents (S020). At the time: "Let me get this straight: it's incredible that Argentina is able to issue this thing, but it's a good buy for a moment." (S058)
Lesson: low-return times push investors to buy crazy things, and a risk premium is only real if it pays for the risk.

## 18. Mistake: the long cycle he missed, and the risk appetite that came back fast
Sources: 4 (S054, S088, S094, S100).
He saw the 2003 to 2007 excesses but not their scale: "I thought 2003-07 was like the other cycles I've lived through, just more so. I missed the fact that it was different not only in degree, but also in kind." (S100) Then he expected the crisis to leave investors risk-averse for years and was wrong: "I thought the lessons of 2007-08 had been etched into people's psyches, and that the return to pro-risk behavior would therefore be slow." (S088)
Lesson: memory is short; the pendulum swings back faster than a scarred observer expects.

## 19. Mistake: Bitcoin, 2017 to 2021
Sources: 4 (S013, S036, S057, S058).
In July 2017 he called digital currencies an unfounded fad and possibly a pyramid scheme (S058). Six weeks later, after talks with two Horizon Kinetics investors, he wrote "I'm a little less of a dinosaur regarding Bitcoin than I was when I wrote my last memo." (S057) By 2021 he said his skepticism had not borne out and that he was not informed enough to hold a firm view (S036). In 2025 he used its 465% two-year rise as a sentiment gauge, not a verdict (S013).
Lesson: pattern recognition about speculation helps avoid losses but does not judge an innovation (S036, paraphrase).

## 20. Years in the wilderness, 2009 to 2019: the cost of caution
Sources: 7 (S003, S025, S037, S044, S056, S066, S072).
With few bargains, Oaktree cut distressed fund raising from $10.9 billion in 2007 to 2008 to $5.5 billion in 2010 and $2.7 billion in 2011, and passed on heavy direct lending (S066, S003, S025). Fully invested but cautious, it lagged benchmarks for years: "we paid a price for being cautious." (S044) Then the 2020 crash arrived: "that caution enabled us to approach our portfolios calmly, generally unconcerned about price declines" (S037)
Lesson: defense is paid for in quiet years and collected in bad ones; he judged in 2016, "I don't think our caution caused us to miss much." (S066)
