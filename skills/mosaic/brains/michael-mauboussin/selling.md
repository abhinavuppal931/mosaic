# Selling: Michael Mauboussin

Built from the verified batch notes on 100 sources (papers, interviews and talks, 1997-01 to 2024-04). His rules for selling, trimming, adding and re-checking, ordered by how many sources carry them, then the kill criteria and re-check triggers this seat would write. He studies how investors sell more than he sells himself, so most rules come from his research on portfolio managers; his own record is noted at the end.

## Rules

1. **Sell when expectations are full, not when the story feels tired.** The sell is the mirror of the buy: the price now implies more than the fundamentals will deliver. "buy low expectations and sell high expectations. Everything else follows from that." (S060) and "you buy when expectations are too low and sell when expectations are full or too high." (S049) Trim when the price implies more value creation (incremental returns, growth, length of advantage) than you can justify (S114, paraphrase). Avoid what has run and embeds optimism: "Avoid assets that have performed well and embed optimistic expectations" (S138) Sources: 8 (S025, S044, S049, S060, S099, S114, S138, S164).

2. **Write dated signposts at entry and sell on them, not on the price.** Sells are where managers add least: "portfolio managers make buy decisions that add value but sell decisions that are poor." (S055) A random sale would have beaten the actual one by about 70 basis points a year (S055). "Use those signposts to examine whether your thesis remains intact or you have to change your mind." (S059) and "Should you develop an investment thesis and find that you are missing widely on your forecasts about outcomes, the signposts present an opportunity to revisit your thesis and, if appropriate, to revise your view." (S040) Sources: 6 (S040, S055, S059, S060, S113, S076).

3. **After a big drop, consult the reference class before you act.** A one-day fall of 10% or more against the market gets a structured re-check, not a reflex: "These sharp drops happen frequently enough that they deserve a thoughtful process to deal with them but infrequently enough that few investment firms have developed such a process." (S075) Weak momentum and a cheap valuation before the drop tilt toward buying more; strong momentum and an expensive valuation tilt toward selling (S101, paraphrase). Keep the emotion out: "take the emotion out, have an action plan, and allow people to act on these things." (S057) and "A READ-DO checklist is useful in periods of high stress." (S076) Sources: 6 (S057, S060, S075, S076, S101, S088).

4. **After a big gain, re-check just as hard.** His image is the climber who celebrates at the summit and forgets the descent (S075, paraphrase), and arousal cuts both ways: "high emotional arousal is not conducive to good decision making." (S075) Cheap, weak-momentum stocks that gap up tend to keep going; hot, expensive ones tend to fade (S085, paraphrase). "Deciding what to do with a stock following a sharp increase is particularly difficult because emotions tend to run high after these events." (S085) Sources: 3 (S060, S075, S085).

5. **Fight confirmation bias once you own it, and hold the view lightly.** "once you’ve made the decision and it’s in the portfolio, your reputation is on the line. You will tend to seek information that confirms your view." (S060) "the view is lightly held, and more malleable when confronted with new evidence." (S057) Updating is the hard part: "one of the most difficult things we have to do as investors, which is to update your view." (S060) "if you’re wrong you have to change your point of view" (S141) Sources: 9 (S057, S060, S074, S076, S089, S113, S141, S183, S208).

6. **The price you paid is irrelevant; beware the disposition effect.** "Irrespective of the stock’s perceived attractiveness, investors tend to sell the stocks above the purchase price and hold onto to stocks below the purchase price." (S183) "People hold on to a security too long because they don't want to take a loss, or they sell a profitable stock too quickly." (S093) Loss aversion also stops people taking the next good bet after a loss: "Loss aversion caused normal participants to forgo a positive expected value bet after having lost when a heads appeared on a toss." (S059) Sources: 5 (S059, S088, S093, S183, S196).

7. **Do not sell, or fire a manager, on short-term results alone.** A good bet can lose for a while: "A trader, or investor, can put on a positive expectation bet (correct process) and still have poor results (outcome) for some period of time due solely to randomness." (S158) Selling after weakness and buying after strength is the documented loser: "Because investors have a tendency to buy a fund after it has done well, they miss the upside but suffer from the subsequent underperformance." (S136) Short-run prices are poor teachers: "market moves, especially in the short term, are very noisy." (S076) Sources: 8 (S040, S059, S076, S117, S133, S136, S141, S158).

8. **Re-check when the advantage period shortens, even with earnings rising.** In late 1995 chip stocks fell up to 50% on record earnings because new capacity cut expected returns: "Future expectations for ROICs were effectively being cut by investors, even as short-term earnings forecasts were rising." (S019) "Rising ROICs tend to be good for shareholders and falling ROICs tend to be bad." (S028) Moats move daily: "Economic moats are almost never stable. Because of competition, they are getting a little bit wider or narrower every day." (S072) Once incremental returns reach the cost of capital the stock earns a commodity multiple (S052, paraphrase). Sources: 7 (S019, S025, S028, S052, S072, S116, S208).

9. **Most of the time the right action is nothing; act only when the gap beats the frictions.** "In some cases that means buying, in others selling, and in most cases doing nothing at all." (S032) "Investing is one field where this isn’t true. Sometimes, doing nothing is the best thing." (S133) "sometimes just sitting on your hands is the best to do" (S057) Sources: 5 (S032, S057, S133, S136, S164).

10. **Hold the long winners through drawdowns, but do not overstay faded alpha.** Drawdowns are what shake holders out of the great compounders; Microsoft's "total return was -69 percent over 111 months from January 2000 to March 2009." (S027) Yet alpha fades with time held: "portfolio managers tend to hold stocks too long, on average." (S055) The tie-breaker is expectations against fundamentals, not the holding period (inferred from S027, S055, S060). Sources: 3 (S027, S055, S060).

11. **A falling price on a sound thesis can be an add, if your value differs from the market's and is right.** "When down, momentum investors sell and value investors buy." (S054) "Cheap can become cheaper, and in that case, by the way, time can often be on your side" (S135) It is not comfortable: "Owning a stock with a large margin of safety that goes nowhere, or down, is not easy." (S130) A price drop widens the margin of safety only if your view is different and correct (S197, paraphrase). Sources: 5 (S054, S130, S135, S148, S197).

12. **Sell into enthusiasm; expect extremes not to last.** "And when everyone's feeling very good about things, it is time to be cautious or selling." (S135) "they will look to sell when stocks are up and everyone else is enthusiastic." (S145) "If you witness performance that is at the extremes, either good or bad, recognize that such performance is unlikely to persist." (S146) Sources: 4 (S135, S145, S146, S148).

13. **When the company sells stock far above value, consider selling alongside it.** After a spike the price tends toward the new, higher value per share, not the old one: "But it should be noted that those holders would be better off selling alongside the firm." (S038) Issuers sell when they think the price is full, so large issuance is a caution flag (S022, S025, paraphrase). Sources: 3 (S022, S025, S038).

14. **Size so you can hold: overbetting forces the sale.** Full Kelly brings deep drawdowns: "the path to the terminal wealth resembles a roller coaster." (S167) Overbetting is the opposite error to timidity (S138), and when consequences outrank odds he holds back: "the consequences in this case, of not being able to do that, are more important than the probabilities and so I sat on cash." (S141) Confidence in your probabilities, not just the discount, should set weight (S030). Sources: 5 (S030, S138, S141, S150, S167).

15. **Re-underwrite when the capital allocator leaves or the plan changes.** "the day this CEO leaves the company, all bets are off." (S137) "Consider whether new management intends to allocate capital differently than what was done previously." (S032) Sources: 2 (S032, S137).

## Selling mistakes on record

- He runs research, not a book: "I don’t really make that many business decisions since we’re mostly doing research." (S051, 2020-07) The corpus holds no record of his personal sells, so the seat should not invent one.
- 2008 (said 2009-11): his firm misread the crisis as one of liquidity rather than collateral and underrated who would be rescued: "we’ve probably mis-characterised the crisis and underestimated it as a result" (S145).
- 2008 (said 2010-05): he held his largest cash position ever while bullish long term, knowingly giving up expected value to protect tuition money (S141). He presents it as sizing by consequence, not as a mistake.
- Research he reports on others: sells cost portfolio managers about 70 basis points a year against a random sale (S055, 2020-03), and plan sponsors who fired laggards for recent winners left an estimated $170 billion on the table (S117, 2013-10).

## Kill criteria this seat would write

Checkable facts, each stated before buying, in his style (inferred from S019, S028, S038, S040, S055, S075, S114, S137):
- The price now implies growth that the reference class says fewer than about 5% of similar-size companies have delivered, and nothing in the case explains why this one is different.
- ROIC drops a full quintile, or incremental returns fall to the cost of capital, while the price still assumes a long advantage period.
- Two dated signposts in a row miss on the value driver the price is most sensitive to (sales growth, margin or investment needs).
- After capitalizing intangibles, the losses are real losses: unit economics do not clear the cost of capital.
- A large acquisition at a high premium, paid in stock, cuts adjusted ROIC and turns the present value of growth negative.
- The company issues a large block of stock well above your value estimate.
- The long-serving capital allocator leaves and the successor signals a different use of cash.
- Share count keeps rising because buybacks only partly offset heavy stock-based pay.
- A plausible bad outcome on the position would force a sale or breach an obligation you cannot meet.

## Re-check triggers

- Any one-day move of 10% or more against the market, up or down: rerun the reference class (momentum, valuation, quality) the next day and review over 30, 60 and 90 trading days (S075, S085, S101).
- Each earnings release, scored against the dated signposts and stated probabilities (S040, S055, S113).
- A deal announcement: compare the market's first reaction with your own prediction (S032).
- A management change or a change in pay design (S032, S137).
- New equity issuance, a large buyback, or a change in buyback type (open market versus tender) (S032, S038).
- Write-downs, impairments or changes in asset lives (S041).
- A yearly check of the company's life-cycle stage (S026).
- Market-wide extremes: risk premium, credit spreads and VIX all signaling fear or greed together, loose underwriting standards, or falling diversity of views (S031, S062, S153).
- A shift in the cost of capital regime that changes the return a holding must clear (S031).
- A periodic review of the stocks you passed on, not only those you own (S040).
- When more study stops changing your view: "gathering additional information can delay a decision, create a cost, or foster a false sense of confidence." (S030)
