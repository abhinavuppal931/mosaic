# Stories: Michael Mauboussin

Built from the verified batch notes on 100 sources (papers, interviews and talks, 1997-01 to 2024-04). He is a researcher more than a portfolio manager, so most of these are studies and cases he cites, labeled as such; the few that are his own decisions or his firm's are labeled too. Ordered by how many sources carry them. Failures and misreads count as much as wins. Figures are his dated numbers, not calls on today's stocks.

1. **The hire-and-fire cycle and outcome bias (study he cites, 2007 to 2019).** Plan sponsors hire managers after three good years and fire laggards; the fired managers then beat the hired ones, and one estimate puts the forgone value at $170 billion (S117, S135, S136, S146, S158). A Brandes study he cites found all 59 top-decile funds of 591 trailed the index in at least one year (S158). He pairs it with Belichick's failed fourth-and-2 in 2009, a sound call judged on its result (S117). "Every day, people who make good decisions with bad short-term outcomes risk losing their jobs." (S117) Lesson: judge the process, and give it time in proportion to the luck in the game. Sources: 8 (S059, S104, S117, S135, S136, S146, S158, S057).

2. **LTCM, the overbettor (case he cites, 1998).** Classic convergence trades at 27-to-1 leverage, correlations that rose to 0.70 against the 0.30 it assumed, and arbitrageurs who lacked capital when spreads widened (S059, S122, S057). "The problem was leverage, which amplified the impact of small changes in the spread." (S099) He uses it as the overbetting case in his Kelly work (S138, S150). Lesson: a good trade sized too big still ruins you; survival comes first. Sources: 9 (S054, S057, S059, S099, S122, S138, S149, S150, S194).

3. **Man overboard and celebrating the summit (his own studies, 2014 to 2016).** He built reference classes for thousands of one-day moves of 10% or more against the market, sorted by momentum, valuation and quality (S075, S085, S101). Symantec in March 2014 (CEO fired, down 12.9%, cheap and high quality) matched a buy signal and beat the market by about 24% over 90 trading days; Tenet Healthcare in November 2008 (down 36.7%, expensive, low quality) matched a sell and trailed the market by about 51% (S101). Grainger in 2012, up 11.4% while hot and expensive, was a sell; Harman in 2013 was a buy (S085). "This is a case where selling Tenet Healthcare stock, notwithstanding the weak results, would have made sense." (S101) Lesson: the base rate sets the direction, the distribution sets the humility: "It’s not an answer. It’s a part of a distribution, but at least something to hold onto." (S060) Sources: 5 (S060, S075, S076, S085, S101).

4. **Rappaport, Ralston Purina and a career launched by a trash can (his own career, 1986 to the early 1990s).** Six Drexel interviewers voted no; the division head hired him after they bonded over a Redskins wastebasket. "So I like to say that my career was launched by a trash can, and that was pure luck." (S103) He failed as a retail broker after the 1987 crash (S076, S120), then read Rappaport's Creating Shareholder Value, which gave him three lessons: accounting numbers are limited, strategy and valuation are joined, and prices reflect expectations (S076, S060). His first report, a full Rappaport cash flow study of Ralston Purina, drew a call from the CEO's office (S060, S076). Lesson: luck opens the door, and first principles keep you in the room. Sources: 7 (S051, S060, S074, S076, S093, S103, S120).

5. **Amazon, where the base rate lost (cases he cites, 2009 to 2021).** He challenged an analyst's claim that Amazon could grow 25% a year for ten years by asking how many companies ever had (S147). In 2014 he would not call it: "It remains to be seen whether Amazon.com lives up to the expectations of its stock price." (S104) At 540 times trailing earnings in 2015, gross profitability of 0.54 told a different story than the P/E (S075). After The Economist's March 2017 cover asked if investors were too optimistic, the stock returned 37.8% a year for four years against 15.8% for the S&P 500 (S045). Lesson he draws: intangibles widen the tails, so base rates are a starting point, not the final word (S045). Sources: 6 (S026, S045, S053, S075, S104, S147).

6. **Tesla's 50% a year goal against the reference class (case he cites, 2015 to 2018).** In February 2015 Musk hoped to grow sales 50% a year for a decade from about $6 billion. In that size class no company had grown 45% or more over ten years (S075). "Such a growth rate is about six standard deviations away from the average, given the sample of recent history." (S090) In 2018 he framed it as a low-probability base case, not an impossibility (S060). Lesson: never done before is a sobriety check, not a veto. Sources: 3 (S060, S075, S090).

7. **Walmart, 1972 to 1986: negative free cash flow, great stock (case he cites).** Free cash flow was negative every year, yet shareholders earned 29% a year (33% in his talk) against 11% for the S&P 500, because ROIC averaged about 18% (S049, S053). "If you knew how good Walmart’s store economics were, you would have stood behind them applauding as they spent more on investments than they earned in income." (S053) Lesson: judge a money-loser by return on investment, not the sign of the cash flow. Sources: 4 (S032, S044, S049, S053).

8. **Ed Thorp, card counting and Kelly (case he cites, 1960s to 1990s).** Thorp paired a counting edge with Kelly sizing, then ran Princeton-Newport at about 20% a year with a 6% standard deviation (S167, S076). "success in investing has two parts: finding edge and fully taking advantage of it through proper position sizing." (S076) His teaching examples: a stock that goes $100 to $200 to $20 averages +5% arithmetic and -55% geometric, and the bet with the highest arithmetic mean can compound worst (S167). Lesson: when you parlay bets, maximize the geometric mean and fear overbetting. Sources: 4 (S076, S150, S167, S183).

9. **The treasurer who fired on batting average (case he cites, 2002 and 2019).** A state pension treasurer ranked managers by the share of holdings that beat the market and nearly let go of the one with mostly losers and the best total return (S196, S056). "Say that you own four stocks, and that three of the stocks go down a bit but the fourth rises substantially. The portfolio will perform well even as the majority of the stocks decline." (S196) He adds Soros, by Bessent's account, with a batting average under 50% and grand-slam winners (S158, S054). Lesson: magnitude beats frequency. Sources: 4 (S054, S056, S158, S196).

10. **2007 to 2009: his firm's misread, then the buying window (his firm's call and his own stances).** In October 2007 he called homebuilders, with expectations near all-time lows, good long-term holds: "While there's no reliable way to know where these stocks will be in the short term, they appear to be very good investments for a longterm holder." (S156) His firm then read 2008 as a liquidity crisis when it was a collateral crisis and underrated who would be rescued: "we’ve probably mis-characterised the crisis and underestimated it as a result" (S145). By February 2009 he called panic an ideal setting for long-term investors (S149), and he later said March 2009 was easy math and hard psychology (S057). Lesson: diagnose the mechanism of a crisis, and expect the bottom to feel worst. Sources: 5 (S057, S093, S145, S149, S156).

11. **His own 2008 cash pile (his own decision).** He held the most cash of his life while bullish on the long run, because tuition for five children made a forced sale intolerable: "the consequences in this case, of not being able to do that, are more important than the probabilities and so I sat on cash." (S141) Lesson: he knowingly gave up expected value to protect a fixed obligation, which is sizing by consequence, not by odds. Sources: 1 (S141).

12. **Microsoft and Intel: value made by a longer advantage period (cases he analyzed, 1986 to 1996).** Microsoft went from $519 million at its 1986 IPO to about $100 billion by 1996, and he puts about two-thirds of that on the market lengthening its implied CAP from 8 to 10 years to 17 to 20 (S019). "Without the concept of CAP, we believe that most of Microsoft’s massive value creation cannot be explained." (S019) Intel's 1996 rise and the late-1995 chip stocks that fell on record earnings show the same lever in both directions (S019). Lesson: watch how long the price assumes excess returns last, not this year's earnings. Sources: 1 (S019).

13. **Coca-Cola at $44, the worked reverse DCF (his teaching case, 2006).** He showed the June 2006 price implied 6.5% EBITA growth, a 45% incremental investment rate, a 7.6% cost of capital and a 12-year CAP (S164). He ran the same drill on an unnamed retailer at $74 in 2012 (almost no value creation priced in) and on Cisco at $14 in 2011, each with no recommendation (S130, S135). "this analysis gives us some sense of what has to be true for today’s stock price to make sense." (S130) Lesson: state what must be true, then argue with it. Sources: 3 (S130, S135, S164).

14. **The turtle traders and Curtis Faith (case he cites).** Of the 13 trainees, Curtis Faith did best because he alone followed the system; his 20-year record had about two-thirds losing trades with winners 2.2 times the losers, and his cocoa run lost 24 of 28 trades yet netted about $56,000 (S158). "Recency bias and loss aversion often cause you to give up right before the trade becomes profitable." (S158) Lesson: the method can be taught, the temperament is the hard part. Sources: 1 (S158).

15. **ConAgra buys Beatrice, 1990 (his own early call).** As a junior analyst he judged the deal value-creating despite about $60 million a year of goodwill amortization; his senior analyst downgraded on the pattern that deals fail and dilution hurts. The stock rose about 4% while the market fell about 1% (S024). "Conagra’s management correctly understood goodwill amortization as an accounting rather than an economic cost." (S024) Lesson: a base rate or a pattern misleads when the economics of the case differ. Sources: 1 (S024).

16. **AT&T, 2012 to 2021: the low P/E value trap (case he analyzed).** A below-market P/E and an above-market yield returned 3.7% a year against 16.6% for the S&P 500, as large deals (DirecTV, Time Warner) cut ROIC and the multiple shrank (S025). "A negative PVGO implies that current earnings are not sustainable, future investments will fail to create value, or some combination of the two." (S025) Lesson: cheap on a multiple is not low expectations if returns are falling. Sources: 1 (S025).

17. **GameStop and AMC, 2021: the company sells into the spike (case he analyzed).** GameStop sold 5 million shares at an average $225.20 against an average target of $65.48, raising over $1.1 billion and lifting intrinsic value per share over 17% for ongoing holders (S038). "These stock price spikes present an opportunity for a wealth transfer via corporate action." (S038) Lesson: when a company issues stock far above value, ongoing holders gain and buyers pay, and holders may do better selling alongside. Sources: 1 (S038).

18. **Peter Lynch at Magellan: alpha versus dollars (case he analyzed).** Monthly alpha was 1.67% in Lynch's first five years on $46 million and 0.24% in his last five on $8.7 billion, yet the dollars extracted were over 25 times larger later (S064). "Small funds win but play for small stakes. The large funds win at a lesser rate but extract much more from the market because they play for high stakes." (S064) Lesson: investors want high alpha, managers want value extracted, and size dilutes skill. Sources: 1 (S064).

19. **The Nifty Fifty, priced about right on average (study he cites, 1972 to 2001).** At P/Es above 40 in 1972, the surviving Nifty Fifty returned 11.8% a year through 2001, the same as the S&P 500, with Philip Morris and Coca-Cola offsetting Polaroid and Burroughs (S193). In 1999 he argued the comparison with the late-1990s leaders was superficial because the leaders' returns on capital were much higher (S008). Lesson: a high multiple is a claim about returns and duration; check the claim, not the number. Sources: 2 (S008, S193).

20. **California Chrome, 2014: the odds against the base rate (case he cites).** The Triple Crown favorite went off at 3-to-5 although speed figures and base rates said the odds were too short, and finished fourth (S099). He uses the racetrack as his model for markets: "what makes you money is mispricing between the tote board and the speed of the horse." (S141) Lesson: compare the implied probability with how often horses like this actually win. Sources: 3 (S099, S138, S141).
