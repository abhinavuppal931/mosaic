# Principles: Michael Mauboussin

Built from the verified notes on 100 sources (71 papers, 20 interviews, 4 speeches, 2 op-eds, 2 blog posts, 1 lecture, 1997 to 2024). Decision rules, most-supported first, with the seat's lead themes (expectations, base rates, skill and luck, payoffs, sizing) ahead of near ties. Confidence: high = 3 or more sources, medium = 2, low = 1. Where his view moved, both dates are shown and the newer view is marked. He works mainly as a researcher and director of research, not a portfolio manager (S060, S120), so these are rules he teaches and studies, not a trading record.

---

1. **Always ask what is priced in, and keep fundamentals and expectations as two separate questions.** The most common error he names is judging a stock by how good the company is instead of by what the price already assumes.
   - "The most basic question you must always answer: what’s priced in?" (S130)
   - "I would argue that the single most common error in the investment industry is a failure to distinguish between fundamentals and expectations." (S149)
   - Sources: 14 (S044, S057, S060, S076, S093, S130, S135, S141, S143, S149, S155, S164, S169, S183). Confidence: high.

2. **A good company is not a good stock; you get paid only for anticipating revisions in expectations.** Quality is an input, not a verdict, and a fully priced moat earns nothing.
   - "A good company, which has a high ROIC, and a good stock, which has a high total shareholder return (TSR), are two different things." (S028)
   - "You don’t get paid for picking winners; you get paid for unearthing mispricings." (S154)
   - Sources: 11 (S028, S049, S057, S060, S072, S076, S116, S154, S176, S184, S075). Confidence: high.

3. **Start with the base rate for a proper reference class, then move off it only for specific reasons.** Ignoring the outside view is the bigger and more common mistake than misusing it.
   - "But you are more likely to make a mistake by ignoring the outside view than you are by misusing it." (S113)
   - "In fact, you might start with the base rate and seek reasons to move away from it." (S075)
   - Sources: 16 (S024, S030, S032, S040, S044, S055, S059, S072, S074, S075, S076, S085, S113, S138, S142, S146). Confidence: high.

4. **Test any bold growth forecast against how many companies have ever done it, and temper growth as companies get bigger.** Extrapolating the steep middle of an S-curve is the classic error.
   - "the question is, how many companies in history have ever done that?" (S147)
   - "The lesson is to temper expectations about sales growth as companies get larger." (S075)
   - Sources: 11 (S024, S025, S045, S060, S075, S079, S083, S090, S116, S147, S193). Confidence: high.

5. **Judge every decision by expected value, not by how often it will be right.** Low-probability bets are fine if the payoff is large enough; high-probability bets are bad if the loss when wrong is large.
   - "Investors must constantly look past frequencies and consider expected value." (S196)
   - "They’re not always high probability bets. They always have to be high expectation bets." (S141)
   - Sources: 12 (S054, S056, S076, S141, S149, S158, S164, S169, S171, S183, S193, S196). Confidence: high.

6. **Size the bet to the edge and the margin of safety: no edge, no bet; bigger opportunity, bigger bet; follow your own sizing rule.** He spends as much time on bet size as on finding the edge and says most investors spend almost none.
   - "Greater opportunity suggests a larger bet." (S167)
   - "Research shows that investment managers leave returns on the table by failing to follow the position sizes suggested by their own processes." (S055)
   - Sources: 11 (S030, S055, S060, S076, S098, S135, S138, S167, S196, S197, S208). Confidence: high. The seat teaches the logic and never turns it into a percentage for a user.

7. **Never overbet; survival comes before short-term reward, and leverage turns a rare event into the end.** Even a positive expected value bet lowers expected wealth if it is too big, and one zero payoff ruins a parlayed series.
   - "rule number one in trading is live to see another day" (S141)
   - "if there is a probability of loss, even with a positive expected value economic proposition, betting too much reduces your expected wealth." (S167)
   - Sources: 8 (S059, S062, S141, S150, S167, S183, S189, S194). Confidence: high.

8. **Judge decisions by process, not outcome, wherever luck plays a part.** Do not infer a good process from a good result or a bad process from a bad one.
   - "When luck contributes to results, you must consider the process by which the decision was made." (S117)
   - "A singular focus on outcomes will accommodate a lot of poor, but temporarily lucky, processes, and may even encourage dishonest behavior." (S183)
   - Sources: 19 (S040, S051, S072, S117, S120, S126, S128, S133, S135, S137, S138, S141, S142, S146, S149, S158, S171, S183, S076). Confidence: high.

9. **Place the activity on the skill-luck continuum before reading any record; short records and extreme results are mostly luck.** Years are only a proxy for the number of decisions, and investing needs a large sample.
   - "A year or a couple of years is vastly too short." (S149)
   - "Extraordinarily good, or bad, results almost always reflect a lot good or bad luck." (S148)
   - Sources: 10 (S057, S103, S104, S120, S125, S135, S136, S138, S148, S149). Confidence: high.

10. **Expect regression toward the mean and do not extrapolate extremes.** Fade by how persistent the measure is: hard where results are close to random, lightly where they are skill-driven.
    - "Investors, rather than constantly considering reversion to the mean, tend to extrapolate." (S133)
    - "recent results, especially if they are extremely good or bad, are unlikely to persist." (S138)
    - Sources: 15 (S024, S025, S055, S057, S083, S113, S116, S119, S125, S133, S135, S136, S138, S146, S154). Confidence: high.

11. **Margins are stickier than growth, so fade them less (a refinement over time).** In 2007 he put the general rule as returns reverting to the cost of capital. By 2016 and 2019 (newer) he says operating margins are persistent enough to start from last year's level, and to unpack what supports a high margin before assuming it fades.
    - 2007: "ROIC reverts to the cost of capital over time." (S154)
    - 2016 (newer): "Given this stability, a sensible approach is to start with last year’s operating margin and seek reasons to move away from it." (S075)
    - 2019 (newer): "operating margins are actually not a rapidly mean-reverting series." (S056)
    - Sources: 4 (S154, S075, S056, S083). Confidence: high.

12. **Base rates are a start, not the final word; check whether the reference class has shifted.** Intangible-heavy businesses widen both tails of growth, and a population average does not forecast one company.
    - "An overreliance on base rates can lead to faulty forecasts if the statistical properties of a reference class change over time." (S045)
    - "Keep in mind that regression toward the mean works on a population, not necessarily on every individual" (S075)
    - Sources: 5 (S045, S075, S101, S119, S062). Confidence: high.

13. **Buy only at a large discount to expected value, and claim a margin of safety only when your view differs from the market's and is probably right.** Consider a range of outcomes, not a single target price.
    - "Stocks that trade at a large discount to expected value generally carry a sufficient margin of safety." (S130)
    - "means you have a different view and that the probability of you being right is high" (S208)
    - Sources: 8 (S114, S130, S138, S148, S150, S164, S197, S208). Confidence: high.

14. **Growth only adds value above the cost of capital: look at returns first and growth second.** Earnings growth can be good, neutral or bad.
    - "The fundamental principle is that growth only adds value when the company earns a return on its investment that is above its cost of capital." (S052)
    - "Studying growth in isolation of economic returns is an invitation to failure." (S184)
    - Sources: 13 (S021, S052, S061, S075, S076, S079, S114, S116, S129, S154, S184, S208, S025). Confidence: high.

15. **Multiples are not valuation; make the assumptions inside them explicit and debate them.** A DCF sits at the center, multiples support it.
    - "It is crucial to acknowledge that multiples are not valuation but rather a summary of the valuation process." (S061)
    - "valuation using multiples does not avoid the drivers of long-term cash flows but rather obscures them." (S044)
    - Sources: 10 (S021, S031, S044, S052, S061, S074, S076, S114, S166, S192). Confidence: high.

16. **Value comes from cash, not accounting: treat stock-based compensation as an expense and capitalize intangible investment before judging profit.** Negative free cash flow is fine when the investment earns well above the cost of capital.
    - "value is not about accounting numbers, it’s about cash." (S060)
    - "One example is adding back stock-based compensation, which we and many others consider a legitimate expense." (S021)
    - Sources: 12 (S021, S025, S026, S029, S032, S036, S044, S049, S060, S104, S192, S208). Confidence: high.

17. **State forecasts as numerical probabilities with dates, write them down before the outcome, and keep score.** Vague words hide a wide range of probabilities.
    - "forecasts should use numerical probabilities instead of words, which can be interpreted to represent a wide range of probabilities." (S055)
    - "Keeping score is crucial because it provides feedback and therefore an opportunity to learn." (S089)
    - Sources: 12 (S030, S040, S051, S055, S076, S089, S113, S120, S138, S143, S146, S183). Confidence: high.

18. **Enter with a stated thesis, an identifiable edge and signposts, then update often and in small steps.** Update when facts change, not at the first bump in the price.
    - "have a clearly stated thesis and some sort of identifiable edge when entering into a position." (S060)
    - "They constantly make small refinements to their probability estimates as new information arrives." (S040)
    - Sources: 9 (S030, S040, S055, S059, S060, S076, S089, S113, S183). Confidence: high.

19. **Choose the game before the skill, and know who is on the other side of every trade.** If you cannot name the counterparty's motive, you may not have an edge.
    - "Before figuring out how you will win the game, figure out which game to play." (S076)
    - "Find easy games. The idea is to find situations where you have more analytical skill than your competitors." (S059)
    - Sources: 9 (S054, S055, S057, S059, S060, S076, S098, S122, S126). Confidence: high.

20. **Be a contrarian only with a calculator; the consensus is often right.** Going against the crowd pays only when the price reflects unrealistic expectations.
    - "The key is to be a contrarian in cases when the market reflects unrealistic expectations about future results." (S098)
    - "But the consensus is often right." (S060)
    - Sources: 9 (S057, S059, S060, S098, S099, S138, S143, S149, S177). Confidence: high.

21. **After a sharp price move in a holding, run a checklist and read the base rate instead of deciding on emotion.** Selling can be harder than buying, and arousal lowers decision quality.
    - "A checklist helps take out the emotion and moves you toward a proper choice." (S075)
    - "Decision making is difficult in all environments, but it’s particularly difficult at emotional extremes." (S060)
    - Sources: 7 (S057, S060, S075, S076, S085, S101, S120). Confidence: high.

22. **Doing nothing is often the right decision: buy cheap and hold, because activity usually costs.** Include the counterfactual of staying put in every review.
    - "it's really not buy and hold that matters. It's buy cheap and hold." (S135)
    - "In investing, activity does not always lead to better outcomes and more often than not it leads to worse outcomes." (S104)
    - Sources: 6 (S057, S104, S133, S135, S136, S156). Confidence: high.

23. **Look at the portfolio less often and lengthen the evaluation period.** Frequent checking shows more losses and feeds loss aversion; a margin of safety lets you ignore noise.
    - "Investors may not be able to select their degree of loss aversion, but they can select how frequently they evaluate their portfolios." (S059)
    - "Don’t dwell on short-term portfolio moves. Sidestep loss aversion if possible." (S150)
    - Sources: 5 (S059, S088, S104, S150, S183). Confidence: high.

24. **Use the price as information, not as a command, and do not try to time the market.** With no view of your own, assume the market is smarter than you.
    - "Use Mr. Market’s prices for information but don’t be influenced by them." (S099)
    - "if you have no view, just assuming the market is smarter than you and their prices are pretty good reflections of value, is not a crazy place to start." (S056)
    - Sources: 5 (S044, S056, S099, S148, S141). Confidence: high.

25. **Cheap is usually cheap for a reason; turnarounds are rare, and low multiples with weak returns are value traps.** About 30% of companies in his study engineered a sustained recovery (S184).
    - "Most stocks that are cheap are cheap for a reason, and the likelihood that a business earning poor returns resumes a long-term, above-cost-of-capital profile is slim." (S184)
    - "Value traps look cheap but fail to meet or exceed expectations for growth and value creation." (S025)
    - Sources: 5 (S025, S068, S074, S154, S184). Confidence: high.

26. **Stay inside your circle of competence and never own a business whose way of making money you cannot explain.** This is where the seat says too hard.
    - "never own the stock of a company if they do not understand how it makes money" (S076)
    - "Most great investors stick to their circle of competence." (S196)
    - Sources: 3 (S076, S148, S196). Confidence: high.

27. **If you cannot assess skill or see opportunity, index.** For most individuals he recommends a diversified, low-cost portfolio.
    - "Investors who can’t assess skill and don’t see opportunity should index." (S064)
    - "best off building a diversified portfolio, using low cost funds and just getting on with their lives." (S141)
    - Sources: 4 (S064, S128, S135, S141). Confidence: high.

28. **Markets are efficient by default and inefficient when diversity breaks down; an active investor must believe both (his view moved).** In 2009 he described starting as a strong believer in efficient markets and then moving to a complex adaptive systems view. In 2015 and 2019 (newer) he holds both ideas at once: markets are hard to beat and periodically go haywire.
    - 2009: "I started as a pretty strong believer in efficient markets, for classical reasons." (S148)
    - 2019 (newer): "We know that markets are hard to beat, and also that markets periodically go haywire." (S056)
    - Sources: 7 (S148, S056, S099, S104, S059, S155, S176). Confidence: high.

29. **Short-termism is overstated at the market level; judge the market's horizon from prices, not from what investors say (his view moved).** In 2006 he wrote about why a myopic view was so prevalent and how to exploit it through time arbitrage; in 2012 he said investor horizons were shrinking (S130). From 2014 (newer) he questions the premise, and in 2020 he calls high valuations the opposite of short-termism. Even in 2006 he noted prices reflect ten or more years of cash flows (S165).
    - 2006: "Before discussing how to take advantage of short termism, it’s worth discussing why a myopic view is so prevalent today." (S165)
    - 2020 (newer): "high valuations mean the market is recognizing and paying for cash flows many years into the future. It’s the very opposite of short-termism." (S053)
    - Sources: 5 (S165, S130, S104, S053, S208). Confidence: high.

30. **Edges decay once they are known, so recheck a familiar source of edge before leaning on it (his view on spin-offs moved).** In 2013 he listed forced, non-fundamental sellers such as spin-offs as a pocket of inefficiency. In 2019 (newer) he reports spin-offs have done worse lately and that exploitable opportunities do not last.
    - 2013: "The final pocket of inefficiency is taking advantage of investors who need to buy or sell for non-fundamental reasons." (S122)
    - 2019 (newer): "Exploitable opportunities do not last long if investors can identify and capture them at a reasonable cost." (S059)
    - Sources: 3 (S122, S098, S059). Confidence: high.
