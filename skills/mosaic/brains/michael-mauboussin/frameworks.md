# Frameworks: Michael Mauboussin

Built from the verified notes on 100 sources (71 papers, 20 interviews, 4 speeches, 2 op-eds, 2 blog posts, 1 lecture, 1997 to 2024). Ordered by how many sources use each tool, with the seat's lead tools (expectations, base rates, skill and luck, payoffs, sizing) placed ahead of near ties. Moats, capital allocation and accounting are marked as supporting tools: they feed the expectations work, they do not replace it. He is a researcher more than a portfolio manager, so most numbers below are from his studies and worked cases, labeled as such. Scoring lines are guidance drawn from his own thresholds and numbers, not rules he wrote for a scorecard. His books (Expectations Investing with Alfred Rappaport, More Than You Know, Think Twice, The Success Equation) are not quoted; where a tool comes mainly from a book it is paraphrase with the book named.

## 1. Expectations investing: fundamentals versus expectations, read through a reverse DCF *(named; he also calls it what is priced in, and uses the handicapper's tote board)*

- **What it is**: a stock price is a set of expectations about future free cash flow. Fundamentals are how the business will actually perform; expectations are what the price already assumes. Excess returns come only from the gap, and from the market revising its expectations. Taught in Expectations Investing (with Rappaport); paraphrase of the book, with the free sources quoted below.
- **Steps**: (1) Estimate price-implied expectations with a reverse DCF: hold the inputs easiest to estimate (cost of capital, tax rate, capital intensity) and solve for the sales growth, margin and competitive advantage period the price needs (S208, S130). (2) Identify expectations opportunities with competitive strategy and the expectations infrastructure: value triggers (sales, operating costs, investment) feed value factors (volume, price and mix, operating leverage, scale, cost and investment efficiencies) which feed value drivers (S164, S166). Sales growth is usually the trigger that matters most (S169). (3) Buy, sell or hold on expected value against price, with a margin of safety big enough to cover turnover, transaction costs and taxes (S164).
- **Worked cases he cites**: Coca-Cola at $42 in December 2005 implied 6.0% EBITA growth, a 45% incremental investment rate, a 7.5% cost of capital and a 12-year competitive advantage period (S169; S164 has the June 2006 update at $44). Kellogg at about $70 implied about 10% cash flow growth for about 15 years (S019). The Cisco at $45 thought experiment: one team asks what must happen for the price to make sense, a second does the fundamentals, and they meet at the end (S060).
- **Use on a stock decision**: the first question on every buy, add, hold, trim or sell. A user who cannot say what the price implies has no thesis yet, only an opinion about the company.
- **Scoring guidance**: 0 = the case is about a good or bad company with no statement of what is priced in; 5 = the implied growth, margin and duration are stated but not compared with anything; 10 = the implied numbers are stated, compared with base rates and the user's own scenarios, and the gap (the variant perception) is named with a reason the market will revise. His rule of thumb: buy when anticipated performance beats implied performance, sell when it is worse (S019).
- **Quote**: "The one job of an equity investor is to take advantage of gaps between expectations and fundamentals." (S049)
- **Quote**: "Do the math. Figure out what you have to believe to justify the prevailing price, and compare that to plausible scenarios." (S059)
- **Quote**: "The fundamentals are how fast the horse will run. The odds in the tote board are what is priced in." (S057)
- **Sources**: 35 (S019, S028, S030, S040, S044, S049, S056, S057, S059, S060, S072, S075, S076, S090, S093, S099, S114, S116, S130, S135, S138, S141, S143, S145, S149, S155, S164, S169, S171, S177, S183, S192, S194, S197, S208)

## 2. Base rates and the outside view *(named; inside view versus outside view, reference class forecasting)*

- **What it is**: the inside view builds a forecast from the specifics of the case and runs optimistic; the outside view asks how a reference class of similar cases actually turned out. He wants a blend, weighted by the luck in the activity: lean on the case when skill dominates and on the base rate when luck dominates (S075, S125).
- **Steps** (the four steps, S075, S113, S055): (1) choose a reference class large enough to be statistically useful but narrow enough to apply; (2) look at the whole distribution of outcomes, which is often skewed; (3) make the forecast with the inside view informed by that distribution; (4) judge how reliable the forecast is and regress toward the mean accordingly. Best version is similarity-based forecasting: use the whole class but weight similar cases more, accepting that you trade sample size for specificity (S075).
- **Use on a stock decision**: before accepting any growth, margin, turnaround, deal or survival assumption, ask what share of the reference class ever did it. Sales growth is where base rates help most (S030).
- **Base rates he publishes (studies)**: 10-year sales growth for US companies starting at $5 to $10 billion of sales is bell-shaped around 4.5% with a standard deviation of 8.5% (S024). A $10 billion company growing 20% a year for 5 years has a base rate of 3.5% (S055). No company with $100 billion or more of sales had grown at a mid-teens rate for long (S045, S060). About 30% of downturned tech and retail firms engineered a sustained recovery (S184). Roughly two-thirds of deals destroy value for the buyer (S208). Only one in eight of 1,800+ companies met all three sustained-growth hurdles (S193). Ten-year default base rate about 2% for investment grade and 23% for speculative grade (S031). About half of listed companies delist within 10 years (S027).
- **Scoring guidance**: 0 = forecast with no reference class; 5 = a base rate is named but the forecast sits far from it with no reason; 10 = the forecast starts at the reference class median (for three years or longer, S075) and every move away from it has a stated, specific reason. A forecast that the base rate says has almost never happened scores near 0 on this dimension however good the story.
- **Quote**: "The outside view considers a problem as an instance of a larger reference class." (S024)
- **Quote**: "If skill determines the outcome, you can rely more on the inside view. If luck plays a large role, you should place more weight on the outside view." (S075)
- **Quote**: "what happened when others were in this situation before?" (S055)
- **Sources**: 32 (S024, S025, S027, S030, S031, S032, S040, S044, S045, S055, S059, S060, S062, S072, S074, S075, S076, S079, S085, S089, S090, S101, S113, S136, S138, S142, S146, S147, S154, S184, S193, S208)

## 3. The skill-luck continuum *(named; two urns, true score theory, the lose-on-purpose test)*

- **What it is**: every result is a draw from a skill urn plus a draw from a luck urn (S138); observed outcome equals skill plus luck (S125). Place the activity between pure skill (chess, a sprinter) and pure luck (roulette) before reading any record or making any forecast. Investing sits toward the luck end. Developed at length in The Success Equation; paraphrase of the book, free sources quoted.
- **Parts**: (1) the lose-on-purpose test: if you cannot lose on purpose, luck dominates (S138); (2) persistence as the test of skill: high correlation of results from one period to the next means skill, low means luck (S064, S083, S119); (3) variance decomposition: variance of skill equals observed variance minus luck variance (S138); (4) luck's three features: it happens to a person or group, can be good or bad, and another outcome was reasonably possible (S120, S125, S133).
- **Use on a stock decision**: before giving weight to a manager's record, a company's streak, or the user's own last few trades, ask how much of it could be luck and how big the sample is.
- **Scoring guidance**: persistence numbers from his studies: baseball strikeout rate r 0.82 versus in-play doubles and triples r 0.14 (S119); five-year persistence of fund gross profit r 0.27, above alpha's near zero (S064). One statistical approach needs more than a dozen years of results to conclude a manager is skilled (S104); a majority of top-decile funds are there by luck (S136). A thesis that cites a short winning record as proof of skill scores low.
- **Quote**: "The single most important concept is determining where the activity sits on the continuum of all-luck, no-skill at one end to no-luck, all-skill at the other." (S125)
- **Quote**: "ask whether you can lose on purpose" (S138)
- **Quote**: "When persistence is high in any activity, we can assume that skill plays a meaningful role in determining results. When persistence is low, luck is dominant." (S064)
- **Sources**: 25 (S051, S057, S064, S072, S075, S083, S093, S103, S104, S117, S119, S120, S122, S125, S126, S128, S131, S133, S135, S136, S138, S141, S147, S148, S149)

## 4. Process versus outcome *(named; the Russo and Schoemaker matrix, and his T theory: process, odds, time)*

- **What it is**: in probabilistic fields, judge a decision by how it was made, because good decisions sometimes lose and bad ones sometimes win. The best performers in every probabilistic field share three things: focus on process over outcome, a constant search for favorable odds, and an understanding of the role of time (S158, S171, S183).
- **Parts**: the four boxes: good process and good outcome is deserved success; good process and bad outcome is a bad break; bad process and good outcome is dumb luck; bad process and bad outcome is poetic justice (S171, S183). A good process is analytical, behavioral and organizational (S135, S138); economically sound, repeatable and disciplined (S149).
- **Use on a stock decision**: grade the user's decision on what was known and written down at the time (thesis, edge, base rate, probabilities, size), not on what the price did after. Applies to selling after a loss: a bad outcome alone is not proof of a bad process (S158).
- **Scoring guidance**: 0 = the case for the decision is the recent price move; 5 = a thesis exists but was not recorded before the outcome; 10 = the thesis, probabilities and signposts were recorded in advance and the review covers good outcomes as well as bad ones (S040).
- **Quote**: "Probabilistic endeavors require a focus on process because, by definition, poor decisions will periodically result in good outcomes, and good decisions will lead to poor outcomes." (S183)
- **Quote**: "While a good process leads to the highest probability of good results over time, the link between process and outcome is loose in the short run." (S117)
- **Sources**: 21 (S040, S051, S062, S072, S076, S113, S117, S120, S126, S128, S133, S135, S137, S138, S141, S142, S146, S149, S158, S171, S183)

## 5. Regression toward the mean, with a shrinkage factor *(named; how fast and to what mean)*

- **What it is**: wherever luck contributes, extreme results are followed by results closer to the average. His forecasting formula: expected outcome equals r times (current outcome minus mean) plus mean, where r is the correlation between this period and the next. r of 1.0 means no reversion; r of 0 means the best guess is the mean (S119, S075).
- **Steps**: (1) how fast: use the multi-year r for the measure, since a one-year r overstates reversion (S119, S075); (2) to what mean: check how stable the mean has been; in volatile sectors shade toward mid-cycle profitability (S119); (3) override the fade only with well-founded, company-specific reasons (S119).
- **Worked cases (studies)**: AutoZone CFROI 20.8% against a sector mean of 8.7% with four-year r of 0.56 projects to 15.5% (S119); Microsoft 16.1% against 9.0% with r of 0.50 projects to 12.6% (S075). Four-year CFROI r by sector runs from 0.70 in consumer staples to 0.36 in energy (S119).
- **Refinement**: margins are stickier than growth. Sales growth persistence is low (about 0.30 for one year, S083) and net income growth persistence is negative (S083), while operating margins are not a rapidly mean-reverting series (S056) and a sensible start is last year's margin (S075).
- **Use on a stock decision**: fade any extreme growth, ROIC or return assumption in the user's model by the r for that measure and sector; ask what keeps a high return from fading.
- **Scoring guidance**: a model that holds an extreme growth rate or return flat for a decade with no fade scores low; one that fades at the sector r and names the reason for any slower fade scores high.
- **Quote**: "when outcomes are close to random, you should regress your forecast substantially. When outcomes are driven by skill and are therefore persistent, you need not regress much at all." (S055)
- **Quote**: "High correlations imply persistence, what you see next will closely resemble what you’ve seen before, and generally indicate the presence of skill." (S119)
- **Sources**: 20 (S024, S025, S028, S055, S056, S057, S072, S075, S083, S104, S113, S116, S119, S125, S133, S135, S138, S146, S154, S184)

## 6. Expected value: frequency versus magnitude *(named; the Babe Ruth effect, batting average versus slugging)*

- **What it is**: what matters is how much you make when right against how much you lose when wrong, not how often you are right. Sum probability times outcome for each scenario; the most likely outcome can still be a bad bet.
- **Parts**: (1) the table: 70% chance of up 1% and 30% chance of down 10% is an expected value of minus 2.3%, a stock priced for perfection; flip it for a downtrodden stock and it is plus 2.3% (S171, S183, S196, S141). (2) Batting average and slugging: an information ratio of 0.3 can come from an 80% hit rate with a 0.3 slugging ratio or a 30% hit rate with a 2.6 slugging ratio (S054). (3) Investing has no ante: pass when expected value is poor, bet more when it is good (S196). Thorp's blackjack: favorable hands arise 9.8% of the time (S196).
- **Use on a stock decision**: require at least three scenarios with probabilities and values, and judge the decision on the probability-weighted sum against price, not on the odds the stock goes up.
- **Scoring guidance**: 0 = the case is a single most-likely outcome; 5 = scenarios exist but without probabilities or with only upside; 10 = scenarios cover adverse outcomes, probabilities sum to one, and the expected value is compared with price. A high-probability, small-gain, large-loss setup scores low even if the story is right.
- **Quote**: "It’s not how often you are right that matters, it’s how much money you make when you’re right versus how much money you lose when you’re wrong." (S054)
- **Quote**: "the frequency of correctness does not matter; it is the magnitude of correctness that matters." (S196)
- **Quote**: "That’s a great probability, but a very poor expected value." (S141)
- **Sources**: 16 (S030, S054, S056, S076, S130, S141, S148, S149, S158, S164, S169, S171, S183, S193, S196, S197)

## 7. Edge plus sizing, and the Kelly criterion *(named; the card-counting model)*

- **What it is**: investing has two parts, finding an edge and betting the right amount given that edge (S060, S076, S167). Kelly is a sizing algorithm that ties the size of the edge to the share of the bankroll; it maximizes the geometric mean return, which is what matters when bets are parlayed (S150, S167). This seat uses it as education about the logic of sizing, never to give a user a percentage.
- **Parts**: (1) Kelly: fraction equals edge divided by odds (S167), or 2p minus 1 for even-money bets (S197, S054). (2) No edge, no bet: in an efficient market the edge and the fraction are zero (S167). (3) Overbetting a positive expected value bet still lowers expected wealth, and a single zero payoff makes the geometric mean zero (S167). (4) Partial Kelly trades some return for less volatility (S167). (5) Kelly works when bets are parlayed, repeated, and the distribution is known (S167), which a stock rarely satisfies. (6) Portfolio construction needs a goal, an opportunity set (many small edges or lumpy large ones) and constraints such as liquidity, drawdowns and leverage (S076). (7) Probability is not confidence; two ideas with the same discount to expected value can deserve different weights (S030).
- **Textbook examples he uses (education only)**: a coin that pays $2 on heads and costs $1 on tails has full Kelly at 25% of bankroll (S150, S167); a 60% coin at even money has 20% (S054). His sizing illustration: three picks finish at plus 50%, 0 and minus 20%; equal weight gives plus 10%, 80% in the loser gives minus 11%, 80% in the winner gives plus 38% (S098).
- **Use on a stock decision**: ask whether the size matches the edge, the margin of safety and the confidence; whether any path ends in ruin; whether leverage is involved; and whether the user is following a sizing rule or a feeling.
- **Scoring guidance**: 0 = size set by conviction alone, or levered so a low-probability event ends the account; 5 = size is tied to conviction but not to the margin of safety or downside; 10 = size scales with the discount to expected value and confidence, no single outcome is ruinous, and the user has a written sizing rule they follow. He flags that the model is simplified and few investors are organized to use it (S197, S167).
- **Quote**: "Betting too little leaves a substantial amount of money on the table, while betting too much leads to near-certain ruin." (S167)
- **Quote**: "One is gaining an edge, and the second is how much you bet, given the edge you have." (S060)
- **Quote**: "Investors should base the magnitude of their investments on the size of the margin of safety." (S197)
- **Sources**: 16 (S030, S040, S054, S055, S060, S076, S098, S135, S138, S141, S150, S167, S183, S196, S197, S208)

## 8. Signposts, linchpins, the decision journal and Brier scores *(named; linchpin analysis after Roger George)*

- **What it is**: turn the variant perception into a few dated, numeric forecasts so the thesis gets feedback before the noisy price verdict arrives. For most stocks two or three linchpin issues drive value (S057, S060).
- **Steps**: (1) at entry, write a clear thesis and the identifiable edge (S060); (2) name the linchpin issues and the working assumptions on each (S040, S113); (3) write signposts: an objectively agreed outcome, a deadline, and importance to the thesis (S055); (4) attach numerical probabilities, not words (S030, S055); (5) record in a decision journal what you decided, why, what you expect and how you feel (S113, S120, S143); (6) score with Brier scores and update in small steps (S040, S089).
- **Use on a stock decision**: every buy or add should leave behind a short list of signposts; a hold or sell review checks them first.
- **Scoring guidance**: Brier scores run 0 to 2, lower is better; 0.50 is a random guess and superforecasters score about 0.20 to 0.25 (S089). Worked example: forecast more than 110 new subscribers with 80% probability when the market has priced in 100 (S040). A thesis with no dated, checkable signposts scores low on process.
- **Quote**: "A useful signpost has three features. It has an outcome that can be objectively agreed upon, within a specified date, and is important to the overall thesis." (S055)
- **Quote**: "write down what you expect to happen, why you expect it to happen, and attach probabilities to your views." (S113)
- **Sources**: 16 (S024, S040, S051, S055, S057, S059, S060, S076, S089, S113, S120, S138, S143, S146, S149, S183)

## 9. Spread first, growth second *(unnamed as a whole; the dollar bill test, economic profit, ROIIC)*

- **What it is**: growth creates value only when the return on incremental capital exceeds the cost of capital; at a return equal to the cost of capital growth is worth nothing, below it growth destroys value. So look at the spread before growth.
- **Parts**: the dollar test: a dollar invested above the cost of capital is worth more than a dollar in the market (S028, S074); economic profit is the spread times invested capital (S022, S028); ROIIC is the change in NOPAT over last year's investment, smoothed over three or five years (S061, S114); the threshold margin is the operating margin at which a company earns exactly its cost of capital (S075, S079).
- **Use on a stock decision**: when a bull case rests on growth, ask what return the growth earns and what investment it needs. Fast growth at the cost of capital earns no premium (S154).
- **Scoring guidance (his tables)**: at an 8% cost of capital, an 8% ROIC warrants 12.5 times earnings at any growth rate, while at 24% ROIC going from 6% to 10% growth lifts the P/E from 19 to 26 (S061). Threshold margin worked case: 35% and 25% investment rates, 10% cost of capital, 35% tax gives 8.4%, so a company at 8.4% is value neutral despite 8% growth (S079). Only 28 of the 100 fastest EBIT growers each year were also top 100 for TSR, 2008 to 2012 (S116).
- **Quote**: "Growth creates a lot of value only when the spread is positive and large, has no effect when the spread is zero, and destroys value when the spread is negative." (S061)
- **Quote**: "Investors should consider returns on capital first and earnings growth second because earnings growth can be neutral, good, or bad" (S116)
- **Sources**: 17 (S008, S021, S022, S028, S032, S036, S052, S061, S074, S075, S079, S114, S116, S129, S154, S184, S208)

## 10. Wisdom and madness of crowds: diversity breakdown *(named; markets as complex adaptive systems)*

- **What it is**: markets are efficient when three conditions hold: diversity of views, a working aggregation mechanism, and incentives. When one breaks, usually diversity, prices can veer far from value. Collective error equals average individual error minus prediction diversity (S059, S099, S155).
- **Parts**: diversity breakdowns are nonlinear: diversity can fall for a while with nothing happening, then the system flips (Millennium Bridge, LeBaron's agent model, S099, S153); imitation, information cascades and adoption thresholds cause them (S076, S176); two kinds, dramatic bubbles and time arbitrage of noise versus signal (S155); markets are hard to beat and periodically go haywire, and both are true (S056).
- **Use on a stock decision**: when sentiment on a stock or sector is uniformly bullish or bearish, flag a possible diversity breakdown and then check what the price implies; when many investors are doing well with the same strategy, assume hidden fragility.
- **Scoring guidance**: a case that leans on the crowd's view in a one-sided market scores low; a case that identifies the breakdown and then does the expectations math scores high. He admits there is no simple way to measure diversity in real time (S153).
- **Quote**: "In other words, when investors lose diversity markets lose efficiency." (S059)
- **Quote**: "As diversity declines, the fragility of the market increases but everything seems fine." (S099)
- **Quote**: "if a large group of investors seem to all be doing well pursuing a similar strategy, chances are there is a great deal of invisible vulnerability." (S153)
- **Sources**: 17 (S051, S056, S059, S062, S076, S099, S122, S130, S133, S141, S146, S149, S153, S155, S176, S177, S195)

## 11. Fat tails, the leverage cycle, and experience versus exposure *(unnamed as a group)*

- **What it is**: market returns are not normal; they have more small moves, fewer medium ones and more large ones, so standard models understate risk (S176, S194). Experience asks how often something happened; exposure asks what happens if it does (S158, S194). The ability to borrow, not the level of rates, drives booms and crashes (S059, S062).
- **Parts**: the leverage cycle (Geanakoplos): optimists borrow to bid up an asset, bad news forces sales, lenders raise margins, more sales follow (S057, S062, S122); the three phases of a crash: bad news after a strong run, levered optimists lose fast, lenders raise collateral and forced sales spread (S156); Minsky's hedge, speculative and Ponzi debt (S062); consequences matter more than probabilities (S141).
- **Use on a stock decision**: before any size or leverage decision, ask what the position and the account look like in the tail, not in the average year.
- **Scoring guidance (his figures)**: October 1987 was about 19 standard deviations (S194). Leverage example: $100 asset on 15% margin; a 5% loss and a margin raised to 25% leave $40 of assets on $10 of equity (S062). A plan that is fine on average but cannot survive a fat-tail move scores low.
- **Quote**: "There are fatter tails than what the standard model suggests." (S194)
- **Quote**: "Geanakoplos argues that the availability of leverage, not interest rates, is the most important variable in understanding bubbles and crashes." (S062)
- **Quote**: "If a low probability event occurs, what are my consequences and can I live?" (S141)
- **Sources**: 13 (S057, S059, S062, S122, S141, S150, S153, S156, S158, S176, S189, S194, S197)

## 12. Multiples are shorthand, not valuation *(named phrase)*

- **What it is**: a multiple compresses a DCF into one number and hides its assumptions about incremental return, growth and the cost of capital. Reverse-engineer the multiple into those drivers before using it (S021, S052, S061, S114).
- **Parts**: the same 15.0 P/E can mean 12% growth on a 0.8 point spread, 6% growth on 3 points, or 3% growth on 15 points (S114); a terminal EBITDA multiple carries implied growth and return (S166); P/E and EV/EBITDA differ with capital intensity, leverage, cash and tax (S021); warranted multiples depend on ROIC, growth and the depreciation factor (S021, S061). A DCF whose value is mostly a terminal multiple is multiples analysis dressed up as a DCF (S021).
- **Use on a stock decision**: when a user says a stock is cheap or expensive on a multiple, translate it into the growth, return and duration it implies, then run those against base rates.
- **Scoring guidance**: a case resting on a peer multiple alone scores low; a case that states what the multiple implies and checks it scores high. His example: a 13.0 times EBITDA terminal multiple implied 6% growth and a 150% return on incremental capital in perpetuity (S166).
- **Quote**: "Multiples are not valuation. They are a shorthand for the valuation process." (S052)
- **Quote**: "So a 15.0 price-earnings multiple can imply very different levels of corporate performance, a fact that the simplicity of the multiple obscures." (S114)
- **Sources**: 13 (S008, S021, S031, S040, S044, S052, S061, S074, S076, S114, S166, S192, S208)

## 13. Free cash flow with intangibles capitalized *(supporting tool; good losses versus bad losses, stock-based compensation as an expense)*

- **What it is**: value is the present value of free cash flow, NOPAT minus investment in future growth. Because most investment is now intangible and expensed, reported earnings and ROIC mislead; capitalize the investment part of R&D and SG&A, and treat stock-based compensation as a real expense (S036, S049, S032).
- **Use on a stock decision**: before calling a money-loser bad or a high-ROIC firm great, sort losses from investment from losses from operations, and rebuild ROIC with intangibles capitalized.
- **Scoring guidance (studies)**: matched-sample test 1980 to 2017: $1 grew to $20.82 in GAAP losers, $7.65 in profitable firms and $1.90 in real losers (S036). Microsoft fiscal 2020 ROIC fell from 52% to 33% after capitalizing intangibles while free cash flow was unchanged (S049). A case that adds back stock-based compensation scores low.
- **Quote**: "The key is to calculate free cash flow, defined as net operating profit after taxes (NOPAT) minus the investment in future growth." (S036)
- **Quote**: "SBC is a legitimate expense that should not be reversed." (S049)
- **Sources**: 13 (S021, S026, S028, S029, S032, S036, S041, S044, S049, S053, S074, S076, S166)

## 14. Margin of safety as a discount to expected value *(named; three-scenario expected value)*

- **What it is**: he restates Graham's margin of safety as the gap between price and probability-weighted value. Risk for a long-term holder is permanent loss, not volatility (S150, S197).
- **Steps**: (1) find the trigger (sales, costs or investment) that moves value most; (2) build at least a high, a low and a consensus scenario; the consensus is what the price implies (S197); (3) assign probabilities and sum; (4) buy only at a large discount (S130, S148).
- **Use on a stock decision**: the buy test after the expectations work. Where outcomes are narrow, only a non-consensus view creates a margin of safety; where they are wide, the consensus case can still leave a good or bad bet (S197).
- **Scoring guidance**: his worked case: low $12 at 20%, consensus $50 at 50%, high $82 at 30% gives $52 against a $50 price, which is almost no margin (S197). A margin of safety claim scores only if the user holds a different view and has good odds of being right (S208).
- **Quote**: "Margin of safety can be restated as a discount to expected value." (S197)
- **Quote**: "We generally recommend considering at least three outcomes: a high value, a low value, and a consensus value." (S197)
- **Quote**: "means you have a different view and that the probability of you being right is high" (S208)
- **Sources**: 11 (S031, S114, S130, S135, S138, S148, S149, S150, S164, S197, S208)

## 15. Steady-state value plus future value creation *(named; Miller and Modigliani split, the commodity P/E)*

- **What it is**: value equals the value of today's earnings held flat forever plus the value of future value creation, which is investment times the spread times the competitive advantage period. The steady-state multiple is one over the cost of equity (S061, S114). It splits any P/E into a commodity part and a franchise part. Greenwald's asset value, earnings power and growth layers map onto it (S099, S149).
- **Use on a stock decision**: compute the steady-state value first. If the price is below it, the market expects no value creation or doubts the earnings; if well above it, ask what spread and duration the excess needs.
- **Scoring guidance (his numbers)**: at an 8% cost of equity the commodity P/E is 12.5, so a stock above 12.5 times current earnings is priced for value creation (S114). Steady-state value explained about two-thirds of the market's value since 1961 (S114); present value of growth averaged 35% of the S&P 500's value (S025). Worked retailer: steady-state value about $105 a share against a $74 price, so the market priced no value creation (S130).
- **Quote**: "Simplistically, we can say that the market expects a company to create shareholder value if its stock trades at above 12.5 times current earnings." (S114)
- **Quote**: "The final piece, and the one that demands the most skepticism, is the value of growth, or more accurately, future value creation." (S099)
- **Sources**: 11 (S019, S025, S026, S044, S049, S052, S061, S099, S114, S130, S149)

## 16. The paradox of skill and choosing the game *(named)*

- **What it is**: results are skill plus luck and the variances add. As everyone gets more skilled, the spread of skill narrows and luck matters more for relative results (S122, S126). So pick games where your skill beats the other players', not just games you are good at.
- **Parts**: three prescriptions: find realms where the variance of skill is still wide; think relative, not absolute; focus on process (S126). The poker table: indexers who left were on average the weaker players, so active investors now face stronger ones (S053). Find easy games where you have more analytical skill than the other side (S059).
- **Use on a stock decision**: ask whether the user has an edge over the likely buyer or seller in this name (a heavily covered megacap versus a thin, complex or forced-selling situation).
- **Scoring guidance**: a thesis in the most crowded, best-covered corner of the market with no stated edge scores low on base rates and sizing; a thesis that names why this game is easier scores higher.
- **Quote**: "Your absolute skill is not what matters; it’s your relative skill." (S122)
- **Quote**: "Find realms where the variance of skill is still wide." (S126)
- **Sources**: 11 (S053, S054, S059, S060, S064, S076, S093, S098, S122, S126, S133)

## 17. Capital allocation and wealth transfers *(supporting tool; the golden rule of buybacks, M&A value test)*

- **What it is**: all capital has an opportunity cost, and every buyback, issuance or deal is a bet on price versus value. Buying back undervalued stock moves value to ongoing holders; buying back overvalued stock moves it to sellers (S032, S038).
- **Parts**: the golden rule of buybacks (S032); three buyback schools: fair value, intrinsic value, and accounting-driven or impure motives (S032, S081); M&A value equals present value of synergies minus premium, and shareholder value at risk is the premium over the buyer's value in a cash deal (S032, S025); M&A base rates: cash beats stock, operational beats transformational, lower premiums beat higher (S032); EPS accretion says nothing about value (S025, S032).
- **Use on a stock decision**: check what management is doing with its own stock and its deals, and whether it steers by long-term value per share.
- **Scoring guidance**: a bull case built on EPS accretion from a deal or buyback scores low; one that tests price against value and synergies against premium scores high. Roughly two-thirds of deals destroy value for the buyer (S208).
- **Quote**: "A company should repurchase its shares only when its stock is trading below its expected value and when no better investment opportunities are available." (S032)
- **Quote**: "All roads in managerial evaluation lead to capital allocation." (S074)
- **Sources**: 11 (S022, S024, S025, S029, S032, S038, S052, S061, S074, S081, S208)

## 18. Contrarian streak plus a calculator *(Seth Klarman's line, which he adopts)*

- **What it is**: contrarianism supplies the idea; the calculator checks whether the one-sided view has actually mispriced expectations and left a margin of safety. Going against the crowd is not enough, because the consensus is often right (S099, S122, S060).
- **Steps**: (1) when views are uniformly bullish or bearish, dispassionately examine the other side (S057); (2) reverse-engineer what the price implies; (3) bet only if the implied expectations are out of line with a reasonable view of fundamentals (S099, S056).
- **Use on a stock decision**: a user buying because a stock is hated, or selling because it is loved, must show the expectations gap.
- **Scoring guidance**: contrarian for its own sake scores low; contrarian with a stated expectations gap and margin of safety scores high.
- **Quote**: "Successful contrarian investing isn’t about going against the grain per se, it’s about exploiting expectations gaps." (S177)
- **Quote**: "Being a contrarian for the sake of being a contrarian is a bad idea." (S060)
- **Sources**: 11 (S056, S057, S059, S060, S098, S099, S122, S138, S143, S149, S177)

## 19. Probability, confidence and Bayesian updating *(unnamed as a group; strength versus weight of evidence)*

- **What it is**: probability is the chance a statement is true; confidence is how sound the basis for that estimate is (S030). Update beliefs in the right direction and size as evidence arrives (S060, S076, S183). People overweight the strength of evidence and underweight its weight, or sample size (S055, S057, S059).
- **Parts**: three dimensions of confidence: reliability of evidence, range of reasonable opinion, responsiveness to new information (S030). Strength versus weight: 7 tails in 10 flips (strong, light) breeds overconfidence; 5,100 in 10,000 (faint, heavy) breeds underconfidence (S055, S057, S059). Three sources of probability: frequency, propensity, subjective (S030, S076, S197).
- **Use on a stock decision**: separate how likely the thesis is from how confident the user should be in that number; lower confidence argues for a smaller weight even at the same expected value.
- **Scoring guidance**: a high-conviction case built on a few vivid data points scores low; one that sizes the evidence and updates in small steps scores high.
- **Quote**: "That nuance may be relevant for determining the appropriate weighting of securities within a portfolio or evaluating diversification." (S030)
- **Quote**: "Forecasters tend to be overconfident when the strength is high and the weight is low." (S055)
- **Sources**: 10 (S030, S040, S055, S057, S059, S060, S076, S089, S183, S197)

## 20. Measuring the moat *(supporting tool; magnitude and duration of value creation)*

- **What it is**: sustainable value creation has two dimensions, the size of the spread of ROIC over the cost of capital and how long it lasts. Start with the industry, then the firm: lay of the land, five forces (most time on entry and rivalry), and disruption (S072).
- **Parts**: margin versus turns: consumer advantages show as high margin, production advantages as high turns (S074, S154); learning curve, network effects, scale, switching costs (S072); a brand alone is not a moat (S072).
- **Use on a stock decision**: a moat is only worth what the market has not already priced, so the moat feeds the expectations work and the fade rate; it is not a reason to buy on its own.
- **Scoring guidance (his thresholds)**: average absolute change in market share of 2.0 or less over five years marks a stable industry (S072); an HHI above 1,800 marks reduced rivalry (S072); a doubling of cumulative output cuts unit costs about 20% for the median firm (S072).
- **Quote**: "Sustainable value creation has two dimensions: the magnitude of the spread between a company’s return on invested capital and the cost of capital and how long it can maintain a positive spread." (S072)
- **Quote**: "But value creation by itself does not lead to superior stock price performance if the market fully anticipates that value creation." (S072)
- **Sources**: 8 (S008, S026, S028, S056, S072, S074, S076, S154)

## 21. Competitive advantage period and market-implied CAP *(named; CAP, MICAP, MI-CAP)*

- **What it is**: the number of years a company is expected to earn returns on new investment above its cost of capital. The market-implied CAP is found by stretching the forecast horizon of a DCF until it equals the current price (S019, S208).
- **Steps**: take consensus cash flows and a cost of capital, extend the explicit period until value equals price, and compare that CAP with the company's history, its industry and its returns (S019); then solve for the return on new investment the market demands over that CAP (S208).
- **Use on a stock decision**: a direct read of how long the price assumes the moat lasts.
- **Scoring guidance (his figures)**: market-implied CAP averaged about 8 years from 1976 to 2007, roughly 5 for very competitive industries and 15 for stable ones (S114); packaged food MICAPs ran 14 to 16 years (S019); high-quality retailers about 10 years (S130). An implied CAP far above the industry's needs a stated moat reason. He admits MICAP is circular by construction (S019).
- **Quote**: "Competitive advantage period (CAP) is the time during which a company is expected to generate returns on incremental investment that exceed its cost of capital." (S019)
- **Quote**: "MI-CAP only tells you how long the market gives a company credit for investing above the cost of capital." (S208)
- **Sources**: 7 (S019, S072, S114, S130, S164, S169, S208)

## 22. Who is on the other side? The BAIT taxonomy *(named)*

- **What it is**: if you expect an excess return, you must know why the seller is selling to you. Inefficiencies come from four sources: behavioral (over-extrapolation, the most persistent and hardest to capture), analytical (same information, better analysis or updating), informational (knowing or noticing first), and technical (forced sellers, fund flows, arbitrageurs without capital) (S057, S059).
- **Use on a stock decision**: ask the user to name the counterparty's motive. Forced or non-fundamental sellers (spin-offs, margin calls, funds with outflows) are the cleanest answer (S098, S122).
- **Scoring guidance**: no answer to who is on the other side means no demonstrated edge. He notes some technical edges fade: spin-offs have not delivered as much value in recent years (S059, 2019).
- **Quote**: "Understanding the investor on the other side of any investment is crucial to having a clear sense of your edge and the motivations of your counterparty." (S055)
- **Quote**: "It’s frequently an opportunity if the person on the other side of the trade is doing something he or she doesn’t want to do." (S098)
- **Sources**: 6 (S051, S055, S057, S059, S098, S122)

## 23. Man overboard and celebrating the summit: READ-DO checklists *(named)*

- **What it is**: a checklist for a holding that moves 10% or more against the S&P 500 in one day, down (man overboard) or up (celebrating the summit), so the decision comes from a base rate and not from emotion (S101, S085, S075).
- **Steps**: (1) earnings or non-earnings event; (2) momentum strong, neutral or weak; (3) valuation cheap, neutral or expensive; (4) quality high, neutral or low (HOLT scores of about 66 or 67 and above strong, 33 and below weak); then read the base rate for that branch over 30, 60 and 90 trading days (S101, S085).
- **Signals from his studies**: after a drop, an earnings-driven fall in a stock with weak momentum going in is a buy signal, stronger if cheap and high quality; strong momentum plus expensive valuation is a sell signal (S101). After a gain, the same strong momentum plus expensive valuation is the sell signal (S085).
- **Use on a stock decision**: the first step on any sharp move in a user's holding, before the thesis review.
- **Scoring guidance**: deciding a big move on feel scores low. He is explicit that the results are a naive default, not answers (S075), and the academic evidence on reversals is mixed (S101).
- **Quote**: "If your state of emotional arousal is high, your capacity to decide well is low." (S101)
- **Quote**: "READ-DO checklists are particularly helpful in stressful situations because they prevent you from being overcome by emotion as you decide how to act." (S101)
- **Sources**: 6 (S057, S060, S075, S076, S085, S101)

## 24. The fundamental law of active management: skill times opportunity *(Grinold's law, as he restates it)*

- **What it is**: excess returns equal skill times opportunity; the information ratio equals the information coefficient times the square root of breadth (S054, S098). Dispersion of returns measures the opportunity for stock selection; dispersion within the winners measures the opportunity for sizing (S098).
- **Use on a stock decision**: mostly for judging a manager or a year; for a single decision, it reminds the user that sizing only adds value if winners differ in size.
- **Scoring guidance (his benchmarks)**: a long-term information ratio of 0.10 is good and 0.50 or better is excellent (S054). Russell 1000 dispersion averaged 51.8 points from 1985 to 2020 (S054); the S&P 500's 33.7 points in 2014 was a 25-year low (S098).
- **Quote**: "Skill without opportunity is futile, and opportunity without skill is wasted." (S064)
- **Quote**: "So for opportunity to exist, you need dispersion of dispersion." (S098)
- **Sources**: 5 (S054, S064, S076, S098, S122)
