# Positions: Michael Mauboussin

Built from the verified batch notes on 100 sources (71 papers, 20 interviews, 4 speeches, 2 op-eds, 2 blog posts, 1 lecture, 1997-01 to 2024-04). Each stance carries the date of the paper or interview it comes from. Topics run most-used first, with the seat's own question (what does the price imply, how often does anyone deliver that, how big a bet does the edge justify) placed at the top. Where a view changed, both are shown with dates and the newer one is marked. He is a researcher, not a portfolio manager: company figures below are dated cases from his papers, not calls on today's stocks.

## 1. Valuation method: read what the price implies (expectations investing, reverse DCF)
Sources: 29 (S019, S025, S028, S030, S036, S044, S049, S055, S056, S059, S060, S072, S075, S076, S079, S099, S114, S130, S135, S138, S141, S148, S149, S164, S166, S169, S177, S184, S192).
- 1997-01: Prices are set on cash flow, risk and how long excess returns last, not on capitalized earnings: "share prices are not set by capitalizing accounting-based earnings, which are at best flawed and at worst substantially misleading." (S019)
- 2002-05: "It often takes 10 or more years of future cash flows to justify a company’s current stock price." (S192)
- 2006-06: The three steps he teaches (from Expectations Investing, written with Al Rappaport; paraphrase): estimate the price-implied expectations with a DCF, find which revisions matter through competitive strategy, then buy, sell or hold on expected value with a margin of safety (S164). His worked case is Coca-Cola at $44 (see Stories).
- 2009-09: "expectations are the key to stock prices" (S148)
- 2011-07: "So figure out what's built in, figure out what's likely to happen, and then it becomes almost an over-under, versus saying I know precisely what those cash flows will be in the future." (S135)
- 2012-08: "The most basic question you must always answer: what’s priced in?" (S130) and "Great investors distinguish between fundamentals and expectations." (S130)
- 2016-08: "Fundamentals are how fast the horse will run, and expectations are the odds. You need to consider those elements separately." (S076)
- 2016-11: "We use a DCF model because it mirrors the way the market prices stocks." (S072)
- 2018-10: "buy low expectations and sell high expectations. Everything else follows from that." (S060) The error he sees most: "I think one of the biggest mistakes you see in the investment business is people fail to distinguish between what’s priced in and what’s going to happen fundamentally." (S060)
- 2019-02: "Do the math. Figure out what you have to believe to justify the prevailing price, and compare that to plausible scenarios." (S059)
- 2020-03: "Anticipating revisions in market expectations is the key to generating long-term excess returns as an investor." (S055)
- 2023-10 (newest restatement): "Investors create the opportunity for excess returns by buying a financial asset when expectations are low and selling it when they are higher." (S025)
- Limits he names: "In the stock market, you have to reverse-engineer expectations from stock prices and the market is essentially perpetual." (S099, 2015-02) and "While it is impossible to know precisely what’s priced in" (S130, 2012-08).

## 2. Base rates and the outside view: how often does anyone deliver that?
Sources: 20 (S024, S030, S040, S045, S051, S055, S060, S068, S072, S075, S085, S090, S101, S113, S138, S142, S147, S154, S193, S074).
- 2002-04: US companies above $500 million grew sales 7.4% a year on average over 1992 to 2001 and under a third kept double digits, while expected three-year earnings growth averaged 14.8% (S193). "Investors and managers must have reasonable expectations." (S193)
- 2007-12: "Both companies and investors tend to be too optimistic about future growth rates." (S154)
- 2010-07: "By leaning more on historical base rates than on individual extrapolation, the outside view provides a better grounding for analysis." (S138)
- 2014-02: "The outside view foils overprecision by compelling the decision maker to consider a range of outcomes consistent with the problem." (S113)
- 2016-09: "Forecasts are commonly too optimistic and too narrow." (S075) He reran Buffett's growth test (of the 200 top earners of 1990, only 14 grew net income 15% a year through 1999): "Buffett’s sense of the base rate is accurate." (S075)
- 2018-10: A base rate is a sobriety check, not a veto (S060, paraphrase): "Might Amazon be the first? Of course. Is it your base case? It would seem optimistic for that to be your base case." (S060)
- 2020-03: "Would you be inclined to modify a base case that assumes a 50 percent probability of 20 percent growth if the base rate reveals a frequency of 3.5 percent?" (S055)
- 2020-07: On the use of base rates: "I think that’s still vastly underutilized and vastly underdeveloped" (S051)
- 2021-06 (refinement, newer): intangibles widen the tails both ways, so old base rates can mislead. "The good news is that intangible-intensive companies can grow faster than their tangible counterparts. The bad news is they can also become irrelevant and shrink fast." (S045)
- 2022-03: "Base rates are underutilized in general because most forecasters place substantial weight on their own analysis and experience and base rates are not readily available in many instances." (S040)
- 2023-03: "Thoughtful forecasts are a blend of base rates and the inside view, but we tend to overweight the inside view." (S030)
- Limits: "The main challenge in applying base rates effectively is selecting an appropriate reference class." (S024, 2023-12) and "Base rates are straightforward to apply for mild distributions and less informative for wild distributions" (S055, 2020-03).

## 3. Skill versus luck, process versus outcome
Sources: 19 (S040, S054, S057, S064, S083, S093, S103, S117, S122, S125, S126, S128, S133, S135, S136, S138, S141, S158, S208).
- 2000-08: "acknowledge the role of luck in the money management/stock picking process" (S208)
- 2007-05: "A trader, or investor, can put on a positive expectation bet (correct process) and still have poor results (outcome) for some period of time due solely to randomness." (S158)
- 2011-03: "On a continuum of skill and luck, with pure skill on one side and pure luck on the other, investing sits a lot closer to the luck side than the skill side." (S136)
- 2012-06: Not all luck. "I want to be clear that I believe that differential skill in investing remains, and that I don’t believe that all results are from randomness." (S133)
- 2012-11: "Investing sits toward the luck side of the continuum not because investors are not skillful but because, in effect, their skills offset one another through efficient prices." (S128)
- 2016-04: Alpha barely persists (year-to-year r of 0.08 for large-cap funds): "when absolute skill is high and relative skill is narrow in competitive realms, luck plays a big role in outcomes." (S083)
- 2019-05: "the more luck there is, essentially, the more patient you have to be to see if a good process is going to reveal a skill over time." (S057)
- 2020-04: Skill needs a field to play on: "all the skill in the world is useless if there is no opportunity." (S054)
- 2022-03 (newest): "All outcomes are the result of a process." (S040)

## 4. Position sizing (education, never a number for a user)
Sources: 18 (S030, S040, S054, S055, S057, S059, S060, S076, S098, S130, S135, S138, S141, S150, S167, S196, S197, S068).
- 2001-08: Size should follow edge, with Kelly offered as a frame and a warning: "The Kelly Optimization Model has limitations and, needless to say, the stock market is more complex than many risk-reward scenarios." (S197)
- 2002-01: Investing has no ante, so pass or press: "you need not participate when you perceive the expected value as unattractive, and you can bet aggressively when a situation appears attractive" (S196)
- 2006-02: "As an investor, maximizing wealth over time requires you to do two things: find situations where you have an analytical edge; and allocate the appropriate amount of capital when you do have an edge." (S167) Both errors cost: "Betting too little leaves a substantial amount of money on the table, while betting too much leads to near-certain ruin." (S167) Fractional Kelly trades return for calm: "It is possible to reduce the strategy’s volatility by taking partial Kelly positions." (S167) Kelly's conditions: "That said, the Kelly Criterion works well when you parlay your bets, face repeated opportunities, and know what the underlying distribution looks like." (S167)
- 2008-12: Overbetting has three drivers: "Worry about the problem of induction, worry about leverage, and worry about incentives." (S150)
- 2010-07: "The other mistake, at the opposite end of the spectrum, is overbetting." (S138)
- 2010-05: Consequences can outrank odds. On holding cash in 2008 with tuition due: "the consequences in this case, of not being able to do that, are more important than the probabilities and so I sat on cash." (S141)
- 2015-03: Sizing adds value only when outcomes spread: "So for opportunity to exist, you need dispersion of dispersion." (S098)
- 2016-08: "Almost all investment firms focus on edge, while position sizing generally gets much less attention." (S076)
- 2019-05: "You and I can have the exact same 25 stocks, but what we know is how we size our positions will have a huge impact on how our portfolio performs over time." (S057)
- 2020-03: "The investment industry spends a lot of time on edge and little time on sizing, and it is likely that sizing decisions are noisy for many portfolio managers." (S055)
- 2023-03 (newest): confidence in the probabilities, not only the size of the discount, bears on weight: "That nuance may be relevant for determining the appropriate weighting of securities within a portfolio or evaluating diversification." (S030)

## 5. Probabilities and expected value: frequency versus magnitude
Sources: 9 (S030, S054, S113, S141, S158, S171, S183, S196, S197).
- 2001-08: "Margin of safety can be restated as a discount to expected value." (S197)
- 2002-01: "the frequency of correctness does not matter; it is the magnitude of correctness that matters." (S196) A likely small win can hide a bad bet: "Stocks are sometimes priced for perfection." (S196)
- 2004-05: "Unfailingly considering every financial situation in probability and outcome terms forms the core of a sound investment process." (S183)
- 2010-05: On a 70% chance of up 1% and a 30% chance of down 10%: "That’s a great probability, but a very poor expected value." (S141)
- 2014-02: Words like "probable" span 25% to 90% in readers' minds, so state numbers (S113, paraphrase of the Sherman Kent finding).
- 2023-03 (newest): "Investing is an activity that is inherently probabilistic." (S030) and "The goal is to invest in situations where the expected value, the sum of the potential outcomes times the probability that they happen, is different than the price." (S030) Updating is Bayesian: "most aspects of investing require a Bayesian approach." (S030)

## 6. What price to pay, and multiples
Sources: 15 (S019, S021, S031, S044, S049, S060, S061, S074, S075, S114, S166, S171, S183, S192, S208).
- 2002-05: "The simplicity of multiples is a sign of inaccuracy, not accuracy." (S192)
- 2006-03: "Multiples are not valuation; they represent shorthand for the valuation process." (S166)
- 2014-01: The same multiple can mean very different businesses: "So a 15.0 price-earnings multiple can imply very different levels of corporate performance, a fact that the simplicity of the multiple obscures." (S114) Low expectations can pay: "Provided the base business is stable and the company can generate some value, the stock of a company with low expectations can deliver very attractive returns." (S114)
- 2018-09: "There is nothing magical about 10 times. The essential point is businesses have different warranted multiples that are important to understand as you buy and sell their securities." (S061) and "Evidence shows that buying a stock at a premium to its warranted multiple leads to poor excess returns and buying at a discount leads to attractive excess returns" (S061)
- 2018-10: Heuristics are rarely fatal and rarely an edge: "heuristics like P/E or Enterprise Value to EBITDA, you don’t blow yourself up because the markets are smarter than you are, so your heuristics don’t really make that big a difference." (S060)
- 2020-09: "shorthands such as multiples are useful only to the extent that they accurately capture value. They rarely do." (S049)
- 2023-02: "Using a multiple does not circumvent the problems with forecasting cash flows and discount rates but rather buries them." (S031)
- 2024-04 (newest): "Most investors and executives price, versus value, the stocks of companies." (S021)

## 7. Growth versus value (view changed)
Sources: 15 (S019, S025, S036, S049, S052, S060, S061, S093, S099, S114, S116, S145, S165, S193, S208).
- 1997-01: The labels collapse into cash flow, risk and duration: "The essence of growth investing, it appears, is to purchase stocks of companies with high returns, and stable or expanding CAPs." (S019)
- 2000-08: "most growth and value funds own the same stocks" (S208)
- 2009-11: "We’d call ourselves value investors rather than contrarian where it makes sense." (S145) and "Value investing tends to have a difficult time in markets that are very momentum-orientated" (S145)
- 2015-02 (older view): "Second, value investing works." (S099), citing a 250 basis point premium from 1927 to 2014; in 2015-06 low price to book and low P/E were "good indicators" of low expectations (S093).
- 2018-10: "value is statistically cheap stuff, which is a proxy for low expectations, but sometimes it’s just bad stuff." (S060)
- 2020-06: "the distinction between value and growth investing is hollow." (S052)
- 2020-09 (newer view): the value factor has weakened partly because intangibles distort book value and earnings: "There is a good argument to be made that the ineffectiveness of the value factor is in part because of its diminished ability to reflect economic reality." (S049)
- 2023-10 (newest): a low P/E is a weak proxy for low expectations; value stocks lose on "growth below the average and P/E multiple contraction that captures fleeting or elusive prospects for value creation." (S025)

## 8. Growth, return on capital and value creation
Sources: 9 (S032, S052, S061, S072, S074, S075, S114, S164, S166).
- 2006-03: "But since growth has no necessary link to value creation, companies can continue to grow without creating any shareholder value." (S166)
- 2006-06: With an 8% WACC and an 8% ROIC the P/E stays at 12.5 whatever the growth rate (S164, paraphrase of his table).
- 2014-01: "the research shows that rapid asset growth is correlated with weak shareholder returns." (S114)
- 2016-09: "Sales growth is the most important driver of corporate value" (S075), but it is not the same as value creation.
- 2016-11: "Growth only creates value when a company generates returns on investment that exceed the cost of capital." (S072)
- 2020-06: "Small changes in growth expectations can lead to large changes in the P/E, especially when growth rates are high." (S052) Below the cost of capital: "The faster the company grows the more wealth it destroys." (S052)

## 9. Moats and the competitive advantage period (a supporting tool)
Sources: 12 (S019, S028, S052, S056, S072, S074, S076, S090, S116, S130, S154, S184).
- 1997-01: How long excess returns last is the neglected value driver. "The CAP for the U.S. stock market, as a whole, is estimated to be between 10 and 15 years." (S019)
- 2007-12: "Most of the proposed causal explanations fall prey to the halo effect." (S154) A great business is not a great stock by default: "A company with great fundamental performance may earn a market rate of return if the stock price already reflects the fundamentals." (S154)
- 2016-08: "The size and longevity of the moat are significant inputs into any thoughtful valuation." (S076)
- 2016-10: "There is no formula. The excess returns for high ROIC businesses are not exceptional, because the market knows they are high ROIC businesses." (S074)
- 2016-11 (older view): "First, the time that an average company can sustain excess returns is shrinking." (S072) and "Economic moats are almost never stable. Because of competition, they are getting a little bit wider or narrower every day." (S072)
- 2019-09 (newer, less sure): asked whether advantage is shrinking, "The long and short of it is I don’t really know." (S056)
- 2023-06: "Companies with high ROICs that can resist the downward pull of RTM are those that will likely exceed expectations for financial results." (S028)

## 10. Reversion to the mean, turnarounds and value traps
Sources: 10 (S028, S075, S116, S119, S125, S138, S146, S154, S166, S184).
- 2004-02: "The classic value trap is buying a cheap company that deserves to be cheap based on poor economic returns." (S184) Fates settle fast: "In other words, the destiny of most firms that live through a downturn is determined rather quickly." (S184)
- 2007-12: About 30% of low-return firms engineered a sustained recovery, and the market already pays for hope: "low-performing companies get higher premiums than average-performing companies, suggesting the market anticipates change for the better." (S154)
- 2009-11: "If you witness performance that is at the extremes, either good or bad, recognize that such performance is unlikely to persist." (S146)
- 2013-09: "Most investors know about reversion to the mean and think that they take it into account as they model corporate performance. But in reality few deal with it properly." (S119)
- 2023-06 (newest): "And 15 percent of the ones that start in the top quintile find themselves in the bottom a few years later." (S028)

## 11. Market efficiency (view changed)
Sources: 12 (S028, S044, S056, S057, S059, S126, S148, S176, S177, S195, S208, S130).
- 2000-08: Markets are well functioning, with "still no way to game the system" (S208)
- 2005-03: "heterogeneous investors create markets, which remain mostly efficient but periodically go to excesses." (S176)
- 2009-09 (how his view moved): "I started as a pretty strong believer in efficient markets, for classical reasons." (S148) He then moved to arbitrage limits and, after Santa Fe, to markets as complex adaptive systems (S148, paraphrase).
- 2012-11: "But it is a random walk only because investors are so collectively skillful." (S126)
- 2019-05: "that should be your default assumption is that markets are pretty smart, and probably smarter than you are." (S057)
- 2019-09: Inefficiency appears when the crowd stops being diverse. "By far, the most likely to be violated is diversity." (S056)
- 2021-08 (newest): "Markets are not completely efficient, of course, but by and large markets are reasonably effective at reflecting known information in prices." (S044)

## 12. Index funds and active management (view sharpened)
Sources: 16 (S008, S027, S053, S060, S064, S068, S076, S083, S093, S098, S122, S128, S135, S147, S155, S176).
- 1999-02: "In 1998, the S&P 500 index outperformed 90% of active money managers for the third straight year." (S008)
- 2005-03: "The efficient market hypothesis offers a practically sound prescription: most investors are best served investing in low cost, passive index funds." (S176)
- 2009-09: "investors would be better off with index funds" (S147)
- 2012-11: Not everyone can index: "There has to be some active management in order to ensure that information is reflected in prices." (S128)
- 2016-08: "The truth is that weak players, whom the strong players require to generate excess returns, are fleeing at a record pace." (S076)
- 2017-06: "Are investors indexing because the market is efficient, or is the market efficient because investors are indexing?" (S064)
- 2018-10: "I think it’s actually gotten harder to generate alpha, even though intuitively you might think fewer participants should make the game easier." (S060)
- 2020-05 (newest): he rejects the view that indexing makes active easier: "you are left playing the stronger players, making it harder, not easier, to generate risk-adjusted excess returns, or alpha." (S053)

## 13. Market timing and macro forecasting
Sources: 12 (S024, S030, S049, S052, S054, S062, S098, S113, S135, S145, S146, S089).
- 2009-11 (a rare dated macro lean): a regression of rebound size on prior decline pointed to US GDP growth of 8 to 9% in 2010 against a consensus near 2.5%; "we would lean towards the notion that it will be better than people think." (S145)
- 2011-07: "One of the things I know by studying expert performance is no one really does know what's going to happen." (S135)
- 2014-02: A one-year S&P 500 forecast should sit near the long-run average (S113, paraphrase).
- 2015-03: "There is little evidence that mutual fund and hedge fund managers can time the market." (S098)
- 2018-06: He prepares rather than predicts: "Maintain a vigilant watch for signals that indicate the future may be different than the past." (S062)
- 2020-04: "The evidence suggests that most investors are not skillful at timing the market." (S054)
- 2020-09: Macro factors are mostly outside an investor's control and not a source of excess returns (S049, paraphrase).
- 2023-12 (newest): expert forecasts "were little better than chance and usually worse than those produced by simple extrapolation algorithms." (S024)

## 14. Interest rates, inflation, cost of capital and the equity risk premium
Sources: 9 (S022, S030, S031, S052, S114, S130, S149, S166, S025).
- 2006-03: "Some academics suggest the equity risk premium is in the 3 to 4 percentage point range, which strikes us as reasonable." (S166) On beta: "Beta is wonderful theoretically but fails practically and empirically." (S166)
- 2009-02: "Volatility is a broad proxy for the cost of capital." (S149)
- 2012-08: "Certainly, the cost of capital appears low by any historical standard." (S130)
- 2020-06: "No one knows where interest rates or the ERP are headed" (S052)
- 2023-02: "Periods when the cost of capital is high are followed by periods of returns that are above normal, on average, and a low cost of capital tends to precede subpar returns." (S031)
- 2023-03: Inflation: no forecast on record. He treats the 2010 warning that quantitative easing would cause inflation, which did not play out, as a case where "reasonable people could disagree on the consequences of quantitative easing" (S030)
- 2024-02 (newest): "All things being equal, declining interest rates are good for asset prices because future cash flows are worth more when they are discounted at a lower rate." (S022) and the easy-money returns are not a baseline: "The CAGR for the S&P 500 was 9.6 percent from 1928 to 2023, so the returns during the easy money era were exceptional" (S022)

## 15. Cycles, bubbles and crowded trades
Sources: 13 (S024, S027, S031, S055, S056, S059, S062, S076, S122, S130, S148, S153, S176).
- 2005-03: "Also, bubbles tend to pop rather than deflate slowly." (S176)
- 2007-12: "Our estimate is 80 percent of the market’s largest moves in the past 60 years are attributable to endogenous activity." (S153) and "prosperity can breed an unhealthy sense of security." (S153)
- 2009-09: "bubbles appear when there is upside price momentum and easy credit" (S148)

- 2013-07: "Most booms and busts start with a kernel of truth and run to extremes." (S122)

- 2023-02 (newest): "Asset prices are often vulnerable to decline when risk is perceived to be low." (S031)

## 18. Cash
Sources: 4 (S032, S057, S098, S141).
- 2010-05: He held the most cash of his life in 2008 while bullish on the long term, because a fixed obligation made the downside intolerable (S141, see position sizing).
- 2015-03: "Since index funds are basically fully invested and the market tends to go up over time, holding cash creates a modest performance drag for active managers." (S098)
- 2019-05: Steady inflows let a buyer lean against the cycle: "He always has money coming in the door. That really helps for you to be able to behave in a way that counters this pro-cyclicality." (S057)
- 2022-12 (corporate cash): "But companies that hold excess cash and short-term investments should be subject to scrutiny about their capital allocation choices and plans." (S032)

## 19. Buybacks and dividends (view refined)
Sources: 10 (S022, S025, S029, S032, S053, S059, S061, S074, S081, S135).
- 2011-07 (older, simpler): "I would be encouraged if more companies chose to be more systematic in returning cash to shareholders, either via dividends or share repurchases." (S135)
- 2016-05: "The conventional wisdom that dividends make a crucial contribution to accumulated capital over time is wrong." (S081) and "Ultimately, value boils down to cash in the pocket." (S081)
- 2018-09: "Note that a buyback’s impact on earnings per share, whether it adds to or detracts from the bottom line, is independent of whether it adds to or detracts from value." (S061)
- 2022-12: "To be clear, buying back undervalued or overvalued stock does not create or destroy value for the company." (S032) For a holder who is right on value: "But if you own shares of a company because you correctly think it is undervalued, buybacks will by definition increase value per share." (S032) Signal strength: "Open-market purchases, the most commonly used by far, signal the weakest conviction." (S032)
- 2023-10 (newest): "But unlike dividends, which treat all shareholders equally, buybacks that occur at any price other than fair value result in a wealth transfer." (S025) and "There is no evidence that increasing EPS through buybacks creates shareholder value" (S025)

## 20. Stock-based compensation
Sources: 10 (S021, S026, S029, S032, S036, S049, S074, S090, S166, S025).
- 2006-03: Fully diluted share counts miss the cost: "Of course, since fully diluted shares only reflect in-the-money options, the solution does an awful job of capturing the magnitude of the liability." (S166)
- 2016-10: "We argue that C-level executives can be compensated with equity but that the options or restricted stock units should be indexed to the market or an appropriate peer group." (S074)
- 2020-09: SBC runs 15 to 20% of operating cash flow for S&P 500 technology companies, so "capital light" claims mislead (S049, paraphrase).
- 2022-12: "That SBC, a legitimate expense, is added back to cash flow from operations provides the illusion that the business is generating more cash than it is." (S032)
- 2023-04: "Research suggests that nearly all the increase in stock-based compensation (SBC) has replaced cash wages." (S029) and "But tests show that the heavy SBC users have lower total shareholder returns, on average, than the light SBC users." (S029)
- 2024-04 (newest): "One example is adding back stock-based compensation, which we and many others consider a legitimate expense." (S021)

## 21. Accounting quality, earnings and intangibles
Sources: 12 (S021, S036, S041, S049, S061, S075, S076, S114, S129, S166, S208, S032).
- 2006-03: "No distributable cash, no value." (S166)
- 2014-01: "Earnings fail to do this, and managements have a great deal of discretion in determining the earnings they report." (S114)
- 2016-08: "Accounting is one of the main languages of business." (S076)
