# Frameworks: Bill Ackman

Built from the Pershing Square Holdings annual and interim letters (2014 to 2026), the quarterly investor letters, the 2020 hedge letters, the annual investor presentations (2016 to 2026), the 2012 Canadian Pacific proxy letters, the Valeant testimony, and interviews and podcasts from 2014 to 2026. Ordered for this seat: activism and engagement as the catalyst first, then governance, then the asymmetric hedges, then the simple, predictable, free-cash-flow quality filter and the valuation, sizing and capital tools that sit under all of it. Within each group, most-used first. Lines from Pershing Square company text (investment policy, principal risk tables, chairman's statements by board chairs) are paraphrased and labeled, never quoted as his. Scoring lines are guidance drawn from his own numbers and thresholds, not a scorecard he published.

## 1. Long-term arbitrage: be the catalyst that closes the gap

- **What it is:** his name for the core strategy. Buy a large stake in a great business that is underperforming for fixable reasons, then work with (or push) the company until the spread between purchase price and intrinsic value closes. The return comes from the change he causes, not from waiting for the market to notice. He says the catalyst works largely independent of macro.
- **Parts:** (1) a great business with a gap in management, operations, governance, capital allocation or strategy; (2) a price that does not yet reflect the change; (3) a stake large enough to be the largest or second-largest holder; (4) a named set of fixes (new CEO, cost and margin program, separation, buyback, better disclosure); (5) the influence to make them happen; (6) hold until the gap closes, then reassess.
- **The Pershing Square 2.0 recipe (2015):** "Find a great business where there is an opportunity for management, operational, and/or governance improvements. Build a large stake at an attractive price. Work with management and the board to make necessary changes." (S001)
- **Why it is an arbitrage:** sellers of a disappointing great company assume nothing will change; he can change it. "we can buy from people selling because they believe the situation will never change. And then we can effectuate change. And that's a huge kind of arbitrage." (S041)
- **The three fixes he names (2025):** right management with proper incentives, optimal capital allocation, and clear presentation to the market (S064). Elsewhere the five levers are governance, management, capital structure, cost structure and strategy (S010).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** first, on any buy or add where the bull case rests on something improving. Ask who makes the change and how. If the answer is that the market will eventually see it, this seat has no catalyst.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = a great franchise with no structural deficiency, a gap you can measure against the best peer, a named fix, and an owner (Pershing Square or someone like it) with the votes or the relationships to force it; the catalyst does not depend on the economy ("Catalysts for value creation are not dependent on macroeconomic strength" (S067)). 5 = the gap is real, but closing it depends on incumbent management's goodwill or the market re-rating on its own. 0 = no lever at all, or the business itself is broken so no management change can fix it (his Canadian Pacific test was that "CP's network and assets had no structural deficiencies relative to competitors" (S012)).
- **Quote:** "I have come to think of our investment approach as akin to a form of long-term arbitrage, where we invest and then work with our portfolio companies to cause the spread between our purchase price and intrinsic value to narrow." (S001)
- **Sources:** 20 (S001, S002, S005, S010, S011, S012, S013, S014, S015, S021, S039, S041, S043, S046, S050, S052, S064, S065, S067, S081).

## 2. The influence ladder: private first, proxy contest last *(unnamed)*

- **What it is:** how he applies pressure, in escalating steps, and how the ladder has shortened over twenty years as his reputation grew.
- **Steps, least to most visible:** behind-the-scenes talks and ongoing oversight; offer to share diligence privately with the board; a private letter; a public presentation or white paper; board seats; a proxy contest to replace directors (S034, S013). With a 5 to 10% stake he cannot control anything, so the public steps are about persuading other holders (S043).
- **How the eras changed (dated, newest last):** Pershing Square 1.0 (from 2004) was transactional: take a stake and push spinoffs, sales and structure changes from outside the boardroom (S001, S008). 2.0 began with General Growth: join the board, recruit management, hold for years (S001, S008). Proxy contests at Canadian Pacific (2012, won) and ADP (2017, lost but produced commitments) (S092, S027). In March 2022 he named the quieter, invited-in version: "So, if it is helpful to call this quieter approach Pershing Square 3.0, let it hereby be so anointed." (S008) **Newer view (2026):** traditional activism is rarely needed now: "I don't think we'd have to be an activist in any kind of traditional form." (S078) The public lever is not gone: in April 2026 he pitched Universal Music a package of re-listing, board, balance sheet and investor-relations changes as "a complete reboot of the company in terms of how it's positioned in the public markets" (S048).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** to judge how hard the catalyst will be to deliver. A board that invites the large holder in is a cheap catalyst; one that refuses dialogue means a costly, public, uncertain fight.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = the board is open and the owner has enough standing that dialogue gets the answer ("I think we have a sufficient amount of influence that we can get to the right answer with dialogue." (S078)). 5 = a public campaign or settlement for seats is needed. 0 = a controlled company or entrenched board where even a won vote changes little, or a stake too small to matter.
- **Quote:** "activism is only needed if you can't get into the boardroom." (S047) On the fallback: "While we strongly preferred to work collaboratively with ADP's board and management to unlock the company's potential, as we have successfully done in nearly all of our prior active investments, they were unwilling to do so." (S027)
- **Sources:** 16 (S001, S008, S009, S013, S015, S027, S033, S034, S039, S042, S043, S047, S048, S078, S080, S090).

## 3. The proxy contest playbook (Canadian Pacific, 2012) *(unnamed)*

- **What it is:** his template for when the board will not engage. Make the case on peer-relative financial results, bring the replacement CEO with you, and frame the vote as a referendum on the board.
- **Steps:** (1) confirm the company is an underperformer with no structural handicap; (2) score it against the same peers on operating ratio and total shareholder return, and put the loss in dollars a holder can feel; (3) have a proven operator ready (Hunter Harrison); (4) nominate a slate that bought stock with its own money; (5) dismiss single good quarters and outside tailwinds; (6) ask holders to withhold from every incumbent; (7) win, install the CEO, let margins compound.
- **The record:** bought 14% of CP; all seven nominees elected with at least 85% support; operating margin went from 19% to 39% by 2015 and the shares from C$46 to C$197 (S012). At ADP in 2017 the slate lost, but the company's public targets made during the contest became "the floor for prospective performance" (S027).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** whenever a thesis depends on replacing management or the board. It tells you what evidence the change case needs and how long it takes.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = years of last-in-peer-group financial results, a board that still backs the CEO, and a named successor who has done it before. 5 = lagging, but part of the gap may be weather, fuel or mix that peers did not face. 0 = only operating metrics improved; "Absent an accompanying record of improved financial performance, improvements in non-financial operating metrics cannot support a conclusion of sustainable progress." (S094)
- **Quote:** "Proxy contests are about trust, incentives, capabilities, and results." (S093) Also: "we know that 90 days of good weather does not fix six years of underperformance." (S093)
- **Sources:** 9 (S005, S012, S013, S027, S039, S050, S092, S093, S094).

## 4. As is versus as optimized: the gap to the best peer *(unnamed)*

- **What it is:** two valuations. Pay a fair or cheap price for the business as it runs today, and look for a much larger discount to what it would be worth run like the best competitor. The second spread is what an engaged owner creates.
- **Parts:** (1) find the best-in-class peer; (2) measure the margin or returns gap in basis points; (3) name the playbook that closes it (3G-style trade spend and zero-based budgeting at Mondelez, Praxair-level costs at Air Products); (4) value the as-is case without any M&A or platform value; (5) the optimized case is the upside.
- **Worked gaps he cited:** Lowe's EBIT margin about 9% to 9.3% against Home Depot's 14.5% (S033, S034); Agilent more than 800 bps behind Waters (S034); ADP margin from about 19% toward 35% (S005); Zoetis from 25% to 34% (S011); Air Products 16% toward 23% (S023).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** to size the upside of a turnaround and to check that the downside is covered without it.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = the as-is value roughly covers today's price and a several-hundred-basis-point gap to a named peer has a known playbook. 5 = the gap is real but the fix is unproven in this industry. 0 = the base case already needs future deals or "platform value"; his chiseled principle is "Base case valuation not reliant on future M&A or platform value" (S038).
- **Quote:** "We seek investments that trade at a discount to intrinsic value as is, and an even wider spread as optimized." (S011) And: "we believe optimized margins are far higher" (S067)
- **Sources:** 10 (S005, S011, S014, S023, S025, S033, S034, S038, S066, S067).

## 5. Recruit the operator who has done it before *(unnamed)*

- **What it is:** the single most reused catalyst. Find the competitor that outperformed, find the executive who drove it, and bring that person in. CP got Hunter Harrison from Canadian National; Lowe's got Marvin Ellison from Home Depot (S014); Chipotle got Brian Niccol (S033); Valeant got Joe Papa (S025); Air Products got Seifi Ghasemi (S023).
- **Parts:** (1) rate the business above the CEO, because a wrong CEO can be replaced (S080); (2) have a name in mind before you need it (S078); (3) check candidates with expert-network references; (4) prefer someone with a record of the same turnaround, who wants to prove it again (S093); (5) then stay out of the way.
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** whenever a thesis depends on new management. Ask whether the new leader has already run this exact play somewhere else.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = a named leader who closed the same gap at a peer. 5 = a capable executive from outside the industry. 0 = the thesis rests on the current team suddenly doing what it has failed to do for years.
- **Quote:** "finding someone who's done it before is the lowest risk, highest return potential." (S078) And: "We bought 14 percent of the railroad and we had in our pocket Hunter Harrison, who's the greatest railroad CEO of all time." (S050)
- **Sources:** 11 (S014, S023, S025, S028, S033, S039, S050, S065, S078, S080, S093).

## 6. The governance audit: trust, incentives, board *(unnamed)*

- **What it is:** his read on whether the people in charge will create value per share. He checks pay, ownership, board independence and the record before he trusts a plan.
- **Parts:** (1) read the proxy: how are they paid, on what, and can they control it; (2) did directors buy their shares or receive them as grants; (3) is there a controlling holder who can block change; (4) compare what the CEO promised on the first call with what happened (S087); (5) separate tailwinds from execution and insist on financial results; (6) treat a change in the metrics a company reports as a warning (S024); (7) when it fails, look at the board first.
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** on every buy and on every re-check after a management change, a big deal or a new pay plan.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = independent board, no controlling holder, management paid in restricted stock on a few controllable per-share metrics, and a record of doing what they said. 5 = decent people, generic pay, a passive board. 0 = pay that grows with tenure regardless of the stock (S093), a controlling shareholder, or adjusted metrics that hide real costs.
- **Quote:** "just look at the proxy and see how they're being compensated" (S041) Also: "you want to incentivize people based on things they can control." (S041) And: "When a company fails, most people blame the CEO. I generally blame the board." (S039)
- **Sources:** 15 (S008, S024, S026, S038, S039, S041, S043, S066, S080, S081, S084, S087, S092, S093, S094).

## 7. The two-factor hedge test: variant view plus asymmetric instrument *(unnamed)*

- **What it is:** the gate every hedge or macro trade must pass. He hedges only when he sees an extrinsic risk the market is underpricing and can buy protection that pays many times its cost. No standing hedge; if both conditions are not met, he does nothing.
- **Parts:** (1) a variant view on a near or intermediate risk that hurts equity valuations (credit, rates, a pandemic, energy); (2) an instrument priced for that risk being unlikely (credit spreads near record tights, low implied volatility); (3) cost at a low single-digit percent of capital and a potential payoff above ten times; (4) be right on both what happens and when; (5) otherwise sit tight. His macro research comes partly from what portfolio companies report on hiring, pricing and demand (S036).
- **Track record he cites:** bond insurer CDS (mainly MBIA) 2005 to 2009, $64 million in and $1.1 billion out; Covid index CDS 2020, $27 million in and $2.6 billion out; rate swaptions late 2020 to 2022, $384 million in and $2.7 billion out (S055). In 2024 he saw views but no fairly priced instrument, so did none (S064).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** when a user asks how to protect a portfolio or whether to raise cash. This seat never answers with a blanket hedge; it asks whether there is a cheap, convex way to express one specific fear.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = a specific risk you see before the market, protection costing about 1% to 2% of capital, payoff of ten times or more, and the risk would also make your stocks cheap to buy. 5 = the risk is real but the instrument is fairly priced, so wait. 0 = hedging short-term marks, running a permanent hedge, or buying expensive protection after the fear is already in the price.
- **Quote:** "First, we must have a variant view about the near or intermediate term future, and second, we must be able to purchase an instrument which offers a sufficiently asymmetric payoff if our assessment of the future turns out to be correct." (S064) When neither is there: "Without extraordinary opportunities, we sit tight." (S064)
- **Sources:** 14 (S001, S008, S009, S019, S036, S040, S041, S055, S056, S063, S064, S078, S087, S090).

## 8. Hedge, unwind, redeploy: the 2020 credit default swap loop *(unnamed)*

- **What it is:** his signature hedge and the full cycle he wants from one. Buy cheap credit protection when a crash looks likely, sell it when the payoff is no longer asymmetric, and use the proceeds to buy great businesses at crisis prices.
- **Steps (February and March 2020):** (1) reason from China that only an unprecedented shutdown in Europe and the U.S. could stop the virus, so credit spreads must widen (S030); (2) buy index CDS on U.S. investment grade, U.S. high yield and European investment grade while investment grade traded near 50 bps, about $70.7 billion of notional (S035); (3) start selling on March 12 at about 140 bps, because the risk-reward was worse, the position was nearing 40% of capital, and governments were mobilizing (S030, S035); (4) finish the exit March 23 with $2.6 billion of proceeds on about $27 million of premium (S091, S035); (5) reinvest the substantial majority into existing and new holdings while stocks were down (S030).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** when a portfolio faces a sudden outside shock. The choice is sell, sit, or hedge and later buy. He picks the last when he believes the companies will recover.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = the hedge both offsets the drawdown and funds buying at the bottom, and is sold on a stated rule (spread level, position size, policy response). 5 = the hedge pays but the proceeds sit in cash. 0 = the hedge is held hoping for more after the asymmetry is gone, or sold too early for lack of a plan.
- **Quote:** "We sold because the risk-reward ratio of holding the contracts at 140 basis points was not nearly as compelling as when spreads were at 50 basis points." (S030) On the result: "We can turn $27 million into $2.6 billion." (S041)
- **Sources:** 12 (S006, S009, S016, S030, S032, S035, S041, S055, S078, S079, S087, S091).

## 9. Rates as the hedge: swaptions and the long-rate build-up *(unnamed)*

- **What it is:** his second big hedge family. When he thinks the market is wrong about the path of rates, he buys out-of-the-money interest rate swaptions, small in cost and large in payoff, to protect equity valuations.
- **The record, with dates:** December 2020 to early February 2021, bought swaptions for $157 million (1.4% of assets) on the view that stimulus and reopening would bring inflation that was not transitory and force the Fed to hike far earlier than futures implied (S007, S031, S056). January 2022, sold most of the hedge for $1.25 billion to fund Netflix (S031). The swaptions were the top contributor in 2022 (S018, S040). From April 2022 he hedged long rates, using a build-up of 3% long-run inflation plus a 0.5% real short rate plus a 2.0% term premium to reach a 5.5% fair 30-year yield (S019, August 2023). He later admitted the 30-year payer swaptions were held too long (S009). **Newer view (January 2024):** at 5% he judged rates had gone far enough and flipped: "We actually reversed our position and instead of being short, the bond market, we went long, the five year and the one year" (S090). In October 2026 he expected rates to stay up because "there's a lot of demand for capital and a finite supply" (S084).
- **Policy note:** the PSH investment policy says interest rate risk is not routinely hedged and is hedged only when asymmetric profits are available (company text, paraphrased, S010).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** for portfolio and macro questions where rates drive the multiple. He uses rates hedges to protect equity values, not to run a bond book.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = a clear gap between your rate path and what options or futures imply, with a payoff many times the cost. 5 = a rate view with no cheap instrument. 0 = a rate forecast used to trade stocks in and out.
- **Quote:** "interest rate swaptions in late 2020 to the present day in light of our concerns that interest rates would rise more quickly and remain at higher levels than anticipated" (S018)
- **Sources:** 11 (S007, S009, S018, S019, S031, S040, S041, S055, S056, S084, S090).

## 10. Over-insuring the house: what a hedge is for *(unnamed)*

- **What it is:** his purpose test for hedging. A hedge is insurance bought only when the storm is visible and premiums are still cheap. It exists to prevent permanent loss and to hand you cash when everything is on sale, not to smooth monthly marks.
- **Parts:** (1) large deductible, small premium; (2) own businesses that survive the flood; (3) cash in the policy in the storm and buy more of them; (4) judge the hedge by the whole portfolio, so it may be held longer than is optimal for the hedge alone (S009); (5) the best hedge of all is an investment you would make anyway (S001, S002); (6) a selective hedge beats both a big cash pile and a continuous hedge.
- **Evidence he gives:** in the financial crisis the funds beat the S&P 500 by 3,200 bps, peak loss 18.2% against 52.9%; in 2020 by 5,180 bps, peak loss 11.2% against 30.4% (S008).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** when someone proposes holding lots of cash until things calm down. This seat argues for full investment in durable companies plus occasional cheap insurance.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = insurance that turns into buying power exactly when stocks are cheap. 5 = cash held as dry powder, which loses to inflation. 0 = a hedge bought to protect a quarter's performance.
- **Quote:** "We think this approach can be best understood by analogy to over insuring your home in advance of an expected storm" (S008) Also: "We do not use hedges to protect our mark-to-market performance from short-term downward volatility." (S008) And: "We believe our asymmetric hedging strategy is a superior alternative to a large cash position or a continuous hedging program, both of which can be a significant drag on long-term performance" (S036)
- **Sources:** 11 (S001, S002, S008, S009, S011, S031, S032, S036, S040, S055, S056).

## 11. Mispriced options and probabilistic bets *(his name: mispriced options)*

- **What it is:** a small sleeve of positions where he can lose most of the stake but could make many times it. CDS, options, and equities priced like options because an outcome is binary: General Growth in bankruptcy, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, Hertz, the Valeant options add.
- **Parts:** (1) the payoff must be highly asymmetric; (2) stay inside your circle of competence; (3) size so the worst case is a percent or two of capital; (4) judge it on return on invested brain damage, his phrase for effort and risk against payoff (S066, S079); (5) the total sleeve stays a small share of capital.
- **Worked cases:** General Growth: a $60 million equity stake, 1.5% of capital, became almost $3 billion (S041). Fannie and Freddie sized at about 3% to 4% of capital because of legal and political uncertainty (S023, S003); by November 2026 he still calls them "long-term, mispriced, probabilistic options" (S082) while admitting they cost about 800 to 900 bps of 2026 performance (S087). Hertz started at 1.6% of assets because its leverage made permanent loss more likely (S021).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** for binary or deeply distressed situations. This seat sizes them as options, never as core holdings.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = worst case about 1% to 2% of capital, best case ten times or more, and an edge from reading the law or the structure. 5 = asymmetric, but the outcome depends on politics with no date. 0 = a binary bet sized like a core position.
- **Quote:** "I typically call these investments mispriced options." (S001) Also: "So in the worst-case scenario, we lose 1.5%. In the best case, we can make a fortune." (S041) And: "We view our investment in F2 as long-term, mispriced, probabilistic options that have the potential to increase in value by more than 10-fold from current share prices." (S082)
- **Sources:** 13 (S001, S003, S008, S021, S023, S024, S038, S039, S041, S066, S079, S082, S087).

## 12. Short selling: the rules and the retirement *(unnamed)*

- **What it is:** how he once chose shorts, and why he stopped. A view that changed over time.
- **The old rules (2015):** short only where there is a ceiling on valuation and a risk of collapse from a bad model, fraud or thin capital; prefer credit default swaps to borrowed stock; treat technicals (free float, buybacks, index holders) as the third leg (S001, S011, S005). At Herbalife the aim was to get regulators to act (S051). When the float shrank and squeeze risk grew, he converted the short into puts to cap the loss at about 5% of capital, then exited (S027, S005).
- **How the view changed, newest last:** May 2015: "I would have to think very, very hard before another public short." (S069) April 2016: shorts only for fraudulent or illegal businesses causing harm (S046). March 2022: retired from activist shorting for good (S008). **Newer view (2026):** "I never actually liked short selling because it's asymmetry in reverse, right?" (S078) Bearish views now go into credit protection or options, not stock shorts.
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** when a user wants to short. This seat argues against shorting stock and for expressing the bear case through a capped-loss instrument, or simply not owning it.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = a bearish view expressed through CDS or puts with a known maximum loss and a catalyst that does not need a public campaign. 5 = a sound bear case with no cheap instrument. 0 = a naked equity short in a small float.
- **Quote:** "A ceiling on valuation is what we deem to be the equivalent of a margin of safety for long investments." (S001) And: "short selling is just inherently treacherous." (S039)
- **Sources:** 14 (S001, S005, S008, S011, S013, S025, S027, S039, S046, S051, S066, S069, S078, S079).

## 13. Simple, predictable, free-cash-flow-generative: the stone tablet

- **What it is:** his quality filter, which after the Valeant loss he had chiseled into granite and placed on every desk. It decides what is worth fixing and what is worth owning at all.
- **Parts (the chiseled principles):** simple, predictable, free-cash-flow-generative; formidable barriers to entry; limited exposure to extrinsic factors; strong financial profile with low leverage; minimal dependence on capital markets; typically liquid North American large caps; management with integrity, a record and sensible pay; typically no controlling shareholder (S038, S066). Royalty-like and capital-light models are the favorite form: franchisors, music rights, card networks, fee-based hotels (S044, S041, S080).
- **The predictability test:** if he cannot model the cash flows over ten to twenty years within a reasonable band, the name goes in the "Don't Know" pile (S001, S080).
- **How the view changed:** early in his career he bought cheap stocks; now quality comes first. **Newer view (June 2026):** "the biggest change over time is an appreciation for the importance of what we call business quality, long-term, durable, protected, non-disruptible growth." (S042)
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** before anything else on a buy. If it fails, the activist case does not rescue it, and he waives a principle only with mitigating factors and a smaller size (S066).
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = a dominant, capital-light, royalty-like business with pricing power and low debt whose cash flows you can sketch for a decade. 5 = a good business with some commodity, rate or regulatory exposure. 0 = complex, capital-hungry or acquisition-driven, where you cannot explain how it makes money.
- **Quote:** "I invest in what I describe as simple, predictable, free cash flow, general business, and we look for a business like that, that have lost their way." (S052) On the tablet: "if we ever again veer from the core principles, hit me with a baseball bat." (S039) On royalties: "businesses where you own a royalty on other people's capital are the best businesses in the world to invest in." (S044)
- **Sources:** 22 (S001, S005, S006, S008, S011, S013, S022, S027, S032, S033, S038, S039, S041, S042, S044, S052, S054, S064, S066, S078, S079, S080).

## 14. Insulation from extrinsic factors and the balance sheet test *(unnamed)*

- **What it is:** the part of the quality filter that protects against things no one can fix: commodity prices, interest rates, regulation, geopolitics, and the need to raise money.
- **Parts (2025):** (1) business model largely insulated from macro and geopolitical factors; (2) no need to keep raising equity to fund the strategy; (3) a strong balance sheet. He avoids commodity-sensitive and rate-sensitive businesses outright (S080) and says there is no good hedge for geopolitical risk, so the moat and the balance sheet must carry it (S040).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** to judge whether a drawdown can become permanent. A leveraged or capital-markets-dependent company can be forced into the wrong action at the bottom.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = net cash or modest debt, self-funded growth, little macro sensitivity. 5 = some cyclical or rate exposure, offset by pricing power. 0 = high leverage in a cyclical or capital-hungry model ("the companies that live on edge to kind of maximize their return on equity and then die" (S079)).
- **Quote:** "We endeavor to invest in companies: (1) whose business models are largely insulated from macro and geopolitical factors, (2) which do not need to continually raise equity capital to implement their business strategies, and (3) have strong balance sheets." (S064) Also: "Even a great business with a bad balance sheet is a risky proposition." (S064)
- **Sources:** 12 (S006, S011, S020, S032, S040, S041, S044, S063, S064, S066, S079, S080).

## 15. The market-shut-for-a-decade test and disruption risk *(unnamed)*

- **What it is:** his durability question. Would you be happy to own this if the stock market closed for ten years? He calls judging the moat and the risk of disruption the hardest analysis there is, and puts it first in the AI era.
- **Parts:** (1) close your eyes for a decade: will it be bigger and more profitable; (2) how wide is the moat and who could break it; (3) test the obvious threat directly with field work and partnership logic (Airbnb against Hilton, autonomous vehicles against Uber) (S013, S037); (4) then check the balance sheet, management and price. He names four downside protections: business model, balance sheet, management and price (S041).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** on every core holding, and again whenever a new technology fear hits the price.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = you can name why a well-funded new entrant would fail and the business would still be more valuable in ten years. 5 = durable today but exposed to a plausible technology shift. 0 = you cannot rule out a young founder building the replacement.
- **Quote:** "a business where you can close your eyes, stock market shuts for a decade, and you know that 10 years from now it's going to be a more valuable, more profitable company" (S039) Also: "what's the risk that this business is disrupted by a 19 year old who left Stanford to found a company" (S087)
- **Sources:** 12 (S013, S037, S039, S041, S042, S043, S044, S054, S064, S078, S080, S087).

## 16. Value is the present value of cash, bought with a margin of safety *(unnamed; credits Graham)*

- **What it is:** his valuation core. A business is worth the cash it will pay its owners over its life, per share. Because the coupons are uncertain, only predictable businesses can be valued, and he buys at a big discount to a rough estimate.
- **Parts:** (1) model years two through twenty, not next year; (2) in a shock, losing one or two years of cash usually costs only 5% to 10% of value for a strong balance sheet (S006); (3) flip the P/E into an earnings yield and compare it with Treasurys (S044, S039); (4) set the price so an acceptable return survives if growth estimates are wrong (S022); (5) a hurdle of about 20% a year (S078); (6) short term, multiple changes can swamp EPS growth; long term, growth wins (S022).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** on every buy, add and trim, and especially when a scare drives the price on next year's numbers.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = a price that still earns about 20% a year on conservative growth, with a margin for being 30% wrong. 5 = a fair price for the quality, where returns depend on growth estimates holding. 0 = a price that needs heroic growth; "You can buy the best business in the world and if you overpay, you're not going to earn particularly attractive returns." (S039)
- **Quote:** "The value of a business is determined by the present value of the cash it generates over its lifetime, not based on what next year's earnings are going to be." (S002) And: "And the key is to buy at a price that represents a big discount to that approximation." (S039)
- **Sources:** 13 (S001, S002, S006, S022, S024, S026, S039, S040, S042, S044, S078, S079, S082).

## 17. The library and the price trigger *(his name: the library)*

- **What it is:** his entry method. Study a few hundred great businesses for years, keep the models current, and buy when a scare, a miss or a narrative cuts the price far below value.
- **Parts:** (1) the library: companies that pass the quality filter; (2) the trigger: a temporary problem, a guidance miss, a macro sell-off or a fear he can show is wrong; (3) check that intrinsic value did not fall with the price; (4) move fast and buy a full position.
- **How the trigger changed:** **older:** wait for management to stumble and buy from disappointed holders, then fix it (Chipotle 2016) (S026, S087). **Newer (2023 to 2026):** buy great companies the market has thrown out over a narrative or a capex scare, with no fix needed: Alphabet after ChatGPT at about 16 times forward earnings, Amazon after the April 2025 tariff sell-off at about 25 times, Meta at about 20 times, Uber on autonomous-vehicle fears (S010, S038, S087).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** to judge whether a sell-off in a quality name is a buying chance or the start of a break.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = a name you have followed for years, now 30% to 40% cheaper on a fear you can rebut with numbers. 5 = cheap, but you have not done the work. 0 = an exciting new name you have just found, at a full price.
- **Quote:** "We have always believed that a good time to buy a great business is when it is in temporary trouble." (S004) And: "we call it a library as opposed to a stable. We've built a library of very high-quality companies that we'd love to own." (S047) The older form: "Historically, we wait for management to make mistakes. And then we buy a stake from a disappointed shareholder to help fix the business." (S087)
- **Sources:** 15 (S004, S010, S017, S019, S020, S022, S026, S031, S038, S041, S047, S064, S078, S080, S087).

## 18. Permanent capital, time arbitrage and no margin *(his name for the risk part: the sleep-at-night approach)*

- **What it is:** the capital structure that lets the rest work. Capital that cannot leave, no margin leverage, and only long-dated investment-grade bonds without mark-to-market covenants. Then he can buy what forced sellers dump and never sell mid-campaign.
- **Parts:** (1) no margin debt, so no forced sales; (2) modest long-term bonds, about 15% to 20% of assets, no mark-to-market covenants (S009, S015); (3) permanent capital, so redemptions never force selling at the bottom; (4) treat pod shops and index funds as forced, short-term price setters to buy from (S020, S047). He dates his outperformance to the shift from redeemable to perpetual capital in early 2018 (S018, S064).
- **Link to activism:** an open-ended fund kept only about two-thirds of capital in the activist strategy because it had to hold back cash for redemptions (S023).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** to ask whether the person holding the stock can stay through the drawdown. Leverage or short-term money turns a good thesis into a forced sale.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = unlevered, long-horizon capital that can add on the way down. 5 = some leverage with long-dated terms. 0 = margin debt or money that will leave in a bad quarter.
- **Quote:** "Volatility is the enemy of the asset manager with short-term capital, and the friend of the investor with permanent capital." (S063) Also: "We call this approach the sleep-at-night approach to risk management. If I can't, we won't." (S001) On activism: "Being forced to sell an investment in the middle of an activist campaign can be damaging to the execution of that investment" (S023)
- **Sources:** 17 (S001, S009, S011, S015, S018, S020, S023, S024, S035, S036, S047, S055, S063, S064, S065, S079, S080).

## 19. Size by the probability of permanent loss *(unnamed)*

- **What it is:** his sizing rule. Concentrate in the best few ideas, and size each by how much could be permanently lost against how much could be made.
- **Parts:** (1) a core of about 8 to 12 holdings (2014), 10 to 12 (2016), 12 to 15 (2026) (S023, S046, S041); (2) a loss budget of about 5% to 6% of capital per idea (S054); (3) smaller for leverage, complexity, legal or political uncertainty, or new structures (S001, S004, S021); (4) the PSH investment policy caps any single issuer at 25% of gross assets at purchase (company text, paraphrased, S010); (5) why concentrate: a 13th name adds little and forces you to own your 12th-best idea.
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** on any buy or add, and when one winner has grown outsized.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = a large position only where permanent loss is minimal and upside unusually large (Universal Music in 2021 became the largest holding for that reason (S017)). 5 = a normal core weight. 0 = a large weight in something with regulatory, political or leverage risk that could cut intrinsic value (his Valeant lesson (S004)).
- **Quote:** "We size investments based on our estimate of the probability of our suffering a permanent loss of capital compared to the opportunity for potential gain." (S017) And: "you're forced to own your 12th best idea, as opposed to more of your best idea" (S087)
- **Sources:** 11 (S001, S003, S004, S017, S021, S023, S041, S046, S054, S066, S087).

## 20. Structural catalysts: separations, sum of the parts, index inclusion *(unnamed)*

- **What it is:** catalysts that change how the market values the same cash flows: break up a conglomerate, sell a non-core unit, move a listing, join an index, explain the business better.
- **Parts:** (1) a sum-of-the-parts discount (United Technologies at nearly 30%, Brookfield at about 38%) (S029, S064); (2) a separation or sale that lets each piece earn a fair multiple and fits capital structures to businesses (S028); (3) index eligibility, since passive money now sets demand (UMG's U.S. listing, Brookfield's redomicile) (S016, S037); (4) a sale at full value beats a spin when it also removes financing risk (S025).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** when the business is fine but the wrapper is wrong.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = a dated, announced separation or listing change against a measured discount. 5 = a plausible structure fix the board has not adopted. 0 = a discount with no mechanism to close it.
- **Quote:** "We believe the upcoming business separation will serve as a catalyst for significant future share price appreciation as investors begin to value each of UTX's businesses separately." (S029) And: "index inclusion has become an increasingly important catalyst for stock price performance and intrinsic value recognition" (S016)
- **Sources:** 11 (S014, S016, S025, S028, S029, S033, S037, S048, S050, S064, S082).

## 21. Influence without paying for control *(unnamed)*

- **What it is:** why he owns minority stakes instead of whole companies. On the stock market he gets private-equity-style influence over management and strategy without a control premium, heavy leverage or illiquidity, and can add or trim freely. Other holders share the gains without the costs.
- **Parts:** (1) stakes usually under 10% to 20% (S007, S016); (2) influence earned by record and reputation, not legal control (S065); (3) flexibility to buy more of a winner that stays cheap (S065); (4) private-equity value creation with no auction and no forced sale at fund end (S015, S024).
- **How it changed (2025):** at Howard Hughes he moved to control, to build a modern-day Berkshire Hathaway around an insurer whose float sits in short-term Treasurys and whose surplus goes into stocks (S021, S032, S078). He says that model needs control and patience (S042).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** to judge whether a large outside holder can drive change, and whether paying up for control is ever worth it.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = an influential minority holder with board access and no control premium paid. 5 = a passive large holder. 0 = a control deal won at auction with leverage.
- **Quote:** "Our strategy is to acquire smaller pieces of superb businesses over which we have substantial influence, rather than controlling interests in lower quality businesses." (S065) And: "we do not pay a premium for control in investment banking-led auctions that are designed to maximize value for the seller, nor do we materially leverage the balance sheet of a portfolio company to enhance our returns" (S015)
- **Sources:** 16 (S007, S009, S015, S016, S021, S023, S024, S032, S037, S041, S042, S047, S065, S078, S080, S081).

## 22. The new-information fork *(unnamed)*

- **What it is:** his triage when a price drops or a surprise lands. Is this a business problem or a capital-flows problem? If the thesis holds, buy much more; if it broke, get out. No middle path.
- **Parts:** (1) separate signals about the business from selling by index funds, pod shops and macro traders; (2) ask whether a miss changes long-term intrinsic value or only a quarter (S020); (3) before adding to a controversial holding, re-underwrite everything: allegations, regulation, covenants, distress risk (S024); (4) after a CEO or earnings-path change, reset assumptions and the required return (S038).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** after any large drop, miss or management change in a holding.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = per-share business results are on track and the drop is flows, so the add is clear. 5 = the miss may be the start of deterioration; re-underwrite before acting. 0 = certainty is gone; exit.
- **Quote:** "You either have to buy a lot more because the stock's gotten cheap and you believe that the new information is not material... or you have to exit because the thesis is broken." (S078) And: "Our job is to understand when negative stock market signals are a harbinger of the future or are simply the effect of short-term capital flows that are unrelated to the long-term performance of the companies we own." (S064)
- **Sources:** 10 (S002, S016, S018, S020, S024, S038, S063, S064, S078, S087).

## 23. The public post-mortem *(unnamed)*

- **What it is:** his mistake review. List your own errors first, separate unforced errors from a market that simply repriced, publish them, and change the rules.
- **Parts:** (1) the Valeant lessons (2017): do not put material value on a management team's deal-making, size for regulatory and political risk, a great record does not prevent big mistakes, and a large price fall can itself destroy value through morale and reputation (S004); (2) platform value is ephemeral (S002); (3) the 2026 version: on Chipotle and Nike he did not adjust assumptions and required returns after changes, and underestimated the problems left by prior management (S038); (4) turn the lessons into principles on the stone tablet (S039).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** when a position has gone badly. This seat asks which principle was waived before it asks what the market did.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = the error is named, traced to a broken principle, and the rule is changed. 5 = the loss is explained mostly as bad luck. 0 = averaging down without re-underwriting.
- **Quote:** "it is a much more ephemeral form of value than pharmaceutical products, operating businesses, real estate, or other assets" (S002) And: "We carefully study our mistakes, both errors of commission and omission, and we share them publicly to keep you informed and to imprint them even deeper in our minds." (S040)
- **Sources:** 10 (S002, S004, S008, S009, S038, S039, S040, S041, S058, S064).

## 24. Red-team the idea, then one person decides *(unnamed)*

- **What it is:** his decision process. Two people go deep, the rest of the team, who did no work but share the economics, attacks it, and he seeks out the best opposing argument. Then one person decides and owns it.
- **Parts:** (1) two analysts do the deep work: filings, five to ten years of call transcripts, expert networks, former employees, competitors (S078, S039); (2) he does about 20% of the work himself (S079); (3) four or five teammates who did not work on it judge it; (4) find the smartest short seller and answer every point; (5) no individual P&Ls; (6) no committee vote; if fully invested, a new idea must displace an old one (S041).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** before committing. The seat asks what the best bear says and whether each point has been answered.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = the strongest opposing case is written down and rebutted with evidence. 5 = the bear case is known but unanswered. 0 = no one has argued the other side.
- **Quote:** "Find the best short seller in something that you own and hear their arguments." (S080) And: "I don't believe in a committee approach to investing where a majority vote determines each portfolio decision." (S018)
- **Sources:** 8 (S018, S039, S041, S054, S078, S079, S080, S084).
