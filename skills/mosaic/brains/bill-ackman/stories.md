# Stories: Bill Ackman

Built from the verified batch notes on the Pershing Square letters, reports and decks (2014 to 2026), the Canadian Pacific proxy letters (2012), the Valeant testimony (2016) and his interviews and podcasts (2014 to 2026). The case studies he returns to, most-used first, with the losers given the same room as the winners. Figures are as he reported them at the time. Lines marked paraphrase carry no quotation marks.

## 1. Canadian Pacific: the proxy contest that made the playbook, 2011 to 2016
Sources: 22 (S001, S003, S004, S008, S009, S012, S018, S019, S023, S025, S026, S038, S039, S040, S050, S067, S081, S087, S092, S093, S094, S037).
Bought a 14% stake from September 2011 because "Pershing Square began buying CP stock because we believed that the company's underperformance could be addressed by a change in senior management." (S093) He had the operator first: "We bought 14 percent of the railroad and we had in our pocket Hunter Harrison, who's the greatest railroad CEO of all time." (S050) The board resisted, the slate won in May 2012, and the operating ratio fell from the worst in the industry to about 59% by 2016 (S067). He sold in August 2016 with the shares up nearly four times and the bonds upgraded (S004, S012). He rebought in 2021 and sold CPKC in spring 2025 when "both EPS and valuation fell short of our expectations" (S038).
Lesson: find the fixable underperformer and the proven operator, then take it to the vote. He offers it as proof activism is long-term: "CP is a paradigmatic example of the long-term sustainable business performance enhancements and shareholder value creation we have achieved in our core activist holdings." (S004) His regret is selling: "we had to sell our Canadian Pacific to raise capital to pay for investors who are leaving." (S039)

## 2. Valeant: the biggest loss, 2015 to 2017
Sources: 19 (S002, S003, S004, S005, S008, S009, S011, S012, S013, S024, S025, S026, S039, S046, S050, S052, S066, S067, S087).
After the 2014 Allergan bid he bought Valeant in 2015 at an average $196 as a passive holder, trusting management: "We have not sought to influence Valeant’s governance or operations because of our confidence in the company’s management team led by Mike Pearson." (S011) Drug pricing, the Philidor pharmacy and lost market access sent it down 83% from cost (S067). He joined the board, replaced the CEO and refinanced, then sold in 2017 (S003, S025). "Clearly, our investment in Valeant was a huge mistake." (S004) and "In retrospect, we misjudged the prior management team and this contributed to our loss." (S004) Later: "We lost $4 billion." (S087)
Lesson: a passive stake in an acquisition machine that needs capital markets and trust sits outside the principles. "The highly acquisitive nature of Valeant’s business required flawless capital allocation and operational execution, and therefore, a larger than normal degree of reliance on management." (S004) It led to the stone-tablet principles and a smaller, investment-centric firm (S009, S066).

## 3. Herbalife: the public short he quit, 2012 to 2018
Sources: 21 (S001, S003, S004, S005, S011, S012, S023, S024, S026, S027, S039, S046, S050, S051, S066, S069, S070, S078, S079, S084, S090).
Shorted at about $48 average in 2012 calling it a pyramid scheme, funded investigators, pushed regulators, and expected a sudden end: "I do not think the stock gradually goes to zero. I think it is more of an overnight phenomenon." (S050) The FTC settled in 2016 but the stock rose as Carl Icahn bought in, the float shrank and buybacks squeezed the short (S027, S039). He moved to puts in 2017 and exited in 2018 (S005, S066): "we underestimated Herbalife’s ability to access debt capital and use financial engineering" (S005)
Lesson: right on the business, wrong on the stock; a short has unlimited loss and a crowded short can be squeezed. "Unfortunately, I had to have the personal lesson." (S039) The firm no longer shorts, and asked for his worst decision he points to the film: "they made a movie about it" (S084)

## 4. The COVID credit hedge, February to March 2020
Sources: 14 (S006, S007, S009, S016, S019, S030, S035, S040, S041, S042, S056, S079, S080, S091).
Reading the virus from China, he judged a shutdown would widen credit spreads from near-record tights and bought index CDS on about $71 billion of notional (S030, S035). "We chose to hedge coronavirus risk rather than sell because we are long-term investor, and we believe that all of our companies would eventually recover" (S030) It cost $27 million and returned $2.6 billion, and he sold it from March 12 as governments acted and put nearly all of it into Hilton, Lowe's, Berkshire, Restaurant Brands, Agilent, Howard Hughes and Starbucks (S091). "We can turn $27 million into $2.6 billion." (S041) 2020 NAV was up 70.2% (S035).
Lesson: a cheap asymmetric hedge turns a crash into buying power. His own critique: the hedges were too small (S009).

## 5. General Growth Properties to Howard Hughes, 2008 to 2026
Sources: 20 (S001, S010, S021, S023, S032, S037, S038, S039, S041, S042, S047, S052, S063, S064, S068, S072, S077, S078, S081, S087).
In November 2008 he bought about 25% of a mall owner trading at 34 cents, reasoning "I thought the assets were worth substantially more than the liabilities." (S039) He joined the board, led the Chapter 11 and kept the equity: "We bought the stock of a company going bankrupt, sort of the most contrarian investment you can make." (S042) "The stock went from $0.34 to $34." (S042) The spin-off, Howard Hughes, then went nowhere for years: "And 15 years later, we haven't really created much value with it." (S042) In 2025 he put in $900 million, became executive chairman and began turning it into an insurance-led holding company, his version of Buffett's textile mill: "So we had our version of a textile business." (S080)
Lesson: asymmetry plus small size, "So in the worst-case scenario, we lose 1.5%. In the best case, we can make a fortune." (S041), and when the market will not recognize a business, control can change the structure (S037, S064).

## 6. Chipotle: board seats, a recruited CEO, and the sale after he left, 2016 to 2025
Sources: 19 (S004, S005, S013, S016, S026, S027, S028, S029, S033, S034, S035, S038, S039, S041, S065, S066, S078, S079, S081).
Bought 9.9% at $405 in September 2016 after the food-safety collapse, refreshed the board within months and recruited Brian Niccol in 2018 (S033, S065). "We recruited Brian Nichol to run that business." (S081) The firm added at 21% below cost once the new CEO was named (S065), and EPS compounded about 35% a year through 2024 (S038). After Niccol left in 2024 and same-store sales turned negative, it sold the rest in November 2025 and admitted "We did not adequately adjust our assumptions and required returns following these changes" (S038)
Lesson: buy from sellers who think management will never change, pick the CEO, add on proof; when the operator who made the thesis leaves, re-underwrite at once.

## 7. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac: the perpetual option, 2013 to 2026
Sources: 22 (S001, S003, S004, S007, S013, S014, S017, S022, S023, S028, S029, S033, S034, S036, S040, S055, S064, S065, S069, S082, S087, S056).
Bought in late 2013 at about $2 a share, sized at 3 to 4% because of legal risk (S001, S033). Court losses in 2017 and 2021 cut the shares, and he dropped the litigation in 2023 "as we did not see a viable path forward" (S055) while keeping the shares as an option on release. The catalyst moved to politics; the stock rose about eight times after the 2024 election and then fell more than 60% in 2026 as the administration went quiet (S082). "And yes, our 13-year holding period has been a long time to wait." (S082)
Lesson: a policy catalyst can be the whole thesis, so size it small and keep the business value growing while you wait; capital rebuilt through retained earnings is the floor (S022, S082).

## 8. The interest rate swaptions, 2020 to 2023
Sources: 14 (S007, S008, S009, S017, S018, S019, S031, S035, S040, S041, S055, S056, S071, S090).
Bought out-of-the-money swaptions from December 2020 on the view that stimulus and reopening would force earlier, larger Fed hikes than markets priced (S056). They returned about $2.7 billion on $384 million, funded the Netflix purchase in January 2022 and offset much of the 2022 drawdown (S055, S018). He then held 30-year payer swaptions too long and sold them in October 2023 at 33% above cost after being 92% above (S009), and in January 2024 reversed to long bonds: "at 5% I thought that it had gone far enough" (S090)
Lesson: an asymmetric macro bet needs a variant view and cheap optionality; it is a timing bet, and he is willing to flip when the level changes.

## 9. ADP: the proxy contest he lost and still won, 2017 to 2019
Sources: 11 (S005, S013, S014, S015, S027, S028, S029, S033, S034, S065, S066).
Offered his analysis privately, was refused, and ran a slate in 2017 (S013, S027). "While our original approach was to work quietly behind the scenes to achieve our investment objectives, we were quickly forced into a proxy contest which led us to publicly air our concerns" (S015) The nominees lost with 31% support, but management committed in public to growth and margin targets, which became "the floor for prospective performance" (S027). Sold in 2019 for a 64% total return when the price had caught up (S015, S034).
Lesson: the campaign itself can be the catalyst, because public commitments give a checklist to hold management to.

## 10. Wendy's and Tim Hortons: the first campaign, 2005 to 2006
Sources: 4 (S001, S039, S042, S087).
Bought about 10% of Wendy's; when the CEO would not return his calls he hired Blackstone for a fairness opinion on spinning off Tim Hortons and published it, and the company followed within weeks (S039, S042). His summary: "buy Wendy's, spin off Tim Hortons, double our money." (S042)
Lesson: a small, unknown holder has to make the case in public with a quantified plan; this is Pershing Square 1.0, change from outside the boardroom (S001).

## 11. Lowe's: the CEO search as the entry, 2018 to 2023
Sources: 10 (S006, S009, S014, S015, S016, S029, S033, S036, S040, S065).
Sold a first stake in 2011 after a quick pop, then watched Lowe's fall far behind Home Depot (S014). Re-entered in 2018 after the CEO search began, and the board hired Marvin Ellison, the firm's first choice (S033). "We viewed the operating discrepancies with Home Depot to be largely a byproduct of poor focus and suboptimal management which could be fixed with excellent new leadership" (S009) Margins rose about 475 basis points and earnings about 2.5 times; he sold in late 2023 after nearly six years with a 175% gain, as near-term earnings turned uncertain (S036, S009).
Lesson: the investment is the person who can close the gap; when the gap is closed, the catalyst is spent.

## 12. Air Products: the recruited operator, 2013 to 2017
Sources: 8 (S001, S003, S023, S025, S026, S027, S067, S081).
The board adopted a poison pill, then agreed to add three directors; a nine-month search chose his nominee Seifi Ghasemi, and margins went from about 15% to 22% (S067, S027). Sold in 2017 at a 104.7% total return, "concluding a highly successful activist investment." (S027)
Lesson: contested start, cooperative finish; the right operator plus a supportive board closes a margin gap fast.

## 13. Netflix: sold on lost predictability, bought back at half price, 2022 and 2026
Sources: 10 (S008, S018, S022, S031, S040, S041, S055, S078, S082, S087).
Bought in January 2022 with rate-hedge profits after a subscriber miss, then sold weeks later at a 44% loss when management reversed on advertising (S055). "It caused us to lose confidence in the predictability of the outcome." (S087) He admits "Probably should have bought it back then, but we didn't." (S087) In 2026 he re-bought after the stock halved, because the ad model had proved out: "So now we had the high certainty business we thought we owned, they proved themselves, at a price that made sense, we bought it back." (S078)
Lesson: certainty of outcome is a requirement for a core position; selling on a broken thesis does not bar a return once the business proves itself.

## 14. United Technologies: management broke the thesis, 2018 to 2019
Sources: 6 (S015, S028, S029, S033, S034, S065).
Bought at about 16 times earnings and privately pushed a breakup; the company announced a three-way separation in November 2018 (S033). Then the board agreed a stock merger of the aerospace business with Raytheon, which he called value-destroying (S034). He sold at a 3% gain rather than fight: "our loss of confidence in management would have also required us to engage in a more comprehensive battle to replace the company’s leadership" (S015)
Lesson: leave when management's action contradicts the reason you bought, and weigh the cost of a second campaign against a new idea.

## 15. Allergan with Valeant: the hostile bid, 2014
Sources: 7 (S001, S013, S023, S046, S050, S051, S052).
Built a 9.7% stake through a joint venture with Valeant at an average $128, forced a special meeting and sued; Allergan sold to Actavis at about $227.51 a share (S023). He called it a different tool from activism: "it was in the context of a hostile takeover which we consider to be a fundamentally different type of situation." (S013)
Lesson: the gain was large, but the partnership is where the Valeant trust began (S052, paraphrase).

## 16. MBIA and the bond insurers: the first great asymmetric bet, 2002 to 2009
Sources: 6 (S001, S039, S041, S055, S056, S090).
Bought CDS on bond insurers and other highly rated, highly levered credits; about $64 million in became $1.1 billion (S055). "we made the investment asymmetric in our favor, meaning put up a small amount of money, if it works, we make a fortune." (S039) The cost was personal: "MBIA tried to put me out of business and did a pretty good job." (S090)
Lesson: CDS give a short with a capped loss; aggressive public shorts invite aggressive attacks.

## 17. Nike: the turnaround he underwrote too early, 2024 to 2025
Sources: 5 (S010, S020, S037, S038, S064).
Bought at about $91 in spring 2024 with two outcomes in mind: "We were confident that the new strategy would either lead to improving fundamentals if the team could execute, or leadership change if they failed" (S064). The founder's impatience brought a new CEO, but estimates kept falling, tariffs hit margins, and he exited in 2025 (S038). "We underestimated the problems created by prior management, both in our initial diligence and in the impact of actions required to effectuate a turnaround" (S038)
Lesson: underwriting a turnaround means underwriting the prior management's damage, not just the new team.

## 18. Pershing Square Tontine, UMG and SPARC, 2020 to 2023
Sources: 10 (S007, S008, S009, S017, S036, S055, S056, S057, S064, S071).
The $4 billion SPAC agreed to buy part of Universal Music in 2021, the SEC objected to the structure, and the funds took the UMG stake on the original terms; PSTH returned its cash in 2022 (S017, S055). "PSTH’s failed business combination with Universal Music Group was a significant disappointment in 2021." (S008) He then built SPARC to fix the flaws, and still found no deal that met the bar (S064).
Lesson: when a structure fails, return the capital and fix the structure; keeping your word on terms built the reputation that won the UMG deal (S036, S009).

## 19. Berkshire Hathaway: bought cheap, sold at a loss, 2019 to 2020
Sources: 6 (S006, S015, S034, S035, S079, S081).
After watching it since 1988, he bought in 2019 at one of the widest discounts to value in years (S015) and added in March 2020 (S006). When Buffett stayed cautious in the crash and better bargains appeared, he sold in May 2020 at a 15% loss and bought Hilton and Lowe's: "We can sell Berkshire at a loss, which we did." (S081)
Lesson: a cheap stock is a source of funds when the opportunity set widens; he calls the sale a very good decision.

## 20. J.C. Penney: the failed 2.0 engagement, 2010 to 2013
Sources: 4 (S001, S041, S087, S090).
A board-level engagement that went wrong; he exited at a substantial loss (S001). Looking back he set it apart from the railroad: "was a much higher risk situation than a Canadian Pacific" (S087), and lists it with his public failures: "Herbalife and JCPenney and Valeant Pharmaceuticals and Borders Group or whatever they were, or more recently, Netflix, something that we sold with a loss." (S041)
Lesson: being rational about a high-profile mistake means taking the loss and moving on (S001).
