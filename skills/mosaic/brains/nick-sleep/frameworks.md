# Frameworks: Nick Sleep

Built from the Nomad Investment Partnership letters (S001 to S024, 2002 to 2013) and the IGY Foundation pieces (S026 to S031, 2021). Most-used first. Scoring lines are guidance drawn from his own numbers and thresholds, not rules he published as a scorecard.

## 1. Scale economics shared (called scale efficiencies shared until about 2007)

- **What it is:** a business that passes the savings from its growing size back to customers as lower prices. Customers reciprocate by buying more, which gives more scale, which funds more savings. Size, normally a drag on growth and returns, becomes the asset, and the moat widens as the firm grows. Costco is his lead case, then Amazon, GEICO, AirAsia, Carpetright, Nebraska Furniture Mart, with Wal-Mart, Dell, Ford's Model T and Southwest as historical examples.
- **Parts:** (1) a fixed or capped markup, so scale gains cannot leak to shareholders quietly (Costco at 14% branded, 15% private label, S003); (2) costs watched in basis points; (3) the customer reciprocation loop; (4) a founder who will not raise price when no one would notice (the Costco jeans story, S003); (5) the paradox that the firm grows by giving more back (S007).
- **When to use it on a stock decision:** first, on any retailer, airline, insurer, marketplace or distributor with a price-based customer offer. Ask whether savings from scale go to the customer or to the margin line. It is his main test of whether a long runway will stay open.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = price-based offer with a fixed markup, falling unit costs passed on, revenue per foot or per customer well above rivals (Costco sales per foot about four times a typical supermarket, S015), and a moat that visibly widens with size. 5 = real scale but the split is even (GEICO about 1:1, still powerful per S008) or the giveback is new and untested. 0 = scale harvested through rising gross margins, cut advertising or squeezed partners, which he calls growth purgatory (Coca-Cola leaning on bottlers, Gillette, Home Depot, S008), or high-low pricing that trains customers to buy on deal (S015).
- **Quote:** "Most companies pursue scale efficiencies, but few share them. It’s the sharing that makes the model so powerful." (S007) Later: "It works because it turns size, normally an anchor to growth and returns, into an asset." (S016)
- **Sources:** 13 (S003, S007, S008, S011, S012, S014, S015, S016, S017, S018, S020, S021, S022). By mid 2009 it described about sixty percent of the Partnership (S016).

## 2. Price to value ratio and fifty-cent dollars

- **What it is:** buy at about half of what a business is really worth and track the whole portfolio's price to value ratio as the scorecard instead of performance. Early Nomad recycled winners that reached full value into new half-price ideas.
- **Parts:** (1) estimate real business value; (2) buy near 50 cents on the dollar; (3) track the portfolio ratio (51c in mid 2002, 65c in 2003, 61c in 2004, upper 60s in 2005 and 2006); (4) the job is to lower that ratio; (5) reopen to new money only if it lowers the ratio (the golden rule, S010).
- **Arithmetic he gives:** if value grows about 10% a year, U$1 becomes U$1.62 in five years, so a 50c purchase that closes to fair value returns about 26% a year; if half the book are mistakes that stay flat, the portfolio still compounds near 13% (S005).
- **When to use it:** on every entry price, and on whether new cash or a new idea beats what is already owned.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = priced at or under 50c of a conservative appraisal; 5 = around 65c to 70c, hold but do not add new money; 0 = at or above full value, which before 2007 was his sell signal (S004).
- **How his view changed:** in 2002 to 2005 this ratio was the centre (S002 to S008). By December 2007 he warned that "an over-reliance on price to value ratio type analysis, which can encourage a tighter range of outcomes than occurs in reality." (S013) and moved destination analysis to the centre (framework 4). Newer view: the ratio still matters for buying and for opening the fund (S015, S016), but it no longer tells him when to sell a compounder.
- **Quote:** "Our aim is to make investments at prices we consider to be fifty cents on the dollar of what a typical firm is worth." (S005)
- **Sources:** 11 (S001, S002, S003, S004, S005, S006, S008, S010, S011, S015, S016); critique in S013.

## 3. Time arbitrage and the equity yield curve

- **What it is:** most holders are renters with horizons of weeks or months, so they cannot price outcomes five or more years out. Lengthening the horizon both raises return and lowers risk, because business outcomes are more predictable several years out than next quarter.
- **Parts:** (1) measure the holding period of other owners (Xerox under four months, S002; Phelps Dodge three months, S010; Nomad's US names fifty-one days, S014); (2) compare it with your own (about 260 weeks versus 20, S011); (3) look for where short-term holders punish sensible long-term spending; (4) measure your own results on rolling five years.
- **When to use it:** when a stock falls on a profit warning caused by investment, or when the case needs five or more years to show.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = thesis pays only over five years or more and the current owners turn over in months; 5 = thesis needs two to three years; 0 = the case rests on next quarter's numbers or a catalyst. He reported the portfolio curve at over 1000 basis points a year of steepness from newest to oldest holdings (S011), with survivorship bias admitted.
- **Quote:** "the return from investing in shares can be both increased and de-risked by time." (S011)
- **Also:** "Those that set the current prices for our investments (the renters) cannot have an eye on long term value, and that, in a nutshell, is the investment case for Nomad." (S014)
- **Sources:** 9 (S001, S002, S004, S006, S010, S011, S014, S018, S020).

## 4. Destination analysis

- **What it is:** ask where the business ends up in ten or twenty years, and the probability of getting there, instead of fixing a price to value at purchase. Born from the Conseco and Stagecoach mistakes. This is the framework the seat's question comes from.
- **Parts:** (1) describe the end state (market share, cost position, customer relationship, size); (2) attach a probability; (3) ask what culture, incentives and model raise that probability (S015); (4) update the picture as facts change rather than anchoring on the purchase case (S013); (5) size the holding to destination times probability (S012).
- **Fund-level version:** turn U$1 into U$10 in a decade, about 15% a year (S010), or U$16 in twenty years (S009); the exact year matters little next to getting there.
- **When to use it:** on every hold or sell call for a compounder, and whenever the price has risen and a trim is tempting.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = a clear end state many times today's size, a model whose odds improve as it grows, and management behaviour that raises the probability; 5 = a plausible destination but the path depends on one product, one manager or a price the firm has not yet earned; 0 = cannot describe where it ends up, or the destination depends on harvesting customers.
- **Quote:** "Destination analysis is consciously central to how we analyse businesses these days." (S013) And on Amazon: "the high weighting makes sense given our understanding of the destination of the businesses and the probability of reaching that destination." (S012)
- **Sources:** 8 (S009, S010, S011, S012, S013, S015, S016, S023).

## 5. Corporate character and capital allocation questions *(unnamed)*

- **What it is:** the second prime determinant of outcome after price. A check on whether management will put incremental capital to good use for a long time, and whether they behave well because they want to.
- **Parts:** (1) returns on incremental capital and how long they last; (2) incentives that point at per-share value; (3) what the price already discounts (S004); (4) a static or falling share count (his screen for no change in share count over ten years, S004); (5) insider and company buying (two thirds of holdings with insider buying in 2002, S003; fifty-five percent repurchasing or insider buying in 2008, S014); (6) founder or largest-shareholder control (over eighty-five percent of the portfolio in 2007, S012; almost ninety percent in 2008, S014); (7) resistance to the locker room culture of short-term targets (S017).
- **When to use it:** before buying, and as a re-check whenever management changes the capital allocation plan (Readers Digest switched from buybacks to acquisitions, S005).
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = founder-run, share count flat or falling, insiders buying, spending aimed at the customer, candid about mistakes; 5 = good business with hired management and option-heavy pay; 0 = growth for its own sake, dilutive issuance, pay tied to EPS, or managers who promise targets without saying how they will be earned.
- **Quote:** "what are returns on incremental capital and the longevity of those returns, are management correctly incented to allocate capital appropriately, and what is discounted by prices?" (S004)
- **Also:** "There are only two reasons companies behave well. Because they want to, and because they have to." (S006)
- **Sources:** 8 (S001, S003, S004, S005, S006, S012, S014, S017).

## 6. What the price implies *(Prices are a language, S010)*

- **What it is:** reverse the valuation. Work out what growth or what failure the current price assumes, then judge whether that is believable. Used to find great businesses priced as if they would stop growing, and to avoid booms priced as if the peak lasts a decade.
- **Parts:** (1) value the business as a cash cow with growth spending stopped (Amazon about U$26 a share in 2006, S011); (2) find the growth rate the price discounts (Costco at U$39 discounted 5% to 6%, U$62 would discount 10%, S007); (3) compare the implied horizon with the holding period of current owners (Phelps Dodge, S010).
- **When to use it:** when a compounder looks expensive on trailing multiples, or a cyclical looks cheap on peak earnings.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = the price implies little or no growth for a firm growing organically in double digits; 5 = price implies roughly the growth you expect; 0 = price implies record cash flow for years in a business with a supply response coming.
- **Quote:** "Amazon has been priced in the market as if it would not grow in the future" (S015) And of the 2006 price: "This is an odd statement to make for a business growing revenues in excess of twenty percent per annum." (S011)
- **Sources:** 6 (S007, S010, S011, S014, S015, S016).

## 7. Deep discount to replacement cost with latent pricing power

- **What it is:** his second named model in 2005. Buy assets that must eventually be replaced at a fraction of what replacement would cost; prices must rise one day to justify new capacity, and supply stays muted until they do.
- **Parts:** (1) price per unit of capacity versus build cost (Pretoria Portland Cement bought at U$20 a ton against U$180 later, Union Cement at a quarter of replacement cost, Zimbabwe cement near one seventieth, S006, S008); (2) a near-monopoly or oligopoly; (3) patience through the cycle; (4) small sizing where confiscation risk is real.
- **When to use it:** on cement, infrastructure and franchise assets in a crisis or a despised market. It is a cheapness tool, not a runway tool.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = a quarter or less of replacement cost with no new supply coming; 5 = around half; 0 = near or above replacement cost.
- **How his view changed:** in 2013 he wrote that Zimbabwe worked (three to eight-fold in dollars) but should not be repeated, since the compounders did better with less stress (S024). Newer view: prefer compounders.
- **Quote:** "deep discount to replacement cost with latent pricing power" (S008)
- **Sources:** 6 (S001, S006, S008, S009, S016, S024).

## 8. Inputs over outputs

- **What it is:** judge a company, a manager and yourself by controllable inputs (research quality, customer experience, cost culture) rather than by share price or annual results, since the timing of outputs cannot be controlled.
- **When to use it:** when a holding has a bad year, when results look too good, and when reading management's goals (Amazon's controllable inputs, S018).
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = management reports and manages inputs (prices, service, costs) and the thesis can be checked on them; 0 = management steers to quarterly outputs and guidance.
- **Quote:** "We would counsel you to think about the inputs to investing rather than the outputs." (S014)
- **Sources:** 5 (S011, S013, S014, S018, S028).

## 9. Mistake ledger: denial, anchoring, drift, and opportunity cost

- **What it is:** a way to classify errors so that behaviour changes. The three mistakes are denial, anchoring on a static picture formed at purchase, and drift. Opportunity costs (a compounder sold) count more than booked losses (a bankruptcy).
- **Parts:** (1) record opportunity costs next to dollar losses (Conseco loss about U$5m; Stagecoach sale cost about U$12m and counting, S013); (2) analyse mistakes more than successes (S005); (3) price the lesson as an asset (S013); (4) list why investors sold Wal-Mart: wrong mental model, structural need to look active, and not believing in longevity of compound (S016).
- **When to use it:** before any sale of a winner, and in every post-mortem.
- **Quote:** "denial, that is the reinvention of reality in the mind because the truth is too painful to bear; anchoring, that is a static, historic vision of a problem; and drift" (S013)
- **Also:** "mistakes can be put on a price earnings ratio of one and the resultant, conditioned, good behaviour on a ratio of more than one." (S013)
- **Sources:** 5 (S005, S009, S012, S013, S016).

## 10. Probability trees and branches

- **What it is:** the share price is a probability-weighted average of all possible futures, but the company only travels down one branch. For some firms the odds improve with each good branch (a culture or a virtuous loop), so long-term success is left undiscounted. Calibrate with ranges, not points.
- **Parts:** (1) build the decision tree and attach probabilities (Zeckhauser's bridge method, S009); (2) give a median plus 25th, 75th, 1st and 99th percentiles (S015); (3) ask whether success on one branch raises the odds on the next (AirAsia, S018); (4) aim to be generally right rather than precisely wrong (S019).
- **When to use it:** sizing, and any argument that the market has priced in the bad branches too heavily.
- **Quote:** "The point is that the odds associated with any of these branches are not static but, in a hugely important way, they improve as one travels from branch to branch." (S018)
- **Sources:** 4 (S009, S015, S018, S019).

## 11. Capitalise the investment the accounts expense *(unnamed)*

- **What it is:** advertising, marketing, R&D, price givebacks and growth spending go through the income statement as costs, so reported profit hides an asset. Separate maintenance from discretionary growth spend and value the cash cow underneath.
- **Parts:** (1) free cash flow after maintenance capex, marketing and R&D (Xerox, S002); (2) discretionary growth spend added back (Amazon about U$500m on top of U$500m free cash flow, S011); (3) share of voice versus share of market for brands (Weetabix, S004; Estee Lauder, Nike, Coke, S009).
- **When to use it:** when a reinvesting company looks expensive on earnings or when a profit warning comes from deliberate investment.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = depressed margins explained by discretionary spending that widens the moat; 5 = spending of uncertain return; 0 = spending that buys growth with poor economics (he asks such firms why they want to grow, S006).
- **Quote:** "In effect the company had been punished by the markets for being sensible." (S002)
- **Sources:** 4 (S002, S004, S009, S011).

## 12. Business-model buckets for the portfolio

- **What it is:** he groups holdings by the business model of the investee, not by sector or country, and watches how the mix shifts.
- **How the buckets changed:** mid 2002: difficult-to-copy franchises about 37%, discounted asset businesses about 27%, deep value workouts about 17%; winners were in the first group and losers in the last (S002). Mid 2009 (newer): scale economics shared over half, discount to replacement cost with pricing power about 15%, hated agencies 15%, super high-quality thinkers just under 10% (S016).
- **When to use it:** to see whether a portfolio is drifting into cheapness tools at the expense of runway tools.
- **Quote:** "Zak and I think of the Partnership in terms of business models deployed by our investee firms." (S016)
- **Sources:** 4 (S002, S003, S008, S016).

## 13. Capital cycle and the anti-bubble

- **What it is:** high valuations draw in capital and new supply that undermine the returns the price assumes; at the same time, a frenzy in one area leaves an anti-bubble of discarded quality elsewhere.
- **Parts:** (1) capex plans rising (mining capex expected up about forty percent, S010); (2) short holding periods in the hot sector; (3) reinvesting profit and debt into land or capacity late in the cycle (MDC, S013; Thai cement, S009); (4) look where the crowd is not (large quality growth stocks in 2006, S010).
- **When to use it:** on any cyclical or theme stock, and to find great businesses that a boom has left behind.
- **Quote:** "When there is a frenzy of activity in one area of the market there is very often an anti- bubble of discarded companies." (S010)
- **Sources:** 4 (S009, S010, S013, S014).

## 14. Longevity of compound

- **What it is:** markets discount all growth stocks for the average failure rate, so the few that keep growing can be cheap for decades. The paying question is how long the returns on incremental capital last.
- **Numbers he uses:** a growth stock stays one for five years about one time in five, and for ten years about one in ten (S016, citing Empirical Research Partners; also S007). A buyer of Wal-Mart in 1972 could have paid over 150 times the prevailing price and still earned ten percent a year (S016). Nomad's firms were about fifteen years old on average at purchase against an average S&P 500 tenure of twenty-five years (S023).
- **When to use it:** when a compounder looks expensive, and before selling one that has risen.
- **Scoring guidance:** 10 = the firm is one of the exceptions because it keeps passing scale benefits to the buyer (S007); 0 = success that invites competitors with no reinforcing loop.
- **Quote:** "The shares of great companies can therefore be cheap, in some cases, for decades." (S016)
- **Also:** "success encourages competition, and capital flows into an industry to compete away the excess returns." (S007)
- **Sources:** 4 (S007, S012, S016, S023).

## 15. Mouse to elephant scaling laws

- **What it is:** borrowed from Geoff West at the Santa Fe Institute. A firm that can grow from small to huge needs three things.
- **Parts:** (1) self-funded growth with high returns on capital if the opportunity set is large; (2) barriers to entry that rise with size; (3) a simple building block that supports growth without re-engineering (Amazon's fixed web model versus complex high street retail, S012). Large is not the same as old: online retailers can be big and still young, held back by consumer habit rather than capacity (S019).
- **When to use it:** to test runway on a business already large, and on a new market entry (a Thai big box retailer, S022).
- **Quote:** "we want a skeletal structure that can support growth from mouse to elephant without too much skeletal re-engineering." (S012)
- **Sources:** 3 (S012, S019, S022).

## 16. Dilution risk and per-share discipline

- **What it is:** value is per share. Equity issued below intrinsic value at the trough destroys a value buyer even when the business survives.
- **Worked example he gives:** a U$2bn firm with 60m shares is worth U$33 a share; bought at U$15; raising U$300m at U$10, U$5 or U$1 cuts value to U$22, U$16 or U$5.50, turning a 68c purchase into par or three times value (S014).
- **When to use it:** on any leveraged, regulated or capital-hungry business near a crisis; and to prefer firms with a static share base (Weetabix, unchanged since 1932, S004).
- **Scoring guidance:** 0 = cannot know the share count a year out (MBIA, sold in 2008, S014); 10 = self-funded with a flat or shrinking share count.
- **Quote:** "Dilution risk occurs when companies issue equity at less than the firm is worth." (S014)
- **Sources:** 3 (S002, S004, S014).

## 17. Sizing by conviction: Kelly and inverting from a hundred percent

- **What it is:** size to the odds, not to habit. His simplified Kelly rule bets 2.1 x p minus 1.1, so 75% confidence gives 47.5% (S006, via Bill Miller). Or start each idea at a hundred percent and work down until the reasons stop (S012).
- **When to use it:** first purchases of a compounder (he admits Costco and Amazon started too small, S007, S012) and on whether to trim after a rise.
- **Scoring guidance (on sizing, not on the stock):** ten stocks were about 70% of the fund in 2005 (S009) and over 80% in 2007 (S012); about thirty percent sat in one investment in 2009 (S016). Zimbabwe-type confiscation risk caps a holding at a few percent at cost (S008).
- **Quote:** "There is another way to construct a portfolio, which is to invert and start at a hundred percent weighting and work down!" (S012)
- **Sources:** 3 (S006, S007, S012).

## 18. Two ways to invest, and inactivity

- **What it is:** (1) buy cheap, sell at a profit, repeat; or (2) buy a great business at a reasonable price and let it grow, which needs one decision to buy and daily decisions not to sell. Nomad moved from the first to the second.
- **How his view changed:** 2003 to 2005: sell holdings at full value and recycle into new fifty-cent dollars (S004, S005). 2009 onward (newer): own great businesses and do almost nothing; by 2013 purchases and sales had all but stopped and he called inactivity a source of value added (S017, S022, S023).
- **When to use it:** whenever a holding is near fair value on a static model but the destination is still far off.
- **Quote:** "the second way to invest is to buy shares in a great business at a reasonable price and let the business grow." (S017) Earlier rule: "The investment has reached full value and will be sold as new investments become available." (S004)
- **Sources:** 3 for the newer view (S017, S022, S023); 2 for the older (S004, S005).

## 19. Super high-quality thinkers and the terminal portfolio

- **What it is:** an office list (fifteen names in 2004) of intellectually honest, economically rational firms whose owners could give up the right to trade because capital will be well allocated for years. The terminal portfolio is where he wanted Nomad to end up; price was the only barrier, so the work is done early and the buy waits for 50c.
- **When to use it:** to keep a watch list of compounders ready for a crisis price (he bought them in 2008 to 2009, S017, S022).
- **Quote:** "This list is a group of wonderful, honestly run compounding machines." (S006) And: "Even though prices are generally high, the trick is to do the work today, so that we are ready." (S006)
- **Sources:** 2 (S006, S016).

## 20. Three investing edges and four psychological hurdles

- **What it is:** from Bill Miller. Edges are informational, analytical or psychological; informational edges fade, analytical ones mean arranging the same facts differently (capitalising advertising), and the most lasting are psychological, mainly patience. The four hurdles he names are social proof, vivid evidence, poor probability thinking and impatience.
- **When to use it:** to ask why the market is wrong on this stock and whether the seat's edge is real.
- **Quote:** "the most enduring advantages are psychological. And the trick here is to first understand them. And then train yourself out of them!" (S009)
- **Sources:** 2 (S008, S009).

## 21. Customer relationship test: cash-in, cash-out and information shelf life

- **What it is:** reduce a business to the customer relationship (cash in, seen in organic revenue growth) and return on capital (cash out). Weight information by its shelf life; next quarter's earnings expire, the business model does not.
- **Scoring guidance:** in 2011 he reported weighted organic revenue growth at Nomad's firms of over thirty percent a year with falling prices, and returns on capital over twice competitors (S020). Near that = 10; growth bought by acquisition or price rises = low.
- **When to use it:** to filter news. Ask whether the event changes what customers get.
- **Quote:** "does any of this make a meaningful difference to the relationship our businesses have with their customers?" (S020) And: "the information that Zak and I weigh most heavily in thinking about a firm is that which has the longest shelf life" (S021)
- **Sources:** 2 (S020, S021).

## 22. Many small things and the aggregation of marginal gains

- **What it is:** an advantage may be a self-reinforcing web of thousands of small actions rather than one secret, and a rival has to beat all of them. Costs must be plan A from the start, not a crisis response. Good costs (staff, service) are kept; bad costs cut.
- **How his view changed:** for about twenty years he dismissed many small things as a fig leaf for no real advantage; in June 2010 he wrote that he may have been wrong (S018). Newer view: a cost culture of pennies is a moat (S022).
- **When to use it:** when a low-cost leader has no single visible edge, which is when bears say it can be copied.
- **Quote:** "an interlocking, self-reinforcing network of small actions may be more successful than one big thing." (S018) And: "Good things follow when you care about the pennies." (S022)
- **Sources:** 2 (S018, S022).

## 23. Robustness ratio

- **What it is:** a measure of moat for a price-based business: what customers save (plus any extra paid to staff) divided by what shareholders earn.
- **Scoring guidance:** Costco about 5:1 (customers save about U$175 against a U$23 fee, shareholders get about U$32 per member), GEICO about 1:1 (S008). Higher is harder to compete against, but very high can mean shareholders are under-rewarded for now. He warned it is not a magic number and does not suit advertising-led brands such as Nike.
- **When to use it:** to put a number on framework 1, and to watch for drift toward shareholders under Wall Street pressure.
- **Quote:** "It is the amount of money a customer saves compared to the amount earned by shareholders." (S008)
- **Sources:** 1 (S008).
