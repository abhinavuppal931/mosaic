# Stories: Nick Sleep

Built from the Nomad Investment Partnership letters (S001 to S024, 2002 to 2013) and the IGY Foundation pieces (S026 to S031, 2021), through the verified batch notes. Case studies he returns to, most-used first, with losers and selling mistakes given equal room. Figures are as he reported them at the time.

## 1. Costco: scale economics shared, the model case
Sources: 10 (S003, S006, S007, S008, S011, S014, S015, S016, S017, S018).
Bought in 2002 near U$30 after margins dipped on new distribution centres, at what he called close to half price. The fixed markup (14% branded, 15% private label) hands purchasing scale back to members, who buy more, which extends the scale (S003). He measured the moat with the robustness ratio: members saved about five dollars for every one dollar shareholders earned, against about one to one at GEICO (S008). Founder Jim Sinegal refused to lift the markup on cheap designer jeans because the customer contract must not be broken: "if I let you do it this time, you will do it again" (S003, quoting Sinegal). He kept a 1967 Sol Price memo framed in the office (S018).
Lesson: the sharing is the moat. "Look around you. How many companies save five dollars for their customers for every one dollar they keep?" (S008) His first Costco mistake, he said in 2004, was owning too little (S007).

## 2. Amazon: the price that said growth spend was worthless
Sources: 8 (S011, S012, S014, S015, S016, S018, S019, S022).
In 2006 he split Amazon's cash flow into about U$500m of free cash flow and about U$500m of discretionary growth spend, valued it as a cash cow near U$26 a share, and read the price as saying the growth spend had no value (S011). After the shares doubled in 2007 he did not sell, and asked "would selling Amazon today would be the equivalent mistake of selling Wal- Mart in 1980" (S012). By mid 2009 it was about 30% of the Partnership (S016). He admitted the starting weight was too low: "we have probably made the mistake of a starting holding that was almost certainly too low." (S012)
Lesson: a long runway plus scale shared justifies a weight that grows with conviction, not a trim on a rising price.

## 3. Stagecoach: the sale he called his greatest error
Sources: 6 (S003, S005, S011, S012, S013, S017).
Bought at 14p in 2002 after a debt-funded US acquisition broke a simple UK bus business and the founder returned to cut back to the jewel. It became his largest holding, the bonds rose above par and the shares rose six-fold (S003, S005). He sold at around 90p; the shares later traded above £2.50. "The mistake was to leave £1.60 on the table" (S013), caused by anchoring on the purchase analysis rather than the destination. In 2007: "our greatest error was the sale of Stagecoach (which has risen ever since sold), not the purchase of Conseco!" (S012)
Lesson: the opportunity cost of selling a business that keeps compounding is larger than the loss on a bankruptcy, and it is never booked.

## 4. Wal-Mart and IBM: the fund that sold too early
Sources: 3 (S007, S012, S016).
A fund firm sold IBM in the 1950s or 1970s and Wal-Mart in the 1970s; the stakes would have outgrown its whole business. He used Wal-Mart's record to show that an investor in 1972 could have paid over 150 times the prevailing price and still earned ten percent a year (S016). "The shares of great companies can therefore be cheap, in some cases, for decades." (S016) and "the biggest error an investor can make is the sale of a Wal-Mart or a Microsoft in the early stages of the company's growth." (S012)
Lesson: the seat's core story. Price matters far less than the longevity of the compounding.

## 5. Conseco: the loss he wrote about first
Sources: 4 (S003, S005, S013, S017).
Went into Chapter 11 in 2002 after losses on manufactured housing loans; he kept the shares and expected them to be worthless (S003). Dollar loss about U$5m, opportunity cost about U$10m. The root error was a static view formed at purchase: "The analytical mistake in both cases was to have a static view of a firm formed at the time of purchase, which failed to evolve as the facts changed." (S013)
Lesson: mistakes hurt more than winners please, so he over-reported it; the fix was destination analysis.

## 6. Air Asia and Games Workshop: priced as if they should not exist
Sources: 3 (S014, S016, S020).
In 2008 Air Asia, the lowest-cost airline (about 3 cents per seat kilometre against 4.5 at Ryanair), was valued below its fleet; Games Workshop had spent a Lord of the Rings windfall on empty factories and weak shops, and its chairman owned the fault (S014). "If the problems are self-inflicted, they are usually within the powers of the firm to fix." (S014) The Air Asia founder later told him that if a plane went into a swamp he would raise both hands with nothing to hide (S020).
Lesson: candour about a fixable problem is a reason to buy, not to sell.

## 7. MBIA: cheap with an unknowable share count
Sources: 2 (S014, S017).
Bought in 2008 after write-downs, two capital raises and new management buying shares; sold the same year because losses on U$680bn of insured assets could not be judged and further equity raises could swamp value, at a cost of about 1.5% of NAV (S014). "the stock's biggest attraction, its apparent cheapness, becomes the investor's Achilles heel" (S014) He later said trust let him change his mind quickly without fear of rebuke (S017).
Lesson: if you cannot know the share count, you cannot know value per share.

## 8. Monsanto: the missed indemnity
Sources: 2 (S002, S003).
In 2002 he found an indemnity to Pharmacia buried in the notes that he had missed, and sold at about cost; the shares later fell to half the sale price. "In our original analysis we missed the indemnification" (S002) and "we are no longer confident of valuing Monsanto with any certainty and the prudent thing to do was to sell our shares for approximately the price we had paid." (S002)
Lesson: lost confidence in the valuation is a sell signal even at no gain.

## 9. Weetabix: a static share base and a low take-private
Sources: 3 (S004, S005, S011).
A family cereal maker that had not changed its share count since 1932, spent heavily on brands and traded off-market on Ofex; bought near £20 against a worth of £70 to £75 (S004). A buyout came at £53.75 and he voted against, too small to stop it. "Before congratulations are in order, Partners need to weigh in their minds short-term profits against value forgone in the discounted offer price" (S005)
Lesson: a cheap compounder can be taken from you; own enough to defend it.

## 10. Xerox: cash flow through an accounting scandal
Sources: 2 (S002, S006).
Shares fell to 7% of peak after lease accounting and funding problems; he saw about U$1bn a year of cash flow and a price under half of appraised worth, and treated R&D as an asset (S002). "This matters not a jot to us, note again: cash flow is unchanged." (S002)
Lesson: when the accounts change but the cash does not, the market can overshoot.

## 11. MDC and Bank X: the response matters more than the mistake
Sources: 1 (S013).
Both made the same property lending mistake in the early 1990s. MDC changed how it thought and its shares rose ten-fold in ten years; Bank X blamed commercial property, diversified, and rose about ten percent (S013). "what is required is a rational, honest mea-culpa and a low share price." (S013)
Lesson: buy firms that admit and learn from mistakes, at a low price.

## 12. Union Cement: price discipline caps the position
Sources: 1 (S006).
Philippine cement at about a quarter of replacement cost with almost no sellers; his buying lifted the price and he got only about U$1m, 1% of the fund, before Holcim bought control at five times his average price (S006). "it is aggravating to just buy a bit" (S006, quoting Munger)
Lesson: a strict price limit can leave the best ideas too small.

## 13. Zimbabwe: replacement cost bought cheap, not to be repeated
Sources: 4 (S008, S009, S015, S024).
Cement plants at about ten cents on the dollar and breweries at a quarter of cost, bought in Harare through re-registered Johannesburg shares and sized at a few percent because of confiscation risk (S008, S024). Directors valued the holdings at zero after the exchange suspended trading in 2008 (S015). They rose three to eight times in dollars and were sold by 2012. "we can do better with the compounding businesses these days, and they are much less stressful" (S024)
Lesson: deep discounts to replacement cost work, but long-runway compounders are the better use of time and nerve.

## 14. The Welsh auto insurer: many small things
Sources: 2 (S018, S022).
He looked for a smoking gun and found none; a senior executive told him "we don't do one thing brilliantly, we do many, many things slightly better than others" (S018). He had dismissed this answer as a fig leaf for twenty years. "And I think that all this time I may have been wrong." (S018) The same idea runs through Amazon's light bulbs and Carpetright's reusable price cards (S018, S022).
Lesson: a moat can be a culture of caring about pennies. "Good things follow when you care about the pennies." (S022)

## 15. Dell and Apple: missing and selling the winners
Sources: 2 (S007, S009).
He said he would have shunned Dell in 1990 because growth stocks usually fail: "No, we weren’t invested in Dell either. Worse still, we weren’t looking." (S007) In 2005 clients asked about a loss in Northwest Airlines, but "selling Apple has cost us more." (S009)
Lesson: people count actual costs and ignore opportunity costs; this seat counts both.

## 16. Readers Digest: a capital plan abandoned
Sources: 1 (S005).
Management announced a buyback and debt repayment, with incentives to match, then made acquisitions instead; he counted it as a capital allocation mistake (S005, paraphrase).
Lesson: a broken capital allocation promise breaks the thesis.

## 17. The Ferrari 250 GTO: buy right, then hold forever
Sources: 3 (S015, S018, S024).
A collector bought a GTO for £750 in the 1960s; similar cars later sold for U$20m and then U$52m. "in the end, it did not matter what price he paid particularly, as the growth in underlying value made his purchase one of the best investments of all time." (S015) and "It is in this environment that people sell their GTOs for £750. Take heart and look to the horizon." (S015)
Lesson: in a crash, the owner who keeps the asset wins.

## 18. Zak's activity centre: earn less for longer
Sources: 1 (S022).
A family business paid staff more, spent on clean loos and good coffee, priced just under rivals, was funded with equity and no bank debt, and lowered prices again once rivals failed (S022). "far better, Zak argues, to earn less, but for a much longer time." (S022)
Lesson: scale shared in miniature; a monopolist's rent invites new entrants.
