# Selling: Nick Sleep

Built from the Nomad Investment Partnership letters (S001 to S024, 2002 to 2013) and the IGY Foundation postamble (S026, 2021), through the verified batch notes. His rules for selling, trimming, adding and re-checking, most-used first. His view moved from recycling fifty-cent dollars at full value (2003 to 2005) to almost never selling a long-runway compounder (2007 onward); both are shown with dates and the newer one is marked.

## Rules

### 1. Do not sell a compounder early: the opportunity cost is the biggest error
Sources: 7 (S007, S009, S012, S013, S016, S017, S026).
Newer view, held from 2005 on. "the biggest error an investor can make is the sale of a Wal-Mart or a Microsoft in the early stages of the company's growth." (S012) On the fund that sold IBM and Wal-Mart: "In terms of dollars of opportunity lost, it is likely to be the biggest single error this firm will make." (S016) He applied it to his own Amazon holding after it doubled in 2007 (S012).

### 2. Not selling is a decision you make every day
Sources: 4 (S016, S017, S022, S023).
"it requires daily decisions not to sell the shares as well!" (S017) and "the decision not to do something is still an active decision; it is just that the accountants don't capture it." (S022) By 2013 he called inactivity a source of value added. "In action on our part is counter-cultural and deliberate and is easier said than done." (S023)

### 3. A falling price on an intact thesis is a reason to add
Sources: 6 (S002, S003, S007, S014, S015, S022).
"For those shares that have fallen we have bought more." (S002) and "It is then that we will ask you to be contrarian and invest more." (S007) In the 2008 crash he reopened the fund and he and Zak subscribed heavily. "When investments go from hated and reasonable to despised and cheap something is normally up in the markets." (S014)

### 4. Sell when you can no longer value it
Sources: 2 (S002, S014).
Monsanto, 2002, sold at about cost after a missed indemnity: "we are no longer confident of valuing Monsanto with any certainty and the prudent thing to do was to sell our shares for approximately the price we had paid." (S002) MBIA, 2008, sold when losses and future equity raises could not be judged (S014).

### 5. Sell when dilution makes the share count unknowable
Sources: 1 (S014).
"one cannot place a valuation on the business per share without knowing the number of shares outstanding" (S014) Where dilution risk is real, size purchases modestly; if issuance below value becomes likely, cheapness is a trap. "the stock's biggest attraction, its apparent cheapness, becomes the investor's Achilles heel" (S014)

### 6. Re-check the destination, not the purchase case
Sources: 3 (S012, S013, S015).
Both his worst errors (Conseco held, Stagecoach sold) came from anchoring on the analysis made at purchase. "The analytical mistake in both cases was to have a static view of a firm formed at the time of purchase, which failed to evolve as the facts changed." (S013) A heavy weight is fine if the destination supports it: "the high weighting makes sense given our understanding of the destination of the businesses and the probability of reaching that destination." (S012)

### 7. Sell only for an investment reason
Sources: 1 (S009).
Not because the stock rose, because peers are selling or because it became illiquid (S009, paraphrase). Selling to bury an error is worse: "Being in the wrong stock strikes me as an analytical mistake, not a liquidity mistake" (S009)

### 8. Older rule: sell at full value and recycle into new fifty-cent dollars
Sources: 3 (S004, S005, S006).
Older view, 2003 to 2005, applied to turnarounds and cheap assets. "The investment has reached full value and will be sold as new investments become available." (S004) He sold Lucent preferred near full value and trimmed Stagecoach on this rule (S004, S005). He noted the cost in 2004: turnarounds carry reinvestment risk once sold (S006, paraphrase). Superseded for compounders by rule 1; the Stagecoach sale under this rule became his largest admitted error (S013).

### 9. Swaps for a better idea are rare
Sources: 3 (S012, S016, S026).
After reading about a thousand annual reports and holding three hundred interviews: "Overwhelmingly we have preferred our existing businesses to the alternatives." (S016) He warns that selling "the new thing in order to buy the new-new thing is a common human habit" (S012). Looking back in 2021: "we were unlikely to sell any of those to fund the purchase of another cigar butt, Philippine cement company, were we?" (S026)

### 10. Size up with conviction and let winners carry weight
Sources: 4 (S006, S007, S012, S016).
Via the Kelly criterion he concluded in 2004 that "Nomad’s portfolio concentration has at times been too low." (S006) He said the first Costco mistake was owning too little (S007) and the first Amazon weight was too low (S012). By 2009 about 30% sat in one holding (S016). He prefers to start high and work down: "There is another way to construct a portfolio, which is to invert and start at a hundred percent weighting and work down!" (S012)

### 11. Try to kill your best ideas on a routine
Sources: 2 (S008, S022).
"Any year that you don’t destroy one of your best loved ideas is probably a wasted year" (S008, quoting Munger) and "Routinely we try to kill our companies (they can be killed!)" (S022) In 2005 he discarded two of his own biases: that a low share price is better and that high inside ownership is always good (S008).

### 12. Do not sell to a low bid
Sources: 4 (S005, S008, S009, S011).
"being taken out at a low-price amounts to theft." (S008) He refused a low bid for Matichon in 2005 when other institutions sold (S009), voted against the Weetabix buyout (S005) and joined dissenters at Whitehead Mann (S011).

### 13. A thesis breaks on behaviour, not on the quote
Sources: 4 (S005, S008, S017, S020).
Readers Digest broke when management dropped its buyback and debt plan for acquisitions (S005). At Costco he watched for any shift of the customer surplus toward shareholders made to please Wall Street rather than for competitive reasons (S008). The long-term risks he named are "not those that dominate the newspapers and headlines today" (S020) but a disingenuous response to a problem, bubble valuations, and anti-trust or online sales tax (S020, paraphrase).

## Kill criteria this seat would write
Checkable facts that would end a long-runway thesis, in his terms (inferred from S005, S008, S014, S017, S020, S022 unless noted).
- The firm stops passing scale savings to customers: prices or margins rise to please shareholders, so the customer surplus per dollar of shareholder profit falls (S008, S022).
- Management responds to a problem with spin rather than a mea-culpa, or hits targets by cutting corners (S013, S017, S020).
- The founder or owner-manager leaves and incentives shift to short-term outputs such as earnings per share (S002, S014, S017).
- Equity is issued below intrinsic value, or the share count can no longer be estimated (S014).
- Cash from the core is diverted to acquisitions or ventures away from the jewel, or a capital plan is abandoned (S003, S005).
- Returns on incremental capital fall toward competitors' level, so cash-out no longer beats cash-in (S004, S020).
- New facts mean the business can no longer be valued with confidence (S002).
- The price discounts the whole runway, a bubble valuation, which he listed among the real long-term risks (S020).

## Re-check triggers
- Every year: destroy one best-loved idea (S008); routine re-analysis aimed at killing each holding (S022).
- After any share price fall: ask whether value fell or only price; if hated turns to despised, consider adding (S014, S015).
- Any capital raise, rights issue or placing: check the price against intrinsic value and the change in share count (S014).
- Takeover bids or management buyouts: compare the offer with value, not with the last price (S005, S011).
- Management mistakes: judge the response and whether the problem is self-inflicted and fixable (S013, S014).
- News filter: does it change the relationship the business has with its customers (S020).
- Insider buying and buybacks: count them across the portfolio (S003, S014).
- Measurement: judge results on rolling five-year periods, not single years (S004, S006).

## Selling mistakes he admitted, with dates
- 2003 to 2005 (sale), admitted 2007: Stagecoach bought at 14p, sold near 90p, later above £2.50. "The mistake was to leave £1.60 on the table" (S013)
- 2005: sold Apple, which cost more than the loss clients asked about in Northwest Airlines. "selling Apple has cost us more." (S009)
- 2004: Union Cement held to 1% of the fund by price discipline before a bid at five times his cost (S006).
- 2004 and 2007: starting weights in Costco and Amazon too low (S007, S012).
- 2008: MBIA sold at a loss of about 1.5% of NAV after misjudging dilution risk (S014).
- 2014 (written 2021): liquidating the portfolio when Nomad closed. "selling stakes built up over years felt wrong" (S026)
