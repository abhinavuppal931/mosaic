# Sleep: Long runway, scale shared
STANCE: A fine business whose destination I can describe but whose price and growth spend I cannot yet read, so a starter, not a full weight.
OPTION SCORES: A 3/10, B 7/10, C 6/10, D 3/10
PILLAR SCORE (Runway and reinvestment): 6/10
CONVICTION: 2 | EVIDENCE: thin | RUIN RISK: none | DOOR: two-way
THE CALL: I'd take B, a small starter now, adding only when two missing facts arrive: what the price implies, and how much of today's spending is growth rather than maintenance. Five years is a short bus ride for my lens; I care where Microsoft is in twenty.

WHY:
- Destination first (Destination analysis, S013). As general knowledge, Microsoft sells software and cloud capacity that businesses run on, a plausible end state much larger in usage. But "the biggest error an investor can make is the sale of a Wal-Mart or a Microsoft in the early stages of the company's growth." (S012) is about early stages. This is the elephant, not the mouse, so the question is whether the skeleton still scales (Mouse to elephant, S012).
- Who gets the savings? "Most companies pursue scale efficiencies, but few share them. It's the sharing that makes the model so powerful." (S007) Missing: unit price trends for its cloud and software. If prices per unit keep falling as it grows, my pillar score rises toward 8. If scale shows up as rising margins and price increases on locked-in customers, that is harvesting, and it falls toward 4.
- Split growth from maintenance (Capitalise the investment the accounts expense, S011). The heavy data-centre and AI spending is general knowledge; its return is not. Missing: the capex split and incremental returns on capital. Let's invert: "there is no guarantee that investment spending will always work." (S011)
- Read the price backwards (What the price implies, S015). Missing: price and multiple. If the price assumes growth stops, it echoes "Amazon has been priced in the market as if it would not grow in the future" (S015) and I'd move to A. If it assumes the whole AI runway is won, "as prices rise prospective returns go down." (S005) and C wins.

THE RISK I SEE: Fast-changing technology ages runway calls quickly (the early Apple sale is the warning, S009), and a hired manager rather than a founder lowers my confidence in decades of good capital allocation (S006).

I'M WRONG IF: incremental returns on the new data-centre capital fall toward competitors' level, so cash-out no longer beats cash-in (S004, S020).

QUESTION FOR THE INVESTOR: If the price halved and the business did not, would you buy more?
