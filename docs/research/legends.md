# Investment advisory council: legend roster research

Date: 2026-11-17. Scope: who sits on an investing version of Roast Room, what each seat owns, where they overlap, and whether there's enough free primary material to build each one's brain.

Reference read first: Roast Room `README.md`, `.claude/skills/roast-room/SKILL.md`, `references/seats.md`, `.claude/agents/council-buffett.md`, `brains/warren-buffett/_hot.md` and `frameworks.md`. In Roast Room a **seat** is a sub-agent that owns one question and one scorecard dimension, argues that question at full strength, cites a brain built from 30 to 100+ public sources (the Buffett brain uses 103), names where its own lens misfires, and has named natural opponents so the room argues instead of nodding. The Chair caps a verdict when any seat flags ruin risk. Each seat below is written to that contract.

Confidence key: **[V]** checked against a source in this session. **[K]** from background knowledge; check it before the brain build. Source counts are estimates.

That's 12 seats in total, the cap you set. Every default seat has a different job. The five things you required are each covered by a default seat: business quality (Buffett), valuation (Damodaran), risk and ruin (Taleb, with Marks on priced risk), macro (Druckenmiller) and the bear case (Chanos). Munger covers behaviour and inversion. Technicals and flows go to Druckenmiller, who reads price action as information. They don't get a seat of their own (see section 4).

**Changes to your proposed seven:** Lynch, Ackman and Kantesaria move from the default board to the bench. Damodaran, Druckenmiller and Chanos join the default board. Mauboussin and Sleep are new bench seats. The reason: your seven had four quality-business seats (Buffett, Munger, Ackman, Kantesaria) and no seat for valuation, macro, forensics or the bear case. With your seven as the board, a council would agree too fast on any wide-moat stock, which is the failure Roast Room's "unanimity check" is built to catch.

**If you'd rather keep your picks on the default board:** seat Buffett, Munger, Marks, Taleb, Lynch, Damodaran and Chanos. Druckenmiller goes on call. This keeps a growth seat by default and loses the macro seat. Ackman and Kantesaria stay on the bench either way, because by default they repeat Buffett.

## 2. Seat profiles

### Warren Buffett (keep, default)

**Philosophy.** A business is worth the cash it will hand its owners over its life, discounted. So every decision about a stock is a decision about allocating capital. He buys durable economic franchises he understands, run by honest and able managers, at a price that leaves a margin of safety, and he holds them as long as the economics hold. Above all he avoids the mistake that takes him out of the game: leverage, illiquidity, or a reputation loss.

**How he thinks.** Pass/fail before any analysis: is it inside the circle, and can he judge it in minutes? Then owner economics: the moat, return on tangible capital without debt, owner earnings after maintenance capex, and how much capital growth eats. Then people and incentives, then a rough value range against price. The hurdle is his second-best idea. Then the worst case. If it clears every bar he acts big. Otherwise he does nothing. He reasons in dollars and analogies, and he names his own mistakes first.

**Signature frameworks** (all taught in the Berkshire letters, https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/letters.html, and at annual meetings, https://buffett.cnbc.com):
1. Circle of competence (1996 letter; 1999 meeting).
2. Economic castle and moat (1993, 2007 letters).
3. Owner earnings (1986 letter appendix).
4. Intrinsic value as "birds in the bush" (2000 letter).
5. Great, good and gruesome businesses (2007 letter).
6. The $1 retained test and the order of capital allocation (1984, 2012 letters).
7. Mr. Market and margin of safety (1987 letter; adopted from Graham).
8. "The Inevitables" (1996 letter: Coca-Cola, Gillette) [K]. This, not Kantesaria, is where the "inevitability" language comes from.

**Seat question / dimension.** See the table. On an investing council Buffett's job narrows from the Roast Room "cash, risk and long-term value" seat to *the quality of the business and its owner economics*, because Taleb and Marks now own risk and Damodaran owns precise valuation.

**Where the lens misfires.** Fast-changing technology, where he admits missing Google and Amazon [K]. Businesses with no earnings history (pre-profit growth). Situations where waiting for the fat pitch costs more than acting. Signs he's slow to sell: textiles; airlines sold near the 2020 low [K]. His Berkshire advantages (float, permanent capital, size) don't transfer to a small portfolio. **Natural opponents:** Druckenmiller (macro and timing, which Buffett says he ignores), Lynch and Sleep (paying up for growth), and Chanos (Chanos looks for the numbers to be lies; Buffett trusts managers he admires).

**Correction to your one-liner.** "High-quality compounders" is half of it. Buffett is equally about price discipline and margin of safety, capital allocation, and avoiding ruin. He also owns plenty of things that aren't compounders: BNSF, utilities, Japanese trading houses, Occidental [K].

**Sources.** The richest free corpus of any seat: shareholder letters 1965 to 2024 (Berkshire's site has 1977 onward), partnership letters 1957 to 1970, about 30 years of meeting video and transcripts at buffett.cnbc.com, the Owner's Manual, testimony, and long interviews. More than 500 usable sources. The Roast Room Buffett brain already exists (103 sources), so **reuse it and rewrite the lens and agent file for investing.**

### Charlie Munger (keep, default, recast)

**Philosophy.** Buy a wonderful business at a fair price, never a fair business at a wonderful price. Concentrate on the few big, easy decisions, and sit on your hands the rest of the time. Avoid standard stupidity rather than trying to be brilliant. Read across disciplines, because a single model distorts what you see ("to a man with a hammer...").

**How he thinks.** He inverts: what would guarantee failure, and avoid that. He checks incentives before anything else, and he runs a checklist of psychological tendencies over the thesis and over the investor making it. Most ideas go to the "too hard" pile. He compares every new idea with the best one he already owns (opportunity cost) rather than with an abstract hurdle.

**Signature frameworks:**
1. Inversion ("Invert, always invert"). 1986 Harvard School commencement ("How to Guarantee a Life of Misery") [K].
2. Psychology of Human Misjudgment: about 25 tendencies and the lollapalooza effect. Harvard 1995 talk, revised in Poor Charlie's Almanack [K].
3. A latticework of mental models. "A Lesson on Elementary Worldly Wisdom," USC Business School, 1994 [K].
4. Incentives ("Show me the incentive and I'll show you the outcome"). Talks and Berkshire meetings [K].
5. The "too hard" pile and the circle of competence. Berkshire and Daily Journal meetings [K].
6. Opportunity cost as the filter: compare with your best existing idea. Daily Journal meetings 2014 to 2023 [K].
7. Great business at a fair price, sit-on-your-ass investing. Berkshire meetings, 2000s Wesco letters [K].
8. Checklists, and avoiding "standard stupidity." "Practical Thought About Practical Thought" (1996, the Coca-Cola thought experiment) [K].

**Differentiating him from Buffett.** If both seats are asked "is this a good business?", they'll give the same answer. So recast Munger as the **behavioural and inversion seat**. He runs a pre-mortem on the thesis, checks incentives at three levels (management pay, sell-side or promoter incentives, and the investor's own biases: FOMO, social proof, commitment, anchoring to cost), and says "too hard" out loud. That gives him a job no other seat has. It also covers the "behavioural" gap you asked about.

**Where the lens misfires.** The "too hard" pile writes off whole categories of innovation. He also over-trusts a few admired operators: the Alibaba position at Daily Journal lost money, while BYD worked [K]. Extreme patience has a real opportunity cost. **Natural opponents:** Druckenmiller (Munger called macro forecasting "above my pay grade" [K]), Ackman (public activist fights are the opposite of "avoid hassle"), and Sleep and Lynch when innovation is in play.

**Correction to your one-liner.** "Mental models" is right, but too broad to be a seat. The investing core is inversion, incentives and misjudgment.

**Sources.** About 80 to 120. Poor Charlie's Almanack has a **free online edition from Stripe Press**: https://stripepress.substack.com/p/poor-charlies-almanack-is-out-now [V]. Whether that edition is the full text is unconfirmed: one review says the whole book is online, but a library listing calls the Stripe edition abridged. Check before building. Also available: transcripts of the major speeches (USC 1994, Harvard 1995, the 2007 USC Law commencement, the 1998 Foundation Financial Officers talk) on fs.blog and elsewhere [K]; Daily Journal annual-meeting video and transcripts for 2014 to 2023; Wesco letters; Berkshire meeting Q&A on buffett.cnbc.com; the 2023 Acquired interview [K].

### Howard Marks (keep, default)

**Philosophy.** You can't predict the macro future, but you can know where you are in the cycle and act on it. Investing success comes from buying things well, not from buying good things. The same asset is a good or bad investment depending on its price. Risk is the chance of permanent loss, not volatility, and you control it through how much you pay and how investor psychology is running.

**How he thinks.** Second-level thinking: what does the consensus believe, what's priced in, and where is the consensus wrong? He "takes the temperature" of the market (credit standards, deal terms, investor euphoria or despair) and positions more aggressively or more defensively. He doesn't forecast. He's a credit investor first, so he reasons about asymmetry: avoiding losers matters more than catching winners.

**Signature frameworks** (all in the free memo archive, https://www.oaktreecapital.com/insights/memos):
1. Second-level thinking ("The Most Important Thing" memo, 2003; the book in 2011) [K].
2. The pendulum of investor psychology, and taking the market's temperature ("The Happy Medium" 2004; "On the Couch" 2016) [K].
3. Market and credit cycles, the race to the bottom ("The Race to the Bottom" 2007; Mastering the Market Cycle, 2018) [K].
4. Risk means permanent loss, and more things can happen than will happen ("Risk" 2006; "Risk Revisited" 2014) [K].
5. "I know I don't know": the futility of forecasting ("The Value of Predictions" 1993 and later) [K].
6. Asymmetry and defensive investing ("Dare to Be Great" 2006, 2014) [K].
7. Luck and randomness, judging decisions apart from outcomes ("Pigweed"; "Getting Lucky") [K].

**Seat question / dimension.** See the table. Your read ("risk and second-level thinking") is right. Add **cycle positioning** and **is the risk priced**, which separates him from Taleb.

**Where the lens misfires.** He was cautious through long stretches of the 2010s bull market and has said himself that being too early can't be told apart from being wrong [K]. His frameworks are relative ("priced in") rather than absolute. He gives little guidance on long-duration compounders, where the cycle hardly matters. **Natural opponents:** Druckenmiller (forecasts the Fed and liquidity, while Marks says it can't be done), and Sleep and Kantesaria (who say price matters less for a 20-year compounder).

**Sources.** The best single-author free corpus here: about 150 to 170 memos from 1990 to now, plus "The Memo" podcast (audio readings), and 50 or more long interviews (Invest Like the Best, We Study Billionaires, The Knowledge Project, Bloomberg) [K]. His books are largely built from the memos, so the memos are enough.

### Nassim Nicholas Taleb (keep, default, recast)

**Philosophy.** Exposure matters more than prediction. In fat-tailed domains, rare events dominate the outcome and can't be forecast, so you judge a position by how it behaves under extreme stress, not by its expected value. Any strategy with a non-zero chance of ruin will eventually be ruined, because time averages differ from ensemble averages (non-ergodicity). The way to win is convexity: limited downside and open-ended upside. You get it with the barbell, by removing fragilities, and by insisting that decision-makers have skin in the game.

**How he thinks.** Via negativa: first remove whatever can kill you, then look for upside. He asks whether a payoff is concave (hurt by volatility) or convex (helped by it), and distrusts any metric built on thin-tailed assumptions (VaR, Sharpe, mean-variance, "this has never happened"). He uses the Lindy effect to judge durability and checks the agent's skin in the game.

**Signature frameworks:**
1. Ruin and ergodicity: "the risk of ruin" (Medium essay "The Logic of Risk Taking", 2017; Skin in the Game, 2018) [K].
2. Fat tails, Mediocristan versus Extremistan (The Black Swan; free technical treatment in *Statistical Consequences of Fat Tails*, arXiv:2001.10488) [K].
3. Fragility as concavity to stressors, and antifragility (Antifragile; free paper "Mathematical Definition, Mapping, and Detection of (Anti)Fragility", Taleb and Douady) [K].
4. The barbell strategy (Antifragile; interviews) [K].
5. Skin in the game and agency problems (Skin in the Game; free Medium chapters) [K].
6. The turkey problem and silent evidence (The Black Swan) [K].
7. The Lindy effect (Antifragile; Medium) [K].
8. The precautionary principle (free paper with Read, Douady and others, 2014) [K].

**Seat question / dimension.** See the table. He's the seat that triggers the Chair's **ruin override** (Roast Room's "RUIN RISK: likely" cap), which fits him directly.

**Where the lens misfires.** He isn't a stock-picker and offers no way to rank two good businesses. Applied blindly he says no to everything leveraged, complex or concentrated, and taken literally he'd refuse to own equities without a hedge. Tail hedging costs a steady premium. "Black swan" becomes a catch-all excuse. His investing practice runs through Universa (Spitznagel), so many investing-specific views belong to Spitznagel, not Taleb. **Natural opponents:** Kantesaria and Buffett (predictable earnings for decades; Taleb would call that the turkey problem), and Druckenmiller (bet the ranch when you see it).

**Correction to your one-liner.** "Probabilities" is close to backwards. Taleb argues you *can't* estimate the probabilities that matter in fat-tailed domains, so you look at **exposure, fragility, ruin and convexity** instead. The seat should never output a probability estimate as its main answer. It outputs "what's the payoff shape and can this ruin us."

**Sources.** **The core books are copyrighted** (the five-volume Incerto). Free substitutes are strong for *risk* and thin for *investing*:
- Technical papers (40 to 60) on arXiv, SSRN and https://www.fooledbyrandomness.com, including *Statistical Consequences of Fat Tails* (the free "Technical Incerto" volume) [K].
- The Medium publication INCERTO (https://medium.com/incerto), 30 to 50 essays, several of them drafts of Skin in the Game chapters [K].
- About 10 EconTalk episodes, Real World Risk Institute lectures, and long YouTube talks [K].

Estimate: 60 to 100 free sources.

### Peter Lynch (keep, bench)

**Philosophy.** Ordinary investors can beat professionals by spotting good companies in their own lives and industries before Wall Street does, *and then doing the homework*. Behind every stock is a company, and the stock follows earnings over time. Sort every stock into a category, because each category has a different playbook and a different reason to sell. A handful of tenbaggers makes up for many mediocre picks, so let winners run. Timing the market is futile.

**How he thinks.** He visited more than 200 companies a year [V, Frontline]. He gives the "two-minute drill" story, puts the company in one of six categories, then checks the numbers against the story: P/E against growth, balance sheet, inventories, insider buying, how much of the company the product actually drives. He's a high-volume, diversified, "turn over the most rocks" process (Magellan held well over a thousand names). He expects to be right only about six times in ten [V, Frontline interview].

**Signature frameworks:**
1. Six stock categories: slow growers, stalwarts, fast growers, cyclicals, turnarounds, asset plays (One Up on Wall Street, 1989; copyrighted) [K].
2. Invest in what you know, *plus research*. Frontline interview [V]; One Up [K].
3. The two-minute drill / the story check (One Up) [K].
4. P/E against growth, the PEG ratio and its yield-adjusted form (One Up; Beating the Street) [K].
5. Tenbaggers; don't "water the weeds and cut the flowers." Frontline interview [V].
6. "The person that turns over the most rocks wins." Frontline interview [V].
7. Avoid "diworsification" (One Up) [K].
8. Don't time the market; "the stomach is the key organ." Frontline interview [V, via A Wealth of Common Sense].

**Seat question / dimension.** See the table.

**Where the lens misfires.** "Invest in what you know" gets misused as consumer intuition without the homework. PEG breaks down for cyclicals, loss-makers and capital-heavy growth. His edge came from labour (company visits) that an AI seat can't reproduce. His results came from a small- and mid-cap-friendly 1977-to-1990 market. His wide diversification clashes with the concentration seats. **Natural opponents:** Kantesaria (Lynch's six-in-ten against Kantesaria's target of 90% or more winners; fast growers against predictable monopolies), Damodaran (a story with no valuation discipline), and Munger.

**Sources. Thin and book-heavy.** All three books (One Up on Wall Street, Beating the Street, Learn to Earn) are copyrighted, and most of his frameworks live there. Free primary material:
- The PBS Frontline "Betting on the Market" (1997) program and his full Frontline interview: https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/pages/frontline/shows/betting/ and the transcript at https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/pages/frontline/shows/betting/etal/script.html [V].
- Wall Street Week, Charlie Rose and CNBC appearances on YouTube [K].
- A 1994 National Press Club talk (C-SPAN) [K].
- Fidelity interviews (including the "25 years later" pieces) and 1990s Worth magazine columns (some on archive.org) [K].

Estimate: 15 to 30 free sources. The brain would need to paraphrase the book frameworks with very few verbatim quotes, and the check_quotes step will flag most book quotes as unverifiable. **This is the main reason to bench rather than seat him by default.**

### Bill Ackman (keep, bench, recast)

**Philosophy.** Own a concentrated portfolio, typically 8 to 12 core holdings [V, PSH 2025 Annual Report], of "simple, predictable, free-cash-flow-generative" businesses with dominant moats and conservative balance sheets [V, PSH 2014 and 2018 reports]. Ideally buy them at a discount when there's a fixable problem, then **engage** (board seats, public campaigns, or private pressure) to close the gap between price and value. He calls this "long-term arbitrage" [V, PSH 2014 report]. He also runs opportunistic macro hedges for asymmetric payoffs: the 2020 credit-default-swap hedge, interest-rate hedges [K]. PSH says it "may use opportunistic hedging strategies to mitigate market-related downside risk or to take advantage of asymmetric profit opportunities" [V].

**How he thinks.** Thesis first: what the business earns at maturity and what's wrong today. Then the catalyst: who fixes it, and can Pershing Square be the catalyst? Then a DCF framing, where year-one disruption barely moves value. He made that argument publicly in March 2020: losing the first one or two years of cash flow "does not generally destroy more than 5% to 10% of the value" [V, PSH 2019 Annual Report]. He's quick to reverse when new facts break the thesis [V, PSH 2014]. He makes the case publicly and at length (his 2012 Herbalife presentation ran to hundreds of slides).

**Signature frameworks:**
1. Simple, predictable, FCF-generative business with a moat and little leverage (PSH annual reports from 2014) [V].
2. Activism and engagement as the catalyst, "long-term arbitrage" (PSH 2014) [V].
3. Value is the present value of long-term cash, so look past the next 12 to 24 months (PSH 2019 report) [V].
4. Asymmetric macro hedges: 2020 CDS, rates (PSH reports and letters 2020 to 2023) [K].
5. A concentrated book of 8 to 12 names (PSH reports) [V].
6. Changing your mind quickly when a thesis breaks; the post-mortems on Valeant, Herbalife and J.C. Penney (PSH 2017 and 2018 letters) [K].
7. Holding-company and permanent-capital structure (PSH; Howard Hughes Holdings recast from 2025) [K].

**Seat question / dimension.** See the table.

**Where the lens misfires.** Overconfidence that a new manager or a campaign will fix the business (J.C. Penney). Ego and public commitment that keep him in a losing short (Herbalife). Getting it badly wrong on a roll-up's accounting and governance (Valeant, a multi-billion loss) [K]. Political and social-media noise. An individual investor can't act as the catalyst, so for a retail user this seat's value is judging *whether someone else's catalyst is credible*. **Natural opponents:** Kantesaria ("the business is already superb; don't fix it, wait for a dislocation"), Munger (avoid fights and complexity), and Chanos (a roll-up that looks fixable may be a fraud).

**Correction to your one-liner.** "Quality businesses with predictable cash flows" is accurate as a filter, but it's the part that duplicates Buffett. What makes Ackman distinct is **activism and catalysts, and asymmetric hedging**. Build the seat around those, or it's redundant.

**Sources.** Strong: about 120 or more. Pershing Square Holdings annual and interim reports from 2014 to 2025, with long letters, at https://pershingsquareholdings.com (for example https://assets.pershingsquareholdings.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/18175039/Pershing-Square-Holdings-Ltd.-2025-Annual-Report.pdf) [V]. Also: annual investor-day presentations, Ira Sohn talks, the Herbalife and other campaign decks, PSLP letters from 2004 to 2013 (many mirrored), long podcasts (Lex Fridman, Invest Like the Best), and hundreds of long X posts [K].

### Dev Kantesaria / Valley Forge Capital (keep, bench; thinnest source base)

**Philosophy.** Own 8 to 12 of the best businesses in the world, from a "fishing pond" of only about 50 [V, Good Investing 2022 interview]. He looks for natural monopolies and oligopolies with pricing power above inflation, strong organic volume growth, operating leverage, capital-light models and highly predictable earnings for the next 10 years or more [V, Value Investor Insight 2019; TIP 2024]. He buys on rare dislocations, often regulatory or legal scares: Moody's in 2008-09 at a single-digit P/E, and Visa and Mastercard after the Durbin Amendment [V]. Then he barely trades. He calls Moody's "the toll collector on an increasingly busy global road" [V, VII 2019].

**How he thinks.** He sees his edge as temperament and *assessing future risk*, which he describes as choosing the discount rate for future free cash flow. That's a skill he carried over from 18 years of biotech venture capital, where he had to collapse 20 or more risk factors into a single price [V, TIP 2021; Good Investing]. He wants a 90%-plus batting average rather than a venture-style power law [V]. He followed FICO for about seven years before buying [V]. He disqualifies a company on one bad factor: serial acquirers, heavy R&D (biotech, pharma), heavy capex, or excess cash on the balance sheet. He nets stock-based compensation out of free cash flow [V].

**Signature frameworks** (all from interviews; no public letters):
1. Compounding machines / toll booths: monopoly or oligopoly, pricing power, volume growth, operating leverage, capital-light (VII 2019; Good Investing 2022) [V].
2. Predictability over growth ("If you are worried about next quarter's results...") (Good Investing 2022) [V via summary].
3. Pricing power as the hallmark, found through indirect evidence because companies hide it (Good Investing 2022) [V].
4. The tiny fishing pond and extreme concentration (about 50 names watched, 8 to 12 owned) [V].
5. Buy the dislocation: regulatory, legal or earnings scares (VII 2019; Good Investing) [V].
6. Organic growth only, no serial acquirers; SBC-adjusted FCF (Good Investing 2022) [V].
7. Risk as a forward-looking discount rate, not volatility (TIP 2021; Good Investing) [V].

**Seat question / dimension.** See the table.

**Where the lens misfires.** Concentrated **regulatory and political risk inside the "toll booth"**. A live example: on 2025-07-08 the FHFA director allowed lenders to use VantageScore 4.0 for Fannie and Freddie mortgages, and FICO fell about 9% to 19% that day [V, Bloomberg; Investopedia; CNN]. Bloomberg reported it was down a further 17% by late September 2025 [V]. FICO was Valley Forge's largest position, reportedly about 35% at its peak in Q3 2024 per aggregators; check this against the 13F [V, aggregator]. He also pays high multiples, so multiple compression is a risk. He's excluded big tech and all R&D-heavy compounders [V, TIP 2024]. "Predictable for 10 years" is the turkey problem in Taleb's terms. **Natural opponents:** Taleb (fragility hidden inside predictability), Sleep (heavy reinvestment and R&D are fine if the runway is long), Lynch (six-in-ten breadth against nine-in-ten concentration), Damodaran (pricing a monopoly at 35x), and Ackman (fix it versus don't touch it).

**Corrections to your one-liner.** "High free-cash-flow businesses" misleads. It reads like a high FCF *yield*, cheap-stock screen, and he's the opposite: he'll pay up for *predictable, growing, SBC-adjusted* FCF from pricing-power monopolies, and buys only on dislocation. "Inevitability" is Buffett's 1996 word ("The Inevitables"). Kantesaria's own language is "compounding machines," "natural monopolies or oligopolies" and "toll collector." He openly says he follows the Buffett-Munger playbook [V], so by default this seat repeats Buffett more than any other.

**Sources. Thin.** No public letters. Some excerpts circulate (he refers to "one of my letters" on buying Coca-Cola before World War II [V]), but the full letters aren't public. Free long-form:
- The Investor's Podcast, 2021-10-21: https://www.theinvestorspodcast.com/episodes/buffetts-core-principles-w-dev-kantesaria/ [V]
- The Investor's Podcast, 2024-12-05: https://www.theinvestorspodcast.com/episodes/investing-in-exceptional-businesses-for-the-long-run-dev-kantesaria/ [V]
- Good Investing Talks, recorded 2022: https://www.good-investing.net/2023/01/26/dev-kantesaria-what-is-your-formula-for-quality-investing/ (transcript plus YouTube) [V]
- Business Breakdowns, "FICO: A High Score Business", 2023-05-17 [V]
- Value Investor Insight, October 2019 (free): https://valueinvestorinsight.com/Content_Free/investor-insight-standing-out-october-2019.aspx [V]
- ValueTalks (ValueWalk) transcript: https://hedgefundalpha.com/profiles/interview-with-dev-kantesaria-valley-forge-capital-management/ [V]
- Hedgeweek, 2019 [V]; a few 2025 podcasts referenced by secondary blogs [V, unconfirmed hosts]

Estimate: **8 to 15 primary sources**, under Roast Room's `--quick` floor of 12 to 20. Brain depth will be shallow and repetitive, since the same Moody's, FICO and Visa stories recur across interviews.

### Aswath Damodaran (add, default)

**Philosophy.** Every valuation is a story turned into numbers, and every number should trace back to the story. Value and price are different games: intrinsic value is driven by cash flows, growth, reinvestment efficiency and risk, while price is driven by mood, momentum and relative multiples. Both games are legitimate, but you have to know which one you're playing. Uncertainty isn't a reason to stop valuing. You value anyway, then use ranges and simulations.

**How he thinks.** He starts from a narrative and tests it as possible, then plausible, then probable. He converts it into drivers: revenue growth, target margin, sales-to-capital (reinvestment efficiency) and cost of capital. He places the company on the corporate life cycle and runs the DCF. Then he compares with the price and says buy, hold or sell with his own money, publicly. He revisits after every earnings report and changes the story when the facts change.

**Signature frameworks** (blog: https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com; NYU site: https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/):
1. Narrative and numbers, story to value (blog series; the book Narrative and Numbers, 2017, is copyrighted) [K].
2. The value game against the pricing game (blog; Stern lectures) [K].
3. The "3 P" test: possible, plausible, probable (blog) [K].
4. The corporate life cycle and age-appropriate valuation (blog; The Corporate Life Cycle, 2024) [K].
5. Value drivers: growth, reinvestment (sales-to-capital), margins, risk (Stern valuation class, free on YouTube) [K].
6. Implied equity risk premium, updated monthly, and country risk premiums (NYU data pages) [K].
7. Valuing the hard cases: young, distressed and cyclical firms ("The Dark Side of Valuation" papers and lectures) [K].

**Why default.** None of your proposed seats produces a number. Without him "valuation" becomes vibes ("wonderful business at a fair price"). He's also the most public about being wrong and updating.

**Where the lens misfires.** Precise models can give a false sense of precision on optionality-heavy, platform or monopoly businesses. He has written about selling NVIDIA on valuation well before its run ended [K]. He's agnostic about qualitative moats until they show up in margins and reinvestment, so he can underrate network effects. **Natural opponents:** Kantesaria and Sleep (pay up for quality and runway), Druckenmiller (price action and liquidity over intrinsic value), and Lynch (story first).

**Sources.** The largest free corpus here, in the thousands: about 15 years of blog posts (hundreds), papers, lecture notes, full-semester class videos on YouTube, and datasets [K]. No source risk.

### Stanley Druckenmiller (add, default; chosen over Dalio)

**Philosophy.** "Earnings don't move the overall market; it's the Federal Reserve Board... It's liquidity that moves markets" [V, Lost Tree Club 2015]. "Never, ever invest in the present": picture where things will be 18 months out [V, same]. Concentrate when you see something rare. Diversification is "probably the most misguided concept everywhere" [V, same]. Preserve capital and reverse fast when circumstances change.

**How he thinks.** Top-down to bottom-up. Central-bank policy and liquidity set the regime. The regime points to sectors and assets, then to specific stocks. He treats price action as information: the market often knows more than he does. He sizes by conviction ("put all your eggs in one basket and then watch the basket very carefully" [V]) and cuts when he's wrong. He says about 80% of his big money was made in equity bear markets that followed central-bank mistakes [V].

**Signature frameworks:**
1. Liquidity and central banks drive markets (Lost Tree Club, 2015-01-18, transcript at https://covestreetcapital.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/03/Druckenmiller-_Speech.pdf) [V].
2. Never invest in the present; look 18 to 24 months ahead (same) [V].
3. Concentration and betting big on rare setups (the Soros pound story) (same) [V].
4. Change your mind when the facts change; size and stop discipline (same; later interviews) [V/K].
5. The "Mars test": what would the policy rate be if you saw only the data? (same, the 2003-04 example) [V].
6. Price action and sector leadership as a discounting mechanism (Sohn talks; Norges Bank "In Good Company" podcast) [K].
7. Central-bank mistakes as the source of bear markets (Lost Tree; Hoover and Bloomberg Invest talks) [V/K].

**Why Druckenmiller over Dalio.** A council judges *one decision*: buy, add, hold or sell this position, now. Druckenmiller's lens goes straight from macro to an asset or sector on an 18-to-24-month horizon and uses price action, so it reaches that decision. Dalio's lens is systematic and portfolio-level (debt-cycle templates, risk parity, All Weather, maximum diversification). That's useful for allocation, but on a single name it mostly says "diversify." Druckenmiller also covers the **technicals, flows and timing** gap without a separate seat. Trade-off: Dalio's free corpus is far larger (Principles, *Big Debt Crises* PDF, *Changing World Order*, "How the Economic Machine Works") [K]. If the council later adds a portfolio-construction mode, Dalio is the seat to add.

**Where the lens misfires.** Macro calls with poor timing: his widely reported 2022-23 recession warnings didn't play out on his timeline [K]. Concentrated, leveraged macro bets are dangerous for retail sizing. Short horizons fight against compounding. His track record depended on stop discipline that a council can't enforce. **Natural opponents:** Marks ("I know I don't know"), Buffett and Munger (macro is unknowable, so ignore it), and Taleb (concentration).

**Sources.** No letters and no books. About 40 to 70 transcribable talks and interviews: Lost Tree 2015 [V]; Sohn talks; Bloomberg Invest 2023 and 2024; Norges Bank "In Good Company"; Hoover; Real Vision; Morgan Stanley; CNBC [K]. Duquesne Family Office 13Fs show positions, not reasoning. Adequate for a default-depth brain (30 to 50).

### Jim Chanos (add, default)

**Philosophy.** Markets systematically overpay for companies whose reported earnings are inflated, whose business plans are flawed, or that are outright frauds. His 2002 testimony lists exactly these three categories [V]. The tells are in the filings: return on capital below cost of capital, aggressive revenue recognition, opaque related-party transactions, heavy insider selling, and sudden executive departures. "There is no louder alarm bell... than the unexplained, sudden departure of a chief executive officer" [V, SEC roundtable 2003]. Booms breed fraud.

**How he thinks.** He reads the 10-K and footnotes first, recomputes economic returns and compares them with the cost of capital (Enron's 7% pre-tax ROC against roughly 9% cost of capital [V]). He looks for "accounting treadmills" (gain-on-sale, roll-ups, capitalised costs), checks insider behaviour and the sell-side's "trust me" narratives, then judges whether a catalyst can surface the problem.

**Signature frameworks:**
1. Three short categories: overstated earnings, flawed business plan, fraud. Testimony to the House Energy and Commerce Committee, 2002-02-06 (https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-107hhrg77986/html/CHRG-107hhrg77986.htm) [V].
2. ROIC below the cost of capital: profitable on paper, not economically (same) [V].
3. The accounting treadmill: gain-on-sale and mark-to-model (same) [V].
4. Red flags: related-party transactions, insider selling, sudden CEO departure. SEC Roundtable on Hedge Funds, 2003 (https://www.sec.gov/spotlight/hedgefunds/hedge-chanos.htm) [V].
5. "Trust me" stories versus analysable businesses (same) [V].
6. Secular-decline and value-trap shorts; China property "on a treadmill to hell" (2010 interviews) [K].
7. Fraud as a bull-market phenomenon, the "golden age of fraud" (2021-22 interviews) [K].

**Why default.** Every other seat is long-biased. Roast Room's design depends on a seat that will disagree, and in investing that's the forensic short-seller. He gives the council a real bear case and an accounting check that no quality seat runs.

**Where the lens misfires.** Being right on the accounting but very early, or wrong on timing (the long Tesla short) [K]. Shorting is structurally hard, and his firm wound down its funds in 2023, citing pressure on the long/short business model [V, Hedgeweek/Fortune]. He can see fraud in honest high-growth companies that reinvest aggressively. **Natural opponents:** Sleep and Lynch (aggressive reinvestment and growth stories), Ackman (roll-ups and "fixable" situations), and Buffett (trust in admired managers).

**Sources.** About 40 to 70: congressional testimony and the SEC roundtable statement [V], plus many long interviews and podcasts (Odd Lots, Masters in Business, Capital Allocators, Grant's, Bloomberg, CNBC) and conference talks [K]. Reports that he teaches seminars on the history of financial fraud (Yale, Chicago) weren't confirmed in this session. No public fund letters.

### Michael Mauboussin (add, bench)

**Philosophy.** Good investing comes from good process under uncertainty. Read what the price implies, and don't build your own forecast first. Anchor on base rates (the outside view) before the company story. Separate skill from luck. Size positions by edge and odds, because sizing drives results almost as much as selection.

**How he thinks.** He runs a reverse DCF to get the expectations embedded in the price. He checks those against base rates (how often have companies of this size grown at this rate for this long?), estimates the probabilities and payoffs, and then sizes using a fractional-Kelly logic.

**Signature frameworks** (free papers at Morgan Stanley Counterpoint Global, plus his earlier Credit Suisse and Legg Mason papers):
1. Expectations investing / reverse DCF (Expectations Investing, the 2021 edition is copyrighted; many free papers) [K].
2. Base rates and the outside view (The Base Rate Book, Credit Suisse 2016, free) [K].
3. The skill-luck continuum (The Success Equation; free papers) [K].
4. Measuring the moat (free paper, updated several times) [K].
5. Probabilities and payoffs, expected value and Kelly sizing ("Probabilities and Payoffs," Counterpoint Global, 2025-02-19, with Callahan; the 2006 "Size Matters") [V via secondary].
6. Capital allocation and ROIC analysis (free papers) [K].

**Why bench, and how he differs from Damodaran and Marks.** Damodaran builds value forward from a story. Mauboussin reverse-engineers the price and tests it against history. Marks reads sentiment and cycle. Seat Mauboussin when the decision is about *sizing* (add, trim, how big) or a high-expectations stock. He also covers the behavioural and process side, with Munger.

**Where the lens misfires.** Base rates penalise genuine outliers, which by definition beat them. He's a researcher, not a portfolio manager with a long public record, so there are fewer "skin in the game" stories. **Natural opponents:** Druckenmiller (conviction bets over base rates), and Sleep and Lynch (outlier compounders).

**Sources.** Very rich: about 150 or more free papers, plus many podcasts [K].

### Nick Sleep (add, bench; chosen over Philip Fisher)

**Philosophy.** Find businesses that share their scale economies with customers (lower prices as they grow), which builds a widening moat. Owning them for a decade or more lets you exploit the market's short horizon (time arbitrage). The biggest mistakes are mistakes of omission and selling too early. Doing nothing is usually right. Nomad ended up with roughly three positions: Amazon, Costco and Berkshire [K].

**How he thinks.** Destination analysis: where does the business end up if the model keeps working? He checks reinvestment and the rate of return on it, founder alignment, and whether customers win as the company grows. Then he concentrates and holds through volatility.

**Signature frameworks** (Nomad Investment Partnership letters, 2001 to 2013):
1. Scale economies shared [K].
2. Destination analysis [K].
3. Reinvestment runway and the reinvestment moat [K].
4. Time arbitrage and patience, "inactivity" [K].
5. Mistakes of omission, and selling winners too early [K].
6. Aligned, founder-led capital allocators [K].

**Why Sleep over Fisher.** Fisher's scuttlebutt and 15 points are excellent, but his work is almost all in copyrighted books (1958 onward), with very few free primary sources. Lynch covers ground-truth research. Sleep is the strongest *free*, modern growth and long-runway corpus, and he's the natural opponent to Kantesaria (Sleep backs reinvestment and R&D, which Kantesaria refuses).

**Where the lens misfires.** Paying any price for runway. Concentration in three names. Survivorship: Amazon made the record. Runway judgments age quickly in fast-changing technology. **Natural opponents:** Damodaran (price discipline), Kantesaria (capital-light only), Chanos (heavy reinvestment hiding weak economics), and Marks (the cycle).

**Sources.** About 26 letters (annual and interim, 2001 to 2013), widely mirrored as a compiled PDF of roughly 100 or more pages, plus a handful of rare interviews and talks [K]. Check the reuse terms on the letters (they're widely circulated, but the right to reuse them isn't confirmed). Keep quotes short.

| Buffett / Munger | Both: wonderful business, fair price, circle of competence, patience | Buffett = business economics and moat. Munger = inversion, incentives, the investor's own biases, "too hard." Munger's agent file should bar him from scoring moat quality. He scores *folly risk* |

| Marks / Taleb | Both "risk" | Marks = *relative and priced* risk (cycle, sentiment, are we paid). Taleb = *absolute* exposure (ruin, tails, convexity, leverage). Only Taleb can trigger the ruin cap |
| Marks / Druckenmiller | Both read the cycle | Marks won't forecast and reads credit and psychology. Druckenmiller forecasts liquidity and the Fed and reads price action. That makes them natural opponents, which is useful |

## 7. Public-figure, likeness and impersonation notes

- Copy Roast Room's disclaimer pattern. Seats are simulations built from public work. They're not the people, don't speak for them, and nobody is affiliated with or endorses the project. A seat quotes verbatim only from its brain with a source id, `check_quotes` enforces that, and raw sources stay out of git with only short quotes and paraphrase committed. The Roast Room Buffett agent also says "You are not Buffett and never claim to be."
- **Investing-specific additions.** Every verdict and the README should say "not investment advice." Recast Roast Room's "never give stock advice" line: seats judge the user's thesis, they don't issue recommendations in the legend's name. And never write output that could be read as a real person endorsing a security; a simulated Ackman or Chanos naming a stock could look like market commentary from the real person. Living, active managers (Ackman, Druckenmiller, Kantesaria, Chanos, Marks, Damodaran, Mauboussin, Sleep) carry more risk than historical figures.
- **Portraits and names.** Roast Room generates cartoon portraits from real photos. That raises right-of-publicity exposure for living people, and for the deceased in states with post-mortem rights; Munger was a California resident, and California protects post-mortem publicity rights [K; get legal review]. Consider abstract avatars and plain-name labels, and keep names out of any marketing.
